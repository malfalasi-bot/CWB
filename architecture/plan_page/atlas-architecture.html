Why the Atlas keeps limiting the content

The Atlas was built as a map of short records. Anything longer than one or two sentences has no type in it, so the long material piles up in folders the Atlas cannot see. The map is also the only way in, and only things with a place can stand on a map.

17words: the median length of an Atlas record's only prose field (maximum 60)
83%of the 4,666 canon entries never reach the map: people, techniques, periods, styles, movements
0Atlas entries for ballet, against 390,000 words of research and 156 works
10separate stores of facts, using four different confidence vocabularies

Each surface has also invented its own format: the Atlas hub, the Edo unit, the lecture, the ballet page and the grouping pages. A full Atlas record was designed in Deep Dives 1, with four text tiers, typed links, media, rights and upkeep, but it was never built. The database is a stale copy of flat text.

The four layers

A benchmark of museums, encyclopaedias, publishers, broadcasters and course platforms found the same answer everywhere. None of them lets the display hold the content. Each keeps four things apart and builds every view from them. The plan does the same.

1 · Records

What is true

Works, productions, people, objects, places, events, eras, techniques; one claim per fact, with its source and confidence; sources; images and their licences.

Giselle as a work; its Paris, St Petersburg, 1924 and Havana productions; its remakes by Ek, Khan and Masilo as works of their own; its premiere date as a claim.

2 · Writing

What we say, at any length

Cards in the four tiers, deep dives, chapters, lectures, essays, disputes, myths, lessons, object studies. Length belongs to the form, not to the Atlas.

The Giselle card; chapter 3 of the unit; the debate "Who is the author?"; the research files, kept apart from teaching.

3 · Structures

How it is put together

Units, chapters, sessions, lecture runs, routes, walls, timelines, lenses: ordered references with minutes and framing, never knowledge.

The 13-chapter unit with its minutes; the Repertory's routes; the motif walls; the "world of ballet" lens.

built by script, never by hand

4 · Views

The ways in

The Map, the Line, Connections, a page for every entity, the Library of units and lectures, Compare, Search, and Ask (the partner voices).

The Giselle page: its card, productions and remakes on the line, the disputes, the claims with sources, and the chapters that use it.

The map stays. It becomes one way in among eight. Every person, technique, movement and work gets a page of its own, whether or not it has a place.

How much of the Atlas the rest of the curriculum uses

Outside F1, about 80 of the program's roughly 425 other units need historical, cultural or material knowledge. Each takes a small piece: a card, a claim, a section of a chapter, part of a wall or a saved view on the line. No placement takes a whole chapter. So the Atlas is used in two ways:

  • Taken whole. A concept unit such as ballet is a course in its own right, an Atlas course outside F1's hours, with its own paths.
  • Taken in pieces. Other units cite Atlas pieces by id through a link register, and never copy them.

Ballet's material, by research words:

lives only in the topic, about 85–88%feeds F1 units, about 6–7%feeds other modules, about 6–7%

The material only the topic uses is not waste. It is the course a learner who wants ballet can take, and it is the depth the Historian voice answers from. Materials and design movements are reused most (30 to 50 per cent of their knowledge), civilisations least. Nine modules should write their history into the Atlas, not only read it: F1, F2, F4, S1, S3, S4, S5, S6 and R2, plus M2 for the field atlas and every module's regional slot.

Ballet first: everything kept, everything findable

Nothing is deleted. Research stays research, kept in place, anchored and never copied. Learner writing is written from it and versioned, and no fact reaches a learner until a person has accepted it.

Serving it

Pages published here cannot fetch data from another host. So the serving path has two stages.

  • Stage 1, now, at zero cost. Each surface is published with its own slice of the content as data files. Search is an index shipped with the page. Ask uses the page's own ability to ask Claude, passing the retrieved sections so the answer can cite them. Learner records use the page's own storage. The database is a rebuilt copy for analysis, so its pausing after a quiet week does no harm.
  • Stage 2, later. When the program has its own host, a static site serves the same content, and the database serves search, the partner voices and learner accounts. That decision comes later.

Git stays the single source. Ids are permanent and survive a link, and every fact a view shows is a claim cited by id, so a date fixed once is fixed everywhere.

After ballet

  • Bring the rest in without rewriting it. Edo's people, events and sources become records, and its story text becomes a document. The F1.13 lecture becomes one document and one run, instead of three hand-kept shapes. The 28 grouping pages become documents, and the ten fact stores become one.
  • Enrich the canon. Typed links come from the 2,360 references now buried in notes. Every node gets a page, including the 3,900 the map leaves out.
  • Links and learners. The link register is generated from the 38 specs and the placement reviews and checked at each gate. Learners can cite the Atlas in their own sources record.
  • Other modules. M2's field atlas becomes a filter over existing cards. F2, F4, the practice areas and R2 write their lineages into the Atlas, and module openers are drawn from it.
  • Next topics. The movements already in grouping pages are next: the Bauhaus, Art Deco, Arts and Crafts. Each gets its industries lens and, where it has many works, a Repertory.

Your decisions

Program-level decisions are yours to record in the Hub. Each has a default that stands unless you say otherwise. Three earlier decisions are still open and are needed before the steps that use them: the ballet blueprint's approval, the Repertory's four decisions, and design E with M2's comparison (Giselle or the Bauhaus).

1 · Whose Atlas

The program's knowledge base, with F1 as its first author.

Default: yes.

2 · Four layers

Records, writing, structures and views, with the map as one view among several.

Default: yes.

3 · The move

The Atlas and the topics move up from F1 to program level, as the first step, with history kept.

Default: yes.

4 · Length

The four tiers stay for cards only. Every other form has guidance, not a cap.

Default: yes.

5 · One way of recording facts

One claim scheme, using the program's four confidence levels: documented, probable, contested, interpretive.

Default: yes.

6 · Atlas courses

Full concept units sit outside F1's hours, with their own paths and a published rule for which concepts get one.

Default: yes.

7 · The world-of lens

Industry and skill tags, and a "world of" lens for every topic, starting with ballet.

Default: yes.

8 · Names

Ballet's 24 "threads" become motifs, so "thread" keeps its program meaning. The blueprint's 14 walls and the 24 census walls become one set.

Default: yes.

9 · Unit invariants

Every unit must contain a lab, an Apply by lane, a partner step and a log entry. Either concept-unit chapters gain a short lab and a Historian step, or you amend the rule for Atlas courses.

No default: your call.
