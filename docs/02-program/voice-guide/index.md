# Creative World program voice guide, v1

2026-10-02 · Written for F1 "Histories of making"; applies to all program copy · Replaces the step that waited on three writing samples

How Creative World writes, for human writers and AI partner voices. It builds on the twelve writing rules (F1 Deep Dives 1), Historian pack v2 rules 13–20 and the spec brief, and sets their tone from sixteen published exemplars. Quotations were captured through a fetch tool; check each word for word at source before publication.

## 1. The voice

**In one paragraph.** We write like a careful museum colleague at the learner's table. We start with a thing you could point at: an object, a place, a person, a record. We say who made it, even when the record forgot their name. We say how sure we are, in the sentence and not in a footnote. We name violence in plain words, and we quote the perpetrators' words as theirs. Communities come first by their own names. Sentences are short and words plain; the trade's terms stay, explained once. We do not praise objects, people or the learner. We give evidence and then a question, and the learner does the judging.

**Six attributes**

1. **Object first, not category first.** Adamson opens on a teapot in a portrait and Smarthistory on the plaque itself; the Getty and Smithsonian guides ask for visible detail first. Cleveland's gold-weight label opens on a kingdom and reaches the object only in its last sentence, which leaves the reader nothing to check.
2. **Exact about doubt, not vague and not falsely sure.** Heilbrunn sets competing court histories side by side, the British Museum reports that its shield was not Cook's, and Smarthistory says one question will never be settled. "Some say" does none of this; our confidence grammar makes the practice a rule.
3. **Makers named by role, not dissolved into a style.** The Met names the Edo casters' guild in its own spelling. MacGregor says "Benin craftsmen", and Cleveland's label names nobody. "A caster of the guild" is evidence, while "Benin art" hides the people.
4. **Plain about violence, not euphemistic and not lurid.** The British Museum's record says "looted by British forces", and De Bondt in *Eye* states the deaths at colonial exhibitions. MacGregor and Smarthistory both use "Punitive Expedition" as a name, which is the habit rule 7 stops.
5. **Their name first, not ours.** NMAI advises the community's own name. Te Papa leaves the authority to speak for taonga with the iwi, and *Words Matter* treats every term as a choice with a history.
6. **A peer's plain speech, not a teacher's simplification or a fan's enthusiasm.** We keep the 25-word ceiling the Smithsonian and GOV.UK set, but not the Home Office's reading age of 9, because our learners need the trade's terms. We drop "magnificent" and "remarkable". Serrell's test is ours: the reader should finish feeling more competent.

**What we learned, by register**

- **Museum standards.** They agree on length and on starting from what can be seen. They say almost nothing about violence, colonial terms or unnamed makers, so our own rules must cover those.
- **Real labels.** Good institutions still open with the category, leave out the maker and reach for size words. The shield page shows that a revision can be told plainly.
- **Object-led public history.** Close looking and quoted voices are the strength. Inherited terms are the weakness: two of three use the perpetrators' name for 1897 as a label.
- **Design writing.** Point of view, rhythm and named people. De Bondt shows design writing can face colonial violence directly.
- **Decolonial guidance.** The subject is who has the authority to name and to speak, not only which words to use.
- **Plain language.** The mechanics can be scripted. The reading age is set for public services, not for professionals learning a field.
- **Learning products.** "You" works in tasks and fails in reference text. One-right-answer tasks clash with neutral success criteria.

## 4. Calibration passages

All passages are ours. Every fact comes from the F1.24 or F1.25 spec.

**1. Object card (Label tier), F1.25 case C**

*Weak:* "This exquisite Asante gold weight is a masterpiece of African art. Its antelope with enormous horns reflects the ancient wisdom of a wealthy kingdom whose skilled artisans created thousands of such pieces, a testament to their mastery of metal."

*Strong:* "An antelope, cast in the 1800s as a weight for gold dust. Its caster first modelled it in wax; the museum credits an 'Akan-style artist'. Such weights were used since at least 1600, the record says. In the museum's reading, the horns may allude to the proverb 'had I known'. Its history before a Paris gallery in 1935 is not recorded."

*What changed:* object first; the caster credited; the museum's attribution and "may" marked as the museum's; the gap named; the filler gone.

**2. Walk step, F1.24 case 1**

*Weak:* "In 1897 the famous Benin Bronzes were discovered by the British during the Punitive Expedition and brought to Europe, where they amazed the world. This plaque eventually found its way to Cleveland."

*Strong:* "Content note: British forces' assault on Benin City in 1897. — Igun Eronmwon casters made this plaque for the Oba's palace in the 1500s or 1600s. In 1897 it belonged to Oba Ovonramwen. After the assault, Ralph Moor sent it to England. The British Museum held it until 1950, then sold it through a sales agent. It reached Cleveland in 1999. Its record calls 1897 a 'Siege'. Which word would you print?"

*What changed:* every link has a person and a verb; our plain noun replaces the British term; the record's word is quoted as the record's. ("Assault" is held for a reader, per F1.24 §9.)

**3. A contested claim, F1.25 claim 8**

*Weak:* "Nomadic tribes of the Ordos, fierce horsemen on China's frontier, probably made this buckle, although some experts think it may possibly have come from China."

*Strong:* "Who made this buckle is argued. One reading: casters in the Ordos steppe made it for their own people. The other: a Chinese workshop made it for steppe clients. Gold belt plaques from Xigoupan tomb 2, inscribed *shaofu*, are attributed to a Qin state workshop, so the second reading has a precedent. Cleveland's record, 'Ordos region, Western Han dynasty', allows both. We do not choose between them."

*What changed:* both readings get equal grammar; the record shows why the question stays open; "tribes", "fierce horsemen" and the stacked hedge go, and the steppe appears as makers and clients.

**4. A content note, F1.25 case B**

*Weak:* "Warning! Disturbing content ahead. The following section deals with the tragic Long Walk of the Navajo, a dark chapter in history. Viewer discretion is advised."

*Strong:* "Content note: this step describes Hwéeldi, the US Army's forced removal and imprisonment of Diné people. From the fall of 1863, US cavalry forced about 8,500 Diné to Fort Sumner, and over 2,000 died there. The figures come from a Diné-authored source. No images of people are shown. You can skip this step; the summary keeps the dates."

*What changed:* subject, actor and sourced numbers replace mood words; the Diné name comes first; the skip is real. (The 1863 date is held for the pre-read, per F1.25 §9.)

**5. An unnamed maker, F1.25 case B**

*Weak:* "This beautiful Navajo blanket was made by an anonymous weaver. Navajo women were famous for weaving skills passed down through generations in a timeless tradition."

*Strong:* "A Diné woman wove this blanket in about 1875–80. The record does not give her name. It does keep her choices: handspun wool, Germantown yarn, and bayeta, a trade cloth unravelled and rewoven. We read those choices as a designer's. The record does name the donor, J. H. Wade, who gave the blanket to Cleveland in 1921."

*What changed:* the gap moves from the weaver to the archive; her materials stand in for her name; our reading is marked; the named donor makes the unit's point.

**6. A cultural note, F1.24 case 3**

*Weak:* "Indigenous content. This section may contain culturally sensitive Aboriginal artefacts. Please be respectful."

*Strong:* "About this material. Gweagal people made the four spears in this story. They were taken at Kamay (Botany Bay) on 29 April 1770. On 23 April 2024, Trinity College Cambridge returned them to the La Perouse Aboriginal Community. We show no image of them; the link goes to Trinity's account of the return. We do not speak for Gweagal people or say how the spears may be used."

*What changed:* makers and place come first by their own names; it says what we do and claims no permission.

**7. Apply prompt, Maker lane, F1.24**

*Weak:* "Now it's your turn to play curator! Pick an object that means something to you and tell its amazing story. Let your imagination fill in any gaps!"

*Strong:* "Pick one object in your world: an heirloom, a tool, a second-hand chair. Write its chain as dated links: who made it, who held it, and how it moved (made, bought, given, inherited, taken, found). Give a source for each link. Where you have none, write 'not recorded' and leave it. Then write 150 words on the link you are least sure of."

*What changed:* explicit task, verbs and length; "fill the gaps" becomes "never fill them".

**8. Apply prompt, Briefer lane, F1.25**

*Weak:* "Leverage your brand's heritage story! Analyse one of your products and identify opportunities to connect authentically with its cultural roots."

*Strong:* "Choose one thing your organisation sells, gifts or calls 'heritage'. Read it in five layers: material, technique, labour, trade, meaning. Tag each sentence seen, recorded or guessed. For each 'not known' a supplier or maker could answer, write one question. The answers belong to them: record only what they publish or agree to share."

*What changed:* a method replaces marketing verbs, and the prompt says who owns the answers.

**9. Historian reply, F1.24**

*Learner:* "Should Cleveland's plaque go back to Nigeria?"

*Weak:* "Great question! Yes, the Benin Bronzes were stolen and should definitely be returned to Nigeria, their rightful owner. Museums that keep them are on the wrong side of history."

*Strong:* "I can't say who should own it; the program doesn't rule on that. Here is what is on record. Cleveland's chain runs from the Oba's palace through 1897 to the British Museum, which sold the plaque in 1950. Germany transferred ownership of 1,117 Benin objects to Nigeria in 2022, and a 2023 declaration named the Oba owner and custodian of returned objects. Who museums should deal with is argued: the palace, or the National Commission. Which link in Cleveland's chain would a return request turn on? *Sources used: F1.24 claims 2, 6, 7.*"

*What changed:* no verdict; the record instead; "argued" where it is; one question and the sources.

**10. An uncertainty reply, F1.25**

*Learner:* "What animals are on the buckle?"

*Weak:* "The buckle shows two deer, a classic motif of steppe animal style that symbolised the sun and rebirth for nomadic warriors."

*Strong:* "You can see two long-eared animals, but our record does not name them. We could not confirm a species, so I won't guess. The Met's Xianbei belt plaque, which its record describes as a winged horse, is a useful comparison. What would you need to see to name the buckle's animals, and who could tell you?"

*What changed:* seen and recorded are separated, memory is refused (Historian rule 1), and the gap becomes a test.

**11. A success message, F1.25 Lab**

*Weak:* "Amazing work! [confetti] You're a natural historian. You've mastered slow looking!"

*Strong:* "Autopsy saved to your sources record. All five layers are filled, and 14 sentences are tagged: 6 seen, 5 recorded, 3 guessed. Your labour layer names two people; neither of them made the object. Next: the concept, about 7 minutes. You can reopen the autopsy at any time."

*What changed:* it reports and counts instead of praising, and passes on the Mirror's question without a verdict. (The counts are an example of a learner's own data.)

**12. Five-minute summary, F1.24**

*Weak:* "In this unit we explored the fascinating world of museums and how they collect objects. We learned that provenance matters and that restitution is a complex, nuanced topic."

*Strong:* "Three museums, one event. Cleveland says its plaque was sent to England 'following the Siege of Benin (1897)'. The Art Institute of Chicago says its plaque was 'taken during the British military raid'. The Smithsonian says the palace was 'burned and looted'. Each wording matches its source; the verb is each museum's choice. Before you cite a provenance, ask who wrote it, and where it is silent."

*What changed:* the case, not a description of the unit; "complex, nuanced" becomes the actual difference.

## 5. Vocabulary

| Avoid | Use instead | Why |
|---|---|---|

**Arabic and other non-Latin names**

- **Endonym first.** The name the community or place uses comes first. The outside name follows once, in brackets: Kamay (Botany Bay); Diné Bikeyah. Endonyms are not italicised (N5).
- **Arabic in running text.** We follow IJMES: no diacritics on personal names, place names and titles, but ʿayn (ʿ) and hamza (ʾ) are kept, except an initial hamza. Words with an established English spelling (in Merriam-Webster, per IJMES) keep it. Technical terms (*mithqal*) are italicised and glossed at first use.
- **Full form in Identity.** The node card's Identity field holds the original script and a full transliteration with diacritics. Running text uses the short form; the two are linked.
- **Other orthographies.** Keep the marks a community uses in its own writing: Edo tone marks as the Met prints them, Diné (Hwéeldi), Māori macrons. Our specs currently write "Igun Eronmwon" without tone marks. The Edo form should go in Identity and be checked with an Edo reader before the Label tier uses it. Chinese uses pinyin, with tone marks in Identity only. Kyrgyz terms follow UNESCO's spelling (F1.25).
- **The Gulf.** In our own voice, always "the Gulf". Magan is the Oman peninsula, today Oman and the UAE. An institution's or state's own title is quoted exactly as published (Q25).

## 6. Lint checklist

A script runs the first group and a person the second; every drafted tier, prompt and reply passes both.

**Script**

1. The first sentence of each paragraph or tier is 16 words or fewer.
2. No sentence is longer than 25 words; flag any paragraph averaging above 20.
3. No word from the §5 avoid list, including AI filler and praise. Case-insensitive, whole word, outside quotation marks.
4. "first", "invented", "discovered" and "anonymous" outside quotation marks are flagged for review.
5. Perpetrators' terms ("punitive", "Siege of Benin") appear only in quotation marks, attributed in the same sentence, never capitalised as an event name.
6. A bare single year for an object date is flagged against the record's range.
7. Each sentence stating a fact has a claim ID (Deep tier, partner replies).
8. Confidence words match the claim's register value: "argued" requires two readings; "probably" requires a probable claim; "we read it as" requires an interpretive one.
9. "You" does not appear in Line, Label or Deep; it must appear in Apply prompts.
10. Partner replies are 3–6 sentences, end in a question or a test, and carry a "Sources used" line.
11. No exclamation marks outside quotations.
12. No "Persian Gulf" or "Arabian Gulf" outside quotation marks.
13. Every content note begins "Content note:" and contains a skip instruction.

**Person**

14. Does the text open on something that can be seen or checked?
15. Is the maker named, or the gap in the record named?
16. Is each community called by its own name first, spelt its own way?
17. Is violence named with an actor and a verb?
18. Does each tier stay true on its own?
19. Does any sentence rule on ownership, appropriation or a live debate?
20. Is any people described as goods, or ranked?
21. Would the persona this was tested against (Amara, Daniel, Mei or Omar) feel more competent after reading it?

## 7. What we did not take, and why

- **MacGregor's narrator.** One authored voice can carry personal judgement ("magnificent") and claims about whole eras. Our text is drafted at scale and read in fragments, where judgement compounds. We keep his close looking.
- **"You" in reference text.** The V&A and the Smithsonian encourage it on labels. Our tiers are reused on maps and in AI context, where "you" misplaces the reader.
- **A reading age of 9.** Our learners are professionals, and principle 9 keeps the trade's terms. We take the sentence ceiling only.
- **The Getty's "sense of discovery".** The idea is right, but the word collides with our ban, so we say "noticing".
- **One big idea per exhibition** (Serrell). Ours is per unit (the essential question); node text must stand alone.
- **Personal taste and rhetorical questions** (Rawsthorn, Adamson). In signed essays they work; in an unsigned program voice they read as verdicts.
- **OpenLearn's "correct place".** Our success criteria never take a side (H20).
- **The *Words Matter* list wholesale.** It was written for Dutch collections. We took its format; its glossary pages would not load, so no entry is quoted.
- **Te Papa's concepts as our vocabulary.** Mana taonga belongs to Māori practice. We cite it and never brand with it.
- **NMAI's acceptance of all the general terms.** In our own voice we use the specific name first.
- **Local Contexts icons.** Only communities can apply Labels; our notes say what we do and never imply permission.
- **Quoting historical racism to make a point.** MacGregor does it on radio. We allow it only in the Deep tier, attributed and with a reason (N6).
