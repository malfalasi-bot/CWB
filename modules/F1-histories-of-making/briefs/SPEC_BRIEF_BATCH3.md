## What each unit must carry

- **F1.25.** The full Object Autopsy, with five layers:
  - material;
  - technique, the layer added in v3.5;
  - labour;
  - trade;
  - meaning.

  Practice Reading is its sibling for a living practice (v3.6). Slow looking needs a source for the method; verify one (for example Harvard Project Zero's slow-looking work, or a museum's published protocol). Run the autopsy on three open objects from different worlds.

  **Steppe:** an openly licensed steppe object whose provenance passes or is honestly flagged. Candidates: Cleveland steppe bronzes or a felt or textile. Check the 1970 test, and apply the funerary rule.

- **F1.26.** The attribution and consultation protocol, built on CARE (collective benefit, authority to control, responsibility, ethics) and on Local Contexts Notices and Labels. Verify the current Labels licensing or fee terms with Local Contexts' own pages; finding 2.4 flagged a possible fee. Also cover:
  - free, prior and informed consent;
  - the UNESCO 2003 convention, cited from UNESCO's own text;
  - the relation-and-harm plane from F1.2.

  The briefer applies the protocol to a real brief, using a public case of a brand consulting, or failing to consult, a community. The protocol should be usable as a checklist, and it becomes the SRC "consultation" and "protocol" fields. Cases come from at least four worlds.

- **F1.27.** The counter-history research piece is the Mastery capstone for the learner's own lineage. The tool is Atlas authoring: the learner adds nodes and edges with sources and confidence. Specify:
  - the piece's form;
  - its attribution record;
  - how it is assessed, without taking sides;
  - one worked exemplar lineage. Pick one that crosses at least four worlds, for example blue-and-white, cotton, or paper.

  Link to the decolonial critique: pluralism is not decolonisation (Ansari; F1.3).

- **F1.28.** Primary sources and archives, and the Source Grader, which scores provenance, bias, distance and corroboration. Use primary texts from the TXT register in at least four worlds. Include:
  - one translation dated and compared, to show how translations shift;
  - how to read an archive's own catalogue as a source;
  - the briefer grading the sources in a designer's presentation.

  **Steppe:** a source such as Rubruck's or Carpini's account, the Sogdian Ancient Letters, or the Secret History of the Mongols. Use what has a free text you can open.

- **F1.28a.** Reconstruction as a way of knowing. The anchor is the Making and Knowing Project: BnF Ms. Fr. 640 and its open edition. Verify the edition's content licence; the software is MIT. Add at least two reconstructions from other worlds with published sources. Candidates:
  - experimental archaeology of Indus beads or bronze casting;
  - reconstructed Viking or Polynesian voyaging canoes (Hōkūleʻa, with care);
  - Japanese sword or paper making;
  - Andean khipu reconstruction.

  The method:
  - one variable at a time;
  - record what the text omits;
  - label the reconstruction as an interpretation.

  The Fabricator is the partner voice.

- **F1.29.** Oral history, folktale and local taxonomies, with consent. The Oral History Kit sets the consent flow (free, prior and informed consent), narrator review, restrictions and the withdrawal route. Use Oral History Association principles; verify their current text. Also cover:
  - the learner data policy in Phase 4 Foundations, option A: recordings stay on the learner's device;
  - local taxonomies, for example plant or fibre names, or colour terms;
  - folktales about making.

  **Steppe:** for example the Manas epic or akyn improvisation (UNESCO-listed; verify the listing years on UNESCO's own pages), or Kazakh and Kyrgyz craft vocabularies.

  Notice and Label fields apply. Never publish sacred or secret material.

- **F1.30.** Other calendars and periodisations: the Calendars tool shows the learner's lineage on another calendar. Calendars to cover:
  - Hijri;
  - Chinese and East Asian reign eras and the sexagenary cycle;
  - Hindu and Śaka;
  - Mesoamerican (the Long Count, the 260-day count);
  - Ethiopian;
  - Hebrew;
  - Persian (Solar Hijri);
  - Japanese eras.

  Also cover periodisation as an argument (link F1.3, where PeriodO is the model), and dating methods with their uncertainty: radiocarbon calibration ranges, and the BP versus BCE/CE conventions. Use calendar-conversion references you can open.

  **Steppe:** the twelve-animal cycle as used across Turkic and Mongol peoples, or a steppe dating problem such as the Botai revision from the steppe chapter.

- **F1.30a.** Counterfactual history with discipline:
  - one changed variable;
  - consequences traced with sources;
  - the exercise labelled as fiction.

  Models are the Narrative Environments tradition and design fiction; verify sources such as Dunne and Raby or Bleecker's 2009 essay. Historians' critiques and defences of counterfactuals go in as two readings, for example Evans's *Altered Pasts* (2014) against Ferguson's *Virtual History* (1997). The Stranger tests whether the counterfactual world's rules hold. Give worked examples that change one making-relevant variable in at least four worlds. They must never trivialise atrocity.

- **F1.31.** The regional slot, authored locally: the design constraint from finding 2.5. Specify:
  - what the slot is;
  - who may author it;
  - the authorship and review requirements;
  - how it plugs into the Lineage view;
  - a template, with the minimum sources, a living-practice requirement and the consultation record.

  Give one worked demonstration of the slot's structure, not its content, for one region: the Gulf and the UAE is the user's own region, and Al Sadu (PR-001) is in the register. State plainly that its content must be authored locally, not written by us. Plan how a slot's quality is reviewed.
