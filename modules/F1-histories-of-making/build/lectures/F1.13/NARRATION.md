Every factual sentence carries its claim id in square brackets; the ids are `specs/F1.13.md` §3. Bracketed stage notes in italics are not spoken. Content notes are spoken, as learners hear them, and each carries its skip line. Framing lines, look-fors and tap prompts carry no id because they state no fact.

## Title

F1.13. Networks: objects, materials, skills and people. One jar's blue, followed through all four.

## Station 1 — Where did the blue come from?

A jar, painted in blue under its glaze [2]. Potters at Jingdezhen made it in the 1300s; Cleveland holds it as 1962.154 [2]. The record does not give the potters' names [2].

We read a network as four flows at once: objects, materials, skills and people [1]. They share routes, but not terms [1].

One tap before we go. Where did the blue come from: Jingdezhen's own hills, Iran, or Europe?

*[pause 4 s; reveal]*

Cleveland's record says the cobalt was imported from Iran, likely from Kashan [2]. Jiang and colleagues tested later imperial wares, from the Xuande reign of 1426 to 1435 [3]. They write that early blue-and-white relied heavily on imported cobalt from the West [3]. We write "from Iran" [2, 3]. So the potters and the clay were at Jingdezhen, and the blue came from Iran [2, 3]. Look for the dashed line: it marks a material, not an object.

## Station 2 — Blue before China

A footed bowl from Iran, with a blue rim [5]. Cleveland dates it late 1100s to early 1200s and places it probably at Kashan [5]. It is fritware, pierced, and painted under the glaze [5]. Kelekian sold it to the museum in 1915 [5]. So blue under a glaze was in Kashan's workshops a century before the jar [5, 2].

Which mine gave the cobalt is argued [4]. One reading: Qamsar, near Kashan, the most cited source [4]. Abu'l-Qasim's treatise of 1301 places lâjvard, the blue, there [4]. The other reading: deposits near Anarak, 200 kilometres east of Kashan [4]. Jiang and colleagues propose Anarak in their discussion, not their abstract, as one of the most likely sources [4]. We do not choose between them [4]. Look for the two fuzzy marks: an argued claim gets two readings, not one dot.

## Station 3 — By sea

Content note: this station names enslaved people listed in a text of the mid-1st century. You can skip to station four; the summary keeps the dates.

Kilwa's merchants, on the Swahili coast, dealt from the 13th to the 16th centuries in gold, silver, pearls and perfumes, UNESCO's listing says [6]. They dealt also in Arabian crockery, Persian earthenware and Chinese porcelain [6]. Kilwa minted coins in the 11th to 14th centuries [6]. Its Great Mosque has domes and vaults, some set with Chinese porcelain [6]. The listing does not name Kilwa's builders [6].

Far to the east, a ship sank off Belitung in the 9th century: about the 830s by one account, late 800s by another [7]. It held 60,000 ceramics, and it was salvaged commercially from 1998 [7]. We link to that record and show no image of it [7].

The Periplus, a text of the mid-1st century CE, lists enslaved people among exports [8]. We write them as people, in words; the translation's own terms stay in the Deep tier [8]. Look for the people mark: it never shares an arrow with crockery.

## Station 4 — By land

Content note: this station names people moved by order. You can skip to station five; the summary keeps the dates.

A corridor from Chang'an into the Tianshan [9]. UNESCO lists it as a 5,000-kilometre section with 33 sites [9]. It formed between the 2nd century BCE and the 1st century CE, and was used until the 16th century [9].

A skill travels differently from a jar. One tap on the timeline: the earliest documented paper mill in the Islamic world, which band?

*[pause 4 s; reveal]*

Paper was known, and made, in Central Asia for centuries before 751, Bloom writes [10]. The earliest documented paper mill in the Islamic world is Baghdad's, in 794 to 795 [10]. The Talas story, that the battle of 751 carried paper west, is argued: Bloom calls it without factual basis, and F1.27 gives both readings [10].

People travelled too, and not by choice. Three separate weaving communities were moved from Central Asia and Persia to China, to make cloth of gold for the Mongols [11]. The summary names no weaver [11]. Look for the timeline: the skill is a dated line; the people are a mark of their own.

## Station 5 — Across the sand

Content note: this station names enslaved people carried across the Sahara. You can skip to station six; the summary keeps the dates.

Gold was the main commodity of the trans-Saharan trade, the Met's essay says, followed by kola nuts and enslaved people [12]. The essay's own noun for those people is different; ours is "enslaved people" [12]. Mansa Musa's gold, spent in Cairo in 1324 to 1325, caused the market in gold to crash [12].

The Catalan Atlas is attributed to Cresques Abraham, Majorca, 1375; the BnF holds it as Espagnol 30 [13]. It names Musse Melly, lord of Guinea, "the richest and most distinguished ruler of this whole region", for his gold [13]. That caption is a European mapmaker's framing, quoted as his [13].

The argued question. One reading: demand for silk, porcelain and gold built these routes and kept them [14]. The other: routes already there, monsoon sailing, caravans and ports, made such goods possible and shaped them [14]. We hold both [14]. Look for the caravan line and the dated gold: hold both readings of how they relate.

## Station 6 — Across the Pacific

Content note: this station names people enslaved and carried across the Pacific. You can skip to station seven; the summary keeps the dates.

A basin from Puebla, tin-glazed earthenware, about 1650 [16]. The Met credits it to "Master Potter A", a scholars' name for a hand the record does not name [16].

One tap: how much of the silver mined in New Spain and Peru went to Asia? A tenth, a third, or nine tenths?

*[pause 4 s; reveal]*

As much as one-third, by one estimate; we call that probable [15]. Galleons carried porcelain, silk, ivory and spices to Acapulco [15]. Mexican ceramics display the impact of the galleon trade most vividly, Hecht writes [15].

On the same ships, people. Enslaved people from many communities in the Indian subcontinent and Southeast Asia were carried to Mexico [17]. Spanish records grouped them as chinos, a colonial label for people from many places [17]. From 1672 the law counted them as Indians, who could not lawfully be enslaved, Seijas writes [17]. Look for the people mark beside the silver arc, never on it.

## Station 7 — The blue goes north

Content note: this station names potters taken by force. You can skip to station eight; the summary keeps the dates.

A dish with two birds in a water landscape [18]. One tap on the timeline: when was it made?

*[pause 4 s; reveal]*

The Rijksmuseum dates it about 1600 to 1625: China, porcelain in underglaze blue, Kraak ware, potter not named [18]. The blue that came from Iran now leaves China as export ware [18, 2]. How this dish reached Amsterdam is not recorded in the claim [18].

A second route carried a skill, and the people who held it. In Hideyoshi's invasions of the 1590s, many Korean potters were taken to Japan [19]. The Met's essay says they "came"; Korean state-funded sources say they were dragged from Joseon, so our verb is "taken" [19]. Korean potters found porcelain clay at Arita early in the 1600s [19]. A Korean state foundation names one of them, Yi Sam-pyeong [19]. The Dutch then filled the gap left by China, the Met writes [19]. Look for the people mark from Korea to Arita: it carries a name where the record gives one.

## Station 8 — The Atlantic

Content note: this station is about the Atlantic slave trade. Traders forced about 12.5 million African people onto ships, 1501 to 1866, and many died at sea [20]. We show no images of bodies or ships. You can skip this station; the summary keeps the count.

SlaveVoyages estimates that 12,520,000 people departed Africa, and that about 10.7 million disembarked, mainly in the Americas [20]. We draw no diagram: people are a count in words, never a line on a map.

The goods are drawn, as type. European traders bought captive African people with goods: Indian cotton cloth, then Manchester copies, and brass manillas [21]. Guns, gunpowder and alcohol appear in summaries only; we call that part probable [21].

One person the record names. Omar ibn Said was born in Futa Toro, about 1770 [22]. He was captured in 1807 and brought to South Carolina [22]. In 1831, while enslaved in North Carolina, he wrote his life in Arabic [22]. "I continued seeking knowledge for 25 years," he wrote [22]. James Owen of Bladen County was his enslaver [22]. He died, still enslaved, in 1863 or 1864; the sources differ, and we give both years [22]. Look for his two places, Futa Toro and the Carolinas: dots with his name, and no arrow.

## Station 9 — A lenj on the slipway

A wooden hull on a slipway at Guran, on Qeshm island, in the Gulf [24]. The skills of building and sailing a lenj went on UNESCO's Urgent Safeguarding List in 2011 [23]. Sailors used the sun, moon and stars, and each wind was given a name [23]. Practitioners are mostly older people, and fibreglass replaces wood [23].

In 2017, shipwrights at Guran told a photographer how they learned the trade from their fathers, and how orders dried up [24]. We link to that report; their words and names stay theirs until their cooperative has been asked [24].

Across the water, Bahrain's pearling site [25]. UNESCO says pearling dominated between the 2nd and early 20th centuries, until its demise in the 1930s [25]. The page does not say who dived, or on what terms; that is for F1.31's local author to write [25].

Four flows, then. The jar's blue was a material, the dish an object, paper and the lenj skills [1]. The weavers, the potters, the chinos and Omar ibn Said are people [1]. One jar, four flows, and terms that were never shared [1]. Which flow does your own work's material travel on, and on what terms?

## What we simplified

- The map draws land outlines only, with no borders of any date, because the claims give routes and places, not polities.
- Arc origins written "by region" (Arabia, Iran, China's coast, the Sahel, Central Asia, Joseon) stand at a gazetteer town inside the region; the claims name no port or goldfield there.
- Each arc is one line for a flow the claims describe in words; relay trade, port to port and oasis to oasis, is not drawn stop by stop.
- The timeline gives the eleven bands equal width, so a year's position is compressed inside its band.
- Station 1's "from Iran" folds claim 2's record and claim 3's "from the West" into one line, as the spec's own wording does.
- The galleon span, 1565 to 1815, is taken from the title of the Met essay cited in claim 15, not from its text.
- The Kraak dish's journey to Amsterdam is drawn as one arc marked "route not recorded"; the claim gives only where it was made and where it is held.
- Omar ibn Said's purchase at the Fayetteville jail, the date 1810 (NCpedia) and the brothers Owen are left to the Deep tier; the lecture says only "James Owen of Bladen County was his enslaver".
- The Atlantic goods are a list in type. We did not draw the triangle: a goods arc to West Africa would sit beside a human crossing we refuse to draw.
- No image of any object is in this build; each object card shows the record as type, with the holder, accession, date and licence, and leaves the image slot for the CC0 file.
