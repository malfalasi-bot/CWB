The Indian Ocean world

the record: “Indian Ocean trade; Maritime Silk Road (western)” · canon NET005, with the sub-circuits NET006 Arabian Sea, NET007 Bay of Bengal, NET008 South China Sea

A wind that reverses twice a year let printers in Gujarat, potters in Zhejiang and builders at Kilwa work for buyers they never met.

as defined by
historians (K. N. Chaudhuri; Abdul Sheriff) and archaeologists, who made “the Indian Ocean world” a unit of study in the late 20th century (canon NET005; not re-checked here). Its western half was described port by port by the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, a Greek-Egyptian merchant’s text of the mid-1st century CE (TXT073). Named from outside and after the fact: the sailors, merchants and makers inside it used their own names for seas and routes.
kind
network: a set of sea routes, ports and merchant communities, not a state or a people

Indian Ocean world

NET005 · c. 2500 BCE – … · defined by historians (K. N. Chaudhuri; Abdul Sheriff) and archaeologists

Monsoon-timed sailing linked textile, bead, ceramic and metal makers from East Africa to China. The umbrella row: the three circuits beneath it are drawn when selected; the others fall to a quarter.

leaves out (canon): enslaved people moved through the same circuits; coastal and island makers are often reduced to entrepôts.

Arabian Sea circuit

NET006 · c. 2500 BCE – … · the record: “Erythraean Sea trade” · defined by historians; the Periplus (1st c. CE)

The western circuit moved Gujarati and Malabar textiles, pepper, beads, incense and Chinese ceramics between India, Arabia, the Red Sea and East Africa. Drawn: the two Gulf-to-Kilwa legs and the Gujarat-to-Fustat leg, all undated.

leaves out: the canon row carries no “leaves out” line; listed for the editor.

Bay of Bengal circuit

NET007 · c. 400 BCE – … · defined by historians (Sunil Amrith); archaeologists

Exchange between eastern India, Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia carried glass and stone beads, rouletted ware, Buddhist and Hindu image-making and textile patterns.

no leg drawn: the repo holds no placed point and no leg inside this circuit; nothing is invented in its place.

leaves out (canon): South Asian craftspeople working in Southeast Asian ports.

South China Sea circuit

NET008 · c. 500 BCE – … · the record: “Nanyang trade” · defined by historians; maritime archaeologists

Exchange between southern China, Vietnam, the Philippines and the Malay world carried jade, bronze drums, stoneware, porcelain and shipbuilding traditions. Shown: the south China coast by region and the Belitung wreck; the repo holds no leg inside this circuit, so none is drawn.

leaves out: the canon row carries no “leaves out” line; listed for the editor.

what it leaves out
The inland miners, hunters and farmers who supplied gold, ivory and grain; the sailors and caulkers; the printers and dyers, almost never named; women, who appear mostly through men’s letters; the people who were enslaved and forcibly moved along the same routes, whom the old sources list among cargo.

content note · enslavement · human flows

This walk names people who were enslaved and moved by force across this ocean, from at least the 1st century CE into the 1800s (step 8). The Periplus lists them beside cloth and grain; we name them as people, in words. A flow of people is never drawn with the grammar of a goods flow: no mark rides a trade leg, and a human count is a range on a card, never a bubble that grows or a number that climbs. Numbers moved across this ocean are not known with any precision. The tombs at Kilwa (step 5) are described, not shown. You can skip step 8; the span strip keeps its dates.

the walk · nine steps · 1st century CE – 2011

Nine steps across the water

step 1 of 9 · mid-1st century CE

The wind that turns

Sailors timed their year to a wind that blows one way, then the other. Over the Arabian Sea the southwest monsoon runs from about June to September; the northeast monsoon blows in winter. The Periplus credits a pilot, Hippalus, with laying a course “straight across the ocean” (§57).

confidence: wind timing documented · that Hippalus found the route: contested (see What is argued) source: Periplus of the Erythraean Sea (Schoff translation, ToposText); “Monsoon of South Asia” (Wikipedia)
step 2 of 9 · c. 830s (one account) · late 9th century (another)

A ship sewn together

Shipwrights stitched this hull plank to plank with cord, without nails. It sank off Belitung, Indonesia, in about the 830s. Michael Flecker found “every timber was fastened with stitching”, in African Afzelia planks with teak through-beams from India.

confidence: construction and timbers documented (Flecker) · built in the western Indian Ocean: probable · the date is a fuzzy bar: the register holds both accounts protocol level 5, excluded: the Belitung cargo is named here and nowhere drawn; no image, no drawing of our own (commercial salvage from 1998) source: Flecker, A Ninth-Century Arab Shipwreck in Indonesia (Smithsonian)
step 3 of 9 · c. 800–1400, the canon’s approximate spans

Seven ports, seven towns

Ports were towns where people settled, married and prayed, not only stops. Siraf served the Abbasid Gulf; excavations from 1966 found a “massive haul” of Chinese and Islamic pottery. Aden, Calicut, Hormuz and Malacca each held one sea gate; Quanzhou’s Qingjing Mosque and a statue of Mani show a mixed merchant town.

confidence: documented (Whitehouse’s excavations; UNESCO 2021 for Quanzhou) · the dates for Aden, Calicut, Hormuz and Malacca are the canon’s, approximate source: “Siraf, Iran” (Current World Archaeology); Quanzhou: Emporium of the World in Song-Yuan China (UNESCO World Heritage)
step 4 of 9 · c. 1010 (±55) to the late 1600s

Cloth printed for Cairo

Printers and dyers in Gujarat stamped this cotton for buyers in Egypt. The fragments were dug from rubbish mounds near Fustat in the 1920s and 1930s. Radiocarbon dates in the Ashmolean’s Newberry collection run from about 1010 CE (±55) to the late 1600s.

confidence: made in Gujarat, found at Fustat: documented (museum attributions; Barnes) · workshops and the makers’ names: not known source: Shaikh, Made in India, Found in Egypt (The Met, 2023); Barnes, Indian Block-Printed Textiles in Egypt: The Newberry Collection (Ashmolean)
step 5 of 9 · 9th century to c. 1500

Bowls set into walls

Potters in China made glazed bowls and dishes that reached the Swahili coast. Twenty sherds of 9th-century Changsha ware came from Shanga; Longquan greenware and Jingdezhen blue-and-white followed. From the 13th century, Swahili builders set such bowls into the façades of pillar and dome tombs.

confidence: finds and dates documented (the twenty sherds as counted in Carfantan’s survey; UNESCO Kilwa) · what the bowls meant to their owners: interpretive (display; from oral tradition, protection) the tombs are described, not shown (funerary; content note above) source: Carfantan, Chinese Porcelain and Ceramics from the East African Coast (SEACS); Ruins of Kilwa Kisiwani and Ruins of Songo Mnara (UNESCO World Heritage)
step 6 of 9 · 17 October 1132

A merchant’s letters

Abraham Ben Yiju, born in Ifriqiya about 1100, was trading from Mangalore by 1132. He ran a bronze workshop and sent pepper, cardamom and brass bowls to his partner Madmun in Aden. On 17 October 1132 he freed an enslaved woman, Ashu; she took the name Berakha.

confidence: letters, trade and the deed documented (Goitein; Princeton Geniza Project) · that Ashu became his wife: probable (most specialists) · her own voice: not recorded source: Legal document IOM D 55.10 (Princeton Geniza Project); “Abraham Ben Yiju” (Wikipedia)
step 7 of 9 · c. 1512–15 to the mid-1930s

Families across the water

Merchant families lived on both shores for generations. Gujarati traders ran cloth, beads and credit and settled in Malacca, Mombasa and Zanzibar; Tomé Pires recorded them in Malacca c. 1512–15. By the mid-1930s the Hadrami diaspora numbered about 110,000, a third of all Hadramis.

confidence: documented · the 1930s figure: documented as cited (Wikipedia, “Hadhrami diaspora”, after Engseng Ho, 2006) source: “Tomé Pires” (Wikipedia); “Hadhrami diaspora” (Wikipedia)
step 8 of 9 · 1st century CE – 1873 · content note above

People moved by force

Enslavers moved people across this ocean from at least the 1st century CE into the 1800s. The Periplus lists enslaved people beside cloth and grain (§13, §31, §36); we name them as people. In lower Iraq, enslaved workers cleared salt-crusted land and rose in revolt (869–883); Zanzibar’s slave market closed in 1873.

confidence: documented · who the rebels of 869–883 were: contested (see the list twin) · numbers moved across the ocean: not known with any precision; no count is drawn protocol level 2: the content note, once, above the walk; people as a chip on their own layer, never a mark on a leg source: Periplus of the Erythraean Sea (Schoff translation, ToposText); “Zanj Rebellion” (Wikipedia)
step 9 of 9 · 2011

A lenj on the slipway

Shipwrights on Iran’s Gulf coast still build wooden lenj by hand. UNESCO inscribed the skills of building and sailing them in 2011, on its Urgent Safeguarding list. Navigators read the sun, moon and stars and named each wind; in 2011 the practitioners were few and mostly older.

confidence: documented, dated to UNESCO’s 2011 file · present number of yards: not known to us display basis · protocol level 3 · shown as published by the photographer, 2017; link-out · the course notices, it does not label source: Traditional skills of building and sailing Iranian Lenj boats in the Persian Gulf (UNESCO ICH, 2011); “Construction of Iranian Lenj boats” (Wikipedia)

afterlives

Where it went

The word “calico” is named after Calicut (canon PLC221). Kilwa Kisiwani and Songo Mnara were inscribed as World Heritage in 1981; Quanzhou followed in 2021 as “Emporium of the World in Song-Yuan China”. Siraf is now under the village of Taheri and near oil and gas works (Current World Archaeology, undated web page).

The Belitung cargo was sold to Singapore’s Sentosa Leisure Group in 2005 for US$32 million (the figure as given by Trafficking Culture); the Smithsonian cancelled its exhibition in 2011. In 2011 UNESCO recorded that fibreglass boats were replacing wooden lenj and that yards were turning to repair work.

notice · protocol level 1

Siddi (the record: “Sidi; Habshi”; COM203). The course’s TK/BC Notice text and icon are placed here at build, unaltered; the repo does not yet hold them, so the slot is named rather than filled. The course notices; the Labels are the community’s.

The Siddi descend from East Africans who came as soldiers, sailors, merchants and enslaved people. The 2011 census counted 10,477 in Karnataka and 8,661 in Gujarat (the Census of India, 2011, as cited by Wikipedia, “Siddi”).

“Maritime Silk Road” is the name many governments and tourist boards now use for these routes (canon other names). None of these afterlives is routed, so no journey bar is drawn.

what is argued

Two questions, two readings each

argument

Who found the monsoon route?

The Periplus (§57) credits the Greek pilot Hippalus with learning to cross open water.

the case: a text of the mid-1st century CE, one outsider’s claim, shown as that · Periplus of the Erythraean Sea (Schoff translation, ToposText) · claim a-hippalus, contested

Arabian Sea sailors trading between Africa, India and Southeast Asia knew the pattern long before.

the case: the monsoon’s timing is older than any text that names it · “Monsoon of South Asia” (Wikipedia) · claim a-hippalus, contested
argument

Whose coast?

Colonial-era writers framed the Swahili towns as “Arab” or “Persian” colonies.

the case: the colonial historiography, kept as data and flagged · canon CIV004, “defined by” · contested

African archaeology since the 1960s reads them as African towns working in Indian Ocean trade.

the case: Chittick, Horton and Chami (canon CIV004); the Chinese bowls in tomb façades were placed by local builders who chose where they went (Carfantan) · contested

Neither reading is coloured and no choice is marked right: a lean is a position. Two further questions the draft argues, the revolt of 869–883 and whether a salvaged cargo should be shown, are carried on the list twin with both readings.

apply

One lens to switch on

Switch on the “human flows” lens and the globe gains a layer of people: Ben Yiju and Ashu on the Malabar coast, the people moved by force along the Periplus’s coasts, the Siddi and the Gujarati merchant communities. They appear as chips on their own layer; no mark rides a trade leg, and no count climbs.

The lens is a layer the learner inks; it leaves the same way. The draft’s Maker and Briefer tasks (three blocks for a buyer abroad; an order from Madmun in Aden set by the sailing season) are on the list twin.

Also argued

  • What was the revolt of 869–883? One reading: a rising of enslaved East Africans, “Zanj”, against Abbasid landowners. The other: Ghada Talhami and M. A. Shaban argue a wider uprising of the poor, with free Africans, Bedouin and peasants (“Zanj Rebellion”, Wikipedia). Both are shown; neither is marked right.
  • Should a salvaged cargo be shown? The Smithsonian judged that showing the Belitung finds would condone “commercial exploitation of unscientifically excavated objects”; Singapore and Indonesia read the cancellation as a slight (Trafficking Culture). The Atlas links out and states the salvage history.

Apply, from the draft

  • Maker: the Met’s 14th-century Gujarati print (30.112.28) copies woven silk patterns from Iran and Egypt in stamped, mordant-dyed cotton. Design up to three blocks that give a costly surface through a cheaper process, for a buyer in another country; note what your process can and cannot copy; write a maker’s credit for the printer.
  • Briefer: write a one-page order from Madmun in Aden to a workshop in Mangalore for bronze bowls. Set the deadline by the sailing season, not a calendar date; name who carries the risk if the ship is lost; name the workshop’s makers, even as “the casters of Ben Yiju’s workshop”, and state what you do not know about them.
