# F1 Deep Dives 2 — seven worlds, the networks, and two new chapters

2026-09-28 · @Mohammad AlFalasi

## Scope and method

This round runs deep dives on the seven remaining worlds and on the two chapters that Q1 proposes. Together with F1.5 and F1.6 in [Deep Dives 1](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/fb8b1156-e84f-49b2-a97b-ff5fdadba58d), that covers every world chapter and the networks unit. Each follows deep dive template v2 and names its groupings by kind, as set out in [F1 Atlas Logic](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/d60856d8-7c8e-4a13-b844-36c328f13067).

**Each deep dive gives, in the same order:**

- the unit's device, its reach (the sub-regions and periods it covers), and the groupings it uses;
- the claims, each with a source and a confidence;
- the object set, checked live for licence, date and provenance;
- the living practice;
- protocol notes;
- the walk;
- Apply by lane;
- success criteria and links out;
- what the deep dive changed.

**Assumed.** Q1–Q12 and P1–P11 as recommended, so the two new chapters are written in full. If Q1 is declined, their material moves into F1.5, F1.10 and F1.13 as world-hub content.

**How the checks were done.** Object records were read live from the Cleveland Museum of Art and Metropolitan Museum open APIs on 28 September 2026. Cleveland records carry provenance; Met records do not, so every Met object is marked "provenance unchecked" until its web page is read. Claims come from UNESCO listings, museum essays, scholarly articles and, where noted, encyclopedia summaries that must be replaced before G3. The provenance test is the one from Deep Dives 1: an excavation record, a documented history outside the country of discovery before 17 November 1970, or a documented legal export.

**Kept short on purpose.** Each deep dive holds only what the unit needs: 10–16 claims and 6–10 objects for the taught path. Everything else found along the way is logged for the world hub rather than squeezed into the 50-minute unit.

## F1.13 Networks: objects, materials, skills and people

The unit follows one blue-and-white jar and the cobalt that coloured it, then widens to every route the jar's world touched. The deep dive changed three things. New research points the cobalt route to a different mine, so the mockup's dashed line moves. A famous shipwreck became a case about the ethics of salvage rather than an exemplar. And the Atlantic step now counts people from the scholarly database, as people.

**Reach.** The Silk Roads, the Indian Ocean, the trans-Saharan routes, the Manila galleons and the Atlantic, from the 2nd century BCE to the 19th century. The Mediterranean belongs to F1.11 and the Pacific voyages to F1.12, each handed on by name. **Groupings used:** networks (the Silk Roads corridor, the Indian Ocean, the Manila galleon route, the Atlantic); an interaction sphere (the Swahili coast towns); polities (Mali, the Yuan); materials (cobalt, gold, silver).

## F1.12 Australia and the Pacific, read through navigation

This is the chapter with the deepest time and the thinnest open record. People reached Australia at least 50,000 years ago and were grinding axe edges before anyone else in the world; Pacific navigators settled the largest ocean on Earth without writing or metal. Yet the two big open museum APIs offer almost nothing here that is both licensed and appropriate, so the chapter is built on heritage listings, community-led sources and living practice, and it says so.

**Reach.** Australia (added by Q1), Melanesia, Micronesia and Polynesia, from at least 50,000 years ago to today. **Groupings used:** an archaeological culture (Lapita); communities (Gunditjmara; Marshallese; Native Hawaiian); a network (the Lapita and later voyaging spheres); maker communities (navigators, *ri-metos*); the environment lens (ocean, atoll, lava flow).

**Claims**

**Protocol.** This chapter runs on the strictest settings. Human remains and ancestral objects are never shown. The Historian pre-reads as a Pacific specialist, and this chapter goes to a paid sensitivity read as soon as budget exists (P11).

**The walk**

3. **The date argument.** Madjedbebe: 65,000 or about 50,000, both readings drawn.
4. **Farming the water.** Budj Bim: channels and weirs in volcanic stone, six thousand years of eel harvest, a system still running.
5. **Pots that crossed water.** Lapita: one decorated ware from the Bismarcks to Fiji in a few centuries, the trail of a voyaging people.
6. **The model of the sea.** A *rebbelib*: swells, currents, islands, memorised and left on land.
7. **The revival.** Hōkūleʻa, 1976 and 1980: a navigator from Satawal teaches Hawaiʻi to sail home.
8. **Who may use this.** Australia's protocols, and what a designer must do before drawing on Country.

## F1.10 The Islamic world as a network, read through routes and the waqf

The chapter's two devices hold. Routes explain how paper, cobalt, cotton and coins moved; the waqf explains who paid for the buildings, lamps and tiles, and why endowment deeds are the best record of named craftsmen the pre-modern world has. The deep dive also found the chapter's best copying case: three "Mamluk" glass pieces in Cleveland are 19th-century Paris and European work, one of them inscribed with a Mamluk sultan's name.

**Reach.** From Baghdad's first paper mill (790s) to the Ottoman 16th century, across Egypt, Syria, Iran, Anatolia, al-Andalus, the Swahili coast and the Indian Ocean, with the Gulf as the hook for the regional vertical. **Groupings used:** a belief tradition (Islam, with non-Muslim makers inside it named); polities (Abbasid, Fatimid, Mamluk, Ottoman); a network (the Indian Ocean; the Cairo–Hormuz–India route); an institution (the waqf); maker communities (calligraphers; Iznik potters; Mamluk builders); a diaspora (the Geniza's Jewish traders).

## F1.7 The Americas, read through fibre

Fibre is the right device because in the Andes it came first: cotton before pottery, indigo before anyone else, and a state that ran its accounts on knotted cords. The deep dive widened the chapter north and east, to Diné weaving after the Long Walk and to the Amazon's mound and garden cities, and it found a clean repatriation case in Paracas.

**Reach.** The Andes, the Amazon, Mesoamerica and the North American Southwest, from 7,800 years ago to the present; the Arctic, Northwest Coast and Eastern Woodlands stay logged for the world hub. **Groupings used:** horizons (Early, Middle and Late in the Andes); archaeological cultures (Paracas, Marajoara); polities (the Inka state); a community (Diné); a maker community (Diné weavers, named as women artists in the records); the environment lens (desert coast, highland, rainforest).

## F1.8 East Asia, read through the workshop

The workshop is the device because East Asia's great objects were made by organised groups working to written standards: bronze foundries casting in piece-moulds, a state building code with a modular unit, an imperial porcelain bureau ordering hundreds of thousands of pieces, and a city that still has 100,000 ceramic workers. The deep dive's main finding is that the living practice is not a revival but a working industrial city with a young migrant workforce.

**Reach.** China, Korea and Japan from the Shang (c. 1600 BCE) to today; the Jōmon deep end sits in F1.5. Mongolia and the Tibetan plateau go to the steppe chapter and the world hub. **Groupings used:** dynasties (Shang, Song, Goryeo, Yuan, Ming); maker communities (foundries; the Jingdezhen bureau and the civil kilns; the *jingpiao* newcomers); a movement (Mingei, as the Atlas Logic example); a technique family (piece-mould casting, celadon inlay, underglaze blue).

## F1.9 South and Southeast Asia, read through cotton

## F1.11 Europe and the Mediterranean, read through the guild

Europe is one world among several, and the guild is its device: a written rule for who may make, how they learn, and what they may sell. The deep dive widened the chapter to Mediterranean antiquity (Q1), which turns out to strengthen the device, because Roman potters stamped their names on mass-produced bowls a thousand years before Paris wrote its guild statutes. **Reach.** The Mediterranean and Europe from Attic pottery (6th century BCE) to the Great Exhibition (1851), with Byzantium, the Balkans, Scandinavia and the Sámi logged for the world hub. **Groupings used:** polities (Athens, Rome, Paris, the Dutch Republic, Britain); maker communities (Attic workshops, Arretine firms, Paris trades, Delft potteries, Wedgwood's factory, the *compagnons*); a style (black-figure); a movement (the reform circle around the 1851 exhibition); networks (the Roman Mediterranean; the Atlantic, handed to F1.13).

## New chapter: West Asia before Islam, read through the account

The proposed device is the account: the world's earliest writing is a list of rations, its earliest known glass-blowers signed their cups, and its earliest copper trade is known from bills of lading. West Asia is where making first became a matter of record, so the chapter teaches record-keeping as a technology alongside metal, glass and cloth. It also puts the Gulf into F1's first millennia, which the regional vertical will need.

**Reach.** Mesopotamia, Iran, the Levant, Anatolia and the Caucasus, Arabia and the Gulf, from the first cities (4th millennium BCE) to the eve of Islam. **Groupings used:** polities (Uruk, the Assyrian and Achaemenid empires, Rome in the east); archaeological cultures (Luristan bronzes as a style-and-culture label); a network (the Gulf copper route: Magan to Sumer); maker communities (scribes; Levantine glass-blowers; Luristan smiths); materials (copper, glass, wool, purple dye).

## New chapter: the steppe and Central Asia, read through the horse

The horse is the device: the steppe's makers built for movement, and what they made (felt, harness, chariots, portable houses, silk for the road) shaped every settled world around them. The deep dive's main finding is that the steppe's best-known "first" has just been overturned. Botai, taught for a decade as the place horses were first domesticated 5,500 years ago, is now read by geneticists and archaeologists as a hunting site, with modern domestic horses spreading from the Volga–Don steppe about 2000 BCE. The chapter teaches the revision itself.

**Reach.** From the Pontic steppe to Mongolia, with Sogdiana and the Altai, from the 4th millennium BCE to the present. **Groupings used:** archaeological cultures (Yamnaya, Sintashta, Pazyryk); a diaspora (the Sogdian merchants); a network (the Silk Roads corridor); a technique family (felt, pile knotting, compound-weave silk); polities (the Mongol empire); the environment lens (grassland, high altitude, permafrost).
