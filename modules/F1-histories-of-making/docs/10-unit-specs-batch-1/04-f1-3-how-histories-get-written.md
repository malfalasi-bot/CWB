## F1.3 How histories get written: canons, periods, the timeline as an argument; design history versus design studies

Every design timeline makes an argument about who counts, and someone made it at a known date. The learner learns to read a canon, a period and a civilisation label as claims by a source, and to annotate what they leave out.

| Opening move | **Argument first (form 13).** Claim: "A timeline just puts things in order." Counter-claim: "A timeline is an argument. Its start, its breaks and its names were chosen by someone." | Concept | About 650 words, in five short parts. (1) **Canon:** a list of named makers that sets a standard (Vasari; Pevsner as a later example). (2) **Hierarchy of the arts:** academies (Florence 1563, Paris 1648, London 1768) and the Salon split "fine" art from "decorative" or "applied" work; craft, art and design became ranks, not only kinds. (3) **Periods:** a period is a definition by a source with a place and dates. Thomsen's three ages were built on Danish collections and are always regional. (4) **Civilisation as an argument:** Mirabeau to Childe's criteria, which later readers turned into a test; Toynbee and Huntington as historiography; Graeber and Wengrow (2021) against a single path of stages. F1's rule: civilisation is a search label, never ranked. (5) **Design history versus design studies:** Margolin (1992) and Forty (1993). A closing note: adding more cases to a canon (pluralism) is not decolonisation (Decolonising Design, 2019). |

**3. Claims (20)**

1. The earliest known French use of "civilisation" is by Mirabeau in *L'Ami des hommes*. Larousse dates it 1756; Wikipedia says 1757. — documented (the date is contested; give both). — https://www.larousse.fr/encyclopedie/divers/civilisation/34231 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civilization — summary.
2. The word stood for progress and was "opposed to barbarism or rudeness". — documented. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civilization — summary.
3. In Mabo (No 2), 1992, Brennan J quoted the 1919 Privy Council: "Some tribes are so low in the scale of social organization…". He called that basis "false in fact and unacceptable in our society". — documented. — https://alor.org/Storage/Library/Mabo%20v%20Queensland%20[No%202].html — full text (an unofficial mirror; AustLII returned 403).
4. Childe's 1950 *Town Planning Review* article set out a ten-point model of the "urban revolution". — documented. That later readers used the list as a test for "civilisation" is interpretive (our framing). — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_revolution — summary.
5. Toynbee's *A Study of History* traced the rise and, mostly, decline of 21 civilisations. Huntington argued the 21st century would see a "clash of civilizations". — documented. The canon row says "some two dozen"; align it. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civilization — summary.
6. Graeber and Wengrow (2021) argue that people tried many social forms rather than passing through fixed stages. — documented as their argument. Two readings: supporters take it as a break with stage models; one reviewer calls it "a very defensible argument already made several times". The brief's wording "no single original form" is not verified verbatim; a review quotes "no 'original' form of human society". — https://www.sapiens.org/culture/dawn-of-everything-excerpt/ (excerpt); https://notevenpast.org/a-false-dawn/ (review) — partial text.
7. Thomsen named the Stone, Bronze and Iron Ages while arranging Denmark's national collection. He published the scheme in *Ledetraad til nordisk Oldkyndighed* (1836). — documented. — https://www.britannica.com/biography/Christian-Jurgensen-Thomsen — summary.
8. For PeriodO, a period needs a name, time bounds, a region and "some citable source". An authority is "a set of periods that share a source". — documented. — https://perio.do/technical-overview/ — full text.
9. The Met holds the 1568 edition of Vasari's *Le vite* with woodcut illustrations, catalogued as public domain. — documented. — https://collectionapi.metmuseum.org/public/collection/v1/objects/358298 — full record.
10. The Florentine Academy of Design was founded in 1563 by Cosimo I, "at the instigation of" Vasari, and set "in contrast to the guilds". — documented. — https://www.britannica.com/place/Academy-of-Design — summary.
11. The French and English royal academies were founded in 1648 and 1768. From the late 18th century they had "a virtual monopoly" on taste and official patronage. — documented. — https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-salon-and-the-royal-academy-in-the-nineteenth-century — full essay (Rosenfeld, 2004).
12. The Royal Academy's 36 founders included two women, Mary Moser and Angelica Kauffman. The next woman became a full Academician in 1936. — documented. — https://www.royalacademy.org.uk/page/a-brief-history-of-the-ra — full page.
13. Kristeller (1951) called Batteux's 1746 treatise "the decisive step" towards the system of the fine arts. — interpretive (a scholar's thesis). — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Batteux — summary.
14. In 1892 Bent concluded that Great Zimbabwe's builders were "of a Semitic race and of Arabian origin". — documented. — https://www.gutenberg.org/files/69067/69067-h/69067-h.htm — full text.
15. Randall-MacIver (1906) and Caton-Thompson (1929) found Great Zimbabwe to be African work; Randall-MacIver named the ancestors of the Shona. In the 1970s Rhodesia censored guidebooks and museum displays about it, and Garlake was forced to leave in 1970. — documented. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Zimbabwe ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Garlake — summary (citing Sinclair and De Baets); also CL-134.
16. Blair and Bloom (2003) call a distinct "Islamic" art tradition "a product of late nineteenth- and twentieth-century Western scholarship". They add that much of it was not made by Muslims for the faith. — interpretive (a scholarly position, widely cited). — https://www.collegeart.org/pdf/artbulletin/Art%20Bulletin%20Vol%2085%20No%201%20Blair%20and%20Bloom.pdf — full text.
17. The 1910 Munich exhibition showed about 3,600 objects from more than 260 collections. It raised things once seen as "folk art or arts and crafts" to "masterpieces". The Met's 1930 handbook still said "Mohammedan Decorative Arts". — documented. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meisterwerke_muhammedanischer_Kunst (summary); https://www.metmuseum.org/met-publications/a-handbook-of-mohammedan-decorative-arts (publisher page).
18. Margolin (*Design Studies*, 1992) proposed folding design history into design studies. Forty replied in the *Journal of Design History* (1993), and *Design Issues* ran a special issue in 1995. — documented. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Design_studies — summary.
19. The Decolonising Design manifesto (Abdulla, Ansari and others, 2019) says "It is not sufficient for design institutions to simply include a greater diversity of actors or perspectives." — documented. — https://jfsdigital.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/12-Ansari-Decolonising-Design.pdf — full text.
20. The record is uneven. Cleveland's CC0 set held 42,316 records on 29 Sept 2026: 2,544 in Chinese Art, 388 in African Art and 294 in Islamic Art; a keyword search for "Oceania" gave 112. The AIC Asante kente wrapper (1901/50) is flagged "not public domain". Ghana, as State Party, nominated Kente to UNESCO in 2024. — documented (the counts are a snapshot). "The record is biased" is interpretive. — https://openaccess-api.clevelandart.org/api/artworks/?cc0=1&limit=1 ; https://api.artic.edu/api/v1/artworks/65187 ; https://ich.unesco.org/en/decisions/19.COM/7.B.38 — full records.

**Unit framing (interpretive):** a timeline is an argument by a source; civilisation is a search label, never a rank. Cleveland's Islamic count does not show the "strong on the Islamic world" pattern that the decided addition states. That pattern may hold for the Met; a checker must confirm it before the claim is written.

**6. People and credit**

- **Named on the record:** Vasari (author); Sultan 'Ali al-Mashhadi (calligrapher); Sa'di and Hafiz (poets); Bent (author); Randall-MacIver, Caton-Thompson, Garlake and Paul Sinclair (archaeologists); Mary Moser and Angelica Kauffman (RA founders); Victor Margolin, Adrian Forty, and the eight authors of the Decolonising Design manifesto.
- **Unnamed, and named plainly as unnamed:** the woodcut cutters of the 1568 *Lives*; the margin painters who remounted the Herat folio in the 16th century; the Shona-speaking builders of Great Zimbabwe; the Akan casters ("Akan-style artist" is the museum's phrase); the Asante weavers of the AIC wrapper.
- **Who the credit leaves out:** guild craftsmen outside the academies; women barred from life classes; non-Muslim makers inside "Islamic art"; the Zimbabwean and African scholars whose work the Rhodesian state suppressed.

**8. Success criteria**

1. The learner rewrites at least one period label as "X, as defined by Y (date)", with a real source.
2. The learner's annotation names at least two world chapters absent from the line, and one thing the record cannot show.
3. The learner writes one sentence that separates adding cases to a timeline from changing how it is framed, using their own annotation as the example.

**9. Readings and risks**
