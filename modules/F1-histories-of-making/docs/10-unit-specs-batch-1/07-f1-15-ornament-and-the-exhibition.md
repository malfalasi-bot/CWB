## F1.15 Ornament as a global language; the exhibition as taxonomy

Patterns are related by how they were made, by who met whom, or by who displayed them together, and the museum record shows which.

**1. Responsibility block**

The unit must reach these canon groupings and types. Owen Jones PER415, Adolf Loos PER425, the Great Exhibition EVT001, the design reform movement MOV004 and the South Kensington Museum INS052 carry the European classifying story. Islamic geometric pattern construction GAP026, muqarnas TEC168, the Mamluk Sultanate POL042 and Mamluk style STY104 carry the geometric theory of West Asia and North Africa. The Kuba Kingdom POL022, Kuba peoples COM042, Kuba textile design STY089, Kuba raffia textile making PRA006 and technique TEC125 carry central Africa. Khipu OBT139, khipu construction TEC104 and the khipukamayuq MKR024 carry the Andes. Li Jie PER164, the Yingzao fashi TXT092 and the Song POL166 (Northern Song period PRD099) carry East Asia. Vanuatu sand drawings PRA212 with Ni-Vanuatu COM278, and Kakai Tonga COM291 with barkcloth OBT047, carry the Pacific. Māori COM288 enters only through the kōwhaiwhai link-out.

Sub-regions: WA-LEV (Damascus), WA-IRN (Isfahan), AF-CEN, AM-AND, AS-CHN, OC-MEL, OC-POL, EU-BLC, EU-WCE. Periods run from 1103 (Yingzao fashi) to 2006 (the Cleveland geometry show).

World chapters reached with a case or example: F1.10 Islamic world (case A; girih), F1.6 Africa (case C), F1.12 Australia and the Pacific (case B; sand drawing; kōwhaiwhai), F1.8 East Asia (Yingzao fashi), F1.7 Americas (khipu), F1.11 Europe and the Mediterranean (1851, Jones, Loos). **2. Protocol screen**

- **Excluded: the Chester head.** Jones's first chapter reproduces a tattooed head "from the museum at Chester". The Met identifies it as a preserved Māori head (mokomokai), then thought to be a woman's. That is human remains. Its woodcut and any plate showing it are removed from every use of AS-001, including thumbnails and the Pattern Comparer. The story may say that Jones used it, in words only, with toi moko repatriation linked (OCC266, OCC271).
- **Kōwhaiwhai.** Painted rafter sequences in a wharenui depict the descendants of the founding ancestor. We show no specific kōwhaiwhai image and draw none. Link out to Deidre Brown's Te Ara entry only. A Māori reader clears the wording before G3. Notice required.
- **Vanuatu sand drawings.** UNESCO notes drawings carry "sacred or profane meanings". We reproduce no named drawing. Our diagram shows only a generic line on a grid, captioned as a diagram of the method, not a sand drawing. Notice required; the holders' own site or the UNESCO page is the image source, linked.
- **Khipu (1940.469).** Indigenous-community material with no recorded findspot. Show it; Notice on the Andean communities that kept khipu into the 20th century; say the findspot is unknown.
- **Kuba cloth (1991.224).** PRA006 notes some cloths are buried with the dead. This cloth's use is not recorded; no content note needed, but do not call it a burial cloth. Notice on the Kuba peoples.
- **Tongan cloth (case B).** Jones calls its maker's people a "Savage Tribe". The phrase appears only in quotation marks, attributed, once. Notice on Kakai Tonga.
- **Loos's "Papua".** Quoted once, attributed, with a line saying the claim is his and was used to rank peoples.
- No altar objects or grave goods are used. No person is described as goods.

**3. Claims**

1. *Jones's Grammar of Ornament (London: Day and Son, 1856) has 100 chromolithographic plates in its first folio edition, with contributions by J. B. Waring, J. O. Westwood and M. D. Wyatt.* Documented. https://archive.org/details/McGillLibrary-rbsc_grammar-ornament_elfNK1510J71856-22115 — catalogue record.
2. *Its 20 chapters run from "Ornament of Savage Tribes" to "Leaves and Flowers from Nature", sorting ornament by people, place, faith and period.* Documented (chapter list); the reading "sorting by people" is interpretive. https://archive.org/stream/jonesowengrammar00owen/jonesowengrammar00owen_djvu.txt — full text (OCR of an 1856 copy).
3. *Proposition 8 reads: "All ornament should be based upon a geometrical construction."* Documented. Same URL — full text.
4. *Jones reports that on Tongatapu one woman designed all the patterns in use, and was paid a number of yards of cloth for each new one; the dress came via Oswald Brierly.* Documented as Jones's report; her name is not recorded. Same URL — full text.
5. *Jones reproduced a preserved, tattooed Māori head (mokomokai) from the Chester museum, then thought to be a woman's.* Documented. https://www.metmuseum.org/perspectives/grammar-of-ornament (Speelberg and Fleming, 2018) — full article; Jones's footnote in the full text above.
6. *Jones decorated the 1851 building in red, yellow and blue and helped select objects from the exhibition for the School of Design's teaching collection.* Documented. https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/owen-jones-and-the-grammar-of-ornament — full article.
7. *The 1851 exhibits were shown by country and divided into four divisions and 30 classes; over six million people visited.* Documented. https://www.rct.uk/group/381/content/explore/themes/great-exhibition-of-1851/the-exhibition — summary.
8. *The 1851 catalogue's introduction frames the show by "nations" and "every civilized people"; the word "race" does not appear in the introduction we read.* Documented for the text read. https://archive.org/stream/1851OfficialDescriptiveCatalogueSection1Intro_201711/EX.1851.22_djvu.txt — full text (OCR). The brief's "nation and race" framing therefore rests on Jones's chapter order and later exhibitions, not on this text: interpretive.
9. *Loos first gave "Ornament und Verbrechen" as a lecture in 1910; it appeared in French in 1913 and in German in 1929; the 1908 date was added for the 1931 reprint.* Contested. Reading one: 1910, following Christopher Long's research as cited. Reading two: 1908, the date in Loos's own 1931 collection and many anthologies. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ornament_and_Crime — summary; Long not opened.
10. *Loos wrote that the Papuan who tattoos "is no criminal", the modern man who does is "a criminal or a degenerate", and that the evolution of culture means removing ornament from everyday objects.* Documented. https://www.designtagebuch.de/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/adolf-loos-ornament-und-verbrechen.pdf — full text (from Sämtliche Schriften, 1962).
11. *Girih patterns were long read as lines drawn with compass and straightedge; Lu and Steinhardt argue that by about 1200 CE designers also used a set of five tile shapes, and that the Darb-i Imam shrine (Isfahan, 1453) is nearly quasi-crystalline.* Contested. Reading one: tile method, as argued. Reading two: a published comment in Science (2007) disputes the quasi-crystal reading. https://www.physics.rutgers.edu/~pchandra/physics601/Lu_Steinhardt.pdf — full text; the comment was not opened.
12. *The Topkapı Scroll (Timurid, late 15th or early 16th century) holds 114 geometric patterns for wall surfaces and vaulting.* Documented. https://www.getty.edu/publications/virtuallibrary/9780892363353.html — summary (free full PDF linked there, not read).
13. *The Yingzao fashi was completed by 1100 and printed in 1103; its 34 chapters cover measurement, carpentry, stonework, tiling, and painting and decoration.* Documented. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yingzao_Fashi — summary.
14. *Its timber module, the cai, has eight grades; each cai divides into 15 by 10 fen, and every component is measured in fen.* Documented. https://www.archdaily.com/949479/from-ancient-to-modern-modular-construction-in-chinese-timber-architecture (Miao, 2020) — summary.
15. *Vanuatu sand drawings are on UNESCO's Representative List (2008, 3.COM), first proclaimed a Masterpiece in 2003; drawers trace one continuous line on an imagined grid; UNESCO names a purely aesthetic reading as a threat.* Documented (harvested). https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/vanuatu-sand-drawings-00073 — full page.
16. *Kuba men weave the raffia and women embroider it; one analysis finds Kuba designs use 12 of the 17 plane symmetry types.* First part documented; the count is probable. It rests on Donald Crowe, Journal of Geometry 1 (1971): 169–182, which we saw only as a record. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuba_textiles — summary.
17. *Cleveland 1945.133 was published in 1946 as a "Venetian Bronze Bowl", shown in "The Venetian Tradition" (1956–57), and is now catalogued as Damascus, Burji Mamluk, 1450–1500; its panels hold six designs, each twice.* Documented. https://openaccess-api.clevelandart.org/api/artworks/1945.133 — full record.
18. *Exhibition histories group objects: 1945.133 and the Kuba cloth 1991.224 were both in "The Persistence of Geometry" (MOCA Cleveland, 2006); the khipu 1940.469 was in the "Exhibition of Peruvian Art" at the "Museum of Primitive Art", New York (1963–64).* Documented. https://openaccess-api.clevelandart.org/api/artworks/1991.224 and https://openaccess-api.clevelandart.org/api/artworks/1940.469 — full records.
19. *Six Santa Valley khipus match a colonial census record; colour and the direction of cord attachment appear to encode villagers' names.* Documented for the match; interpretive for names (as CL-166). https://dukeupress.wordpress.com/2018/01/31/groundbreaking-study-on-incan-khipus-published-in-ethnohistory/ — summary.
20. *Unit framing: a likeness between patterns is a claim of method, contact or label, and exhibitions and catalogues make most label claims.* Interpretive (ours).

Not used as a claim: Ron Eglash's African Fractals (1999). We read only Bangura's review (Nexus Network Journal, 2000; https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00004-999-0019-3.pdf, full text). It reports Eglash's argument that recursive scaling is intentional in African settlements, braiding and windscreens, and the reviewer's two questions: what distinguishes African fractals, and how self-similarity is measured. Eglash may appear in the Mastery reading list as an argued position, attributed. He should not appear as a fact until the Historian has read the book.

**8. Success criteria**

1. In the Pattern Comparer, the learner labels at least one pair as method, contact or label, and cites the record field or source line that shows it.
2. The learner's GRM.f entry states a making rule that a second learner can follow to draw a matching unit without seeing the original.
3. The Briefer's SRC line names the maker community by its own name, the channel and date of extraction, and one thing the brief will not take.
