## Civilisation page: Aksum and Lalibela, as commonly named

A granite stele 24 m high went to Rome in 1937 and came back in three pieces in 2005; the churches cut from the rock at Lalibela never left, because the Church still prays in them.

**3. A place to stand**

A field of granite stelae stands on the north side of Aksum, in Tigray. The tallest still standing rises more than 23 m and is carved to show a building of nine storeys, with false doors and windows. The largest of all, some 33 m, lies where it fell, perhaps as it was being raised. Munro-Hay reckoned it "among the biggest single stones ever quarried by human labour". Beneath the field are tombs, which this page never shows. Stele 2, 24 m and 150 tons, stood in Rome from 1937 to 2005. Who cut these stones is not recorded.

**4. The name, and who gave it**

"Aksum" is the kingdom's own name. Its kings struck it on coins from the late third century and cut it into stone. The Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, a Greek sailing guide of the 1st century CE, calls the capital "Axomites". Ezana's inscriptions of the fourth century run in Greek, Sabaean and Ge'ez. "Aksumite" as a period label is Ethiopian and European scholarship's. "Lalibela" is a king's name given to a town and its churches. UNESCO's "New Jerusalem" glosses the Church's own naming of the river Jordan and the churches of Golgotha and Bethlehem. "Abyssinia" came from outside and the canon files it as an exonym (CIV017). This page uses the kingdom's name, the king's name for the town, and the Church's own name for itself.

**5. Span and phases**

Every definer gives a different span, and the facts block lists each with its owner. UNESCO's text says the kingdom "lasted from the 1st to the 8th centuries". The same listing dates the ruins "from between the 1st and the 13th century". Wikidata starts the kingdom in the fourth century BCE, "circa", and ends it in 960. The canon runs 50 or 100 to 940. Beneath the label sit D'mt and Yeha (c. 800–400 BCE). Then Endubis's coins, c. 270–300 on the British Museum's dating and c. 295–310 on the Ashmolean's. Then Ezana's conversion in the fourth century, the sixth-century war in Himyar, and "decline", in UNESCO's word, in the tenth. Then the Zagwe, c. 1137–1270, dates debated, and Lalibela's reign, c. 1181–1221. The archaeologists read the churches as cut in phases over at least eleven centuries (§12). No PeriodO definition was found.

**6. Extent**

The map draws a soft-edged area for about 350 and captions it "sites attested; borders inferred". Aksum, Yeha, Adulis, Matara and Qohaito are attested. The Periplus gives the route: Adulis, three days inland to Koloe, "the first trading post for ivory", five more to the metropolis. Every edge is inferred; the highland plateau is drawn as the likely core and nothing more. The sixth-century reach across the Red Sea to Himyar is drawn as a dated arrow and not as a border. For Lalibela the map draws a point in Lasta. The eleven churches sit in two groups across the river the Church calls Jordan, with Biete Giyorgis apart. The morph runs from Yeha to Aksum to Lasta, and the attested points move south.

**7. Its making**

Quarrymen cut granite at Gobedra, west of the town. The stone was dragged several kilometres to the field. Carvers worked the faces of the largest stelae into storeys: beam-ends, window frames, a door with a bolt. The 33 m stele broke into three pieces when it fell. The 24 m stele was cut into three to fly to Rome in 1937 and back in 2005. Die-cutters at the mint struck gold, silver and copper alloy. The British Museum's gold coin of Endubis, c. 270–300, shows the king between two wheat stalks. Above him sit a disc and crescent; the legend is Greek. The Ashmolean reads its Endybis coin as "king of the Aksumites, man of Daku". Ezana's later coins carry a cross. No die-cutter is named.

At Lalibela, cutters worked downwards into the volcanic rock. They isolated each church in a trench and then hollowed it out: doors, windows, columns, floors and roofs from one stone. Drainage ditches and passages joined them (UNESCO's description). The spoil heaps keep a stratigraphy, and the archaeologists found "different cutting phases" in them. The Church's tradition credits King Lalibela, and its account says angels helped; the canon records both as tradition. Scribes in monasteries prepared goatskin, mixed inks and bound gospel books in Ge'ez (PRA008, MKR011), a practice that continues. Many of those books left Ethiopia in 1868 from Maqdala (OCC147). No cutter, scribe or painter of the medieval churches is named in a record this pass found.

**9. What the name hides**

"Aksumite" hides the makers. No quarryman, carver, die-cutter or mason is named in the record, though three kings are. It hides the labour that dragged a 33 m stone and the people who died when it fell, if any did. It hides the enslaved people the Red Sea trade carried (CIV003), named here as people. It hides Himyar's subjects and the Beja. It hides the centuries between Aksum's decline and 1270, which the Solomonic chronicle treated as an interruption; the Zagwe's own dates are debated. "Lalibela" hides the cutters, and it hides the Church's own account, in which the king and angels did the work, beside the archaeologists' phases. Both hide the people living on the sites now: modern houses stand over most of buried Aksum, and the Lasta Tukuls stand beside the churches. And both hide the war since 2020, which this page could not verify.

**10. Neighbours and counterparts**

Beside Aksum, not inside it, lay Saba and Himyar across the Red Sea. Yeha's builders used their script before Ge'ez. Meroë, which Ezana's inscriptions claim to have attacked (Wikipedia, coverage only). The Beja between the plateau and the sea. Rome and Byzantium through Adulis, where the Periplus records ivory and tortoiseshell. The Sasanians, who took Himyar. For Lalibela there is the cave church of Washa Mika'el, which Derat (2021) reads as part of an earlier "troglodytic culture". There is Egypt's Coptic Church, source of Ethiopia's bishops. And there is Jerusalem, which the churches were cut to stand in for. No counterpart is ranked; Himyar sits beside Aksum, not under it.

**11. Who speaks for it now**

The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church holds Lalibela's eleven churches and Maryam Tsion as places of worship. UNESCO calls Lalibela "still today a place of pilgrimage and devotion" and says it has drawn pilgrims "since the 12th century". The state holds the law. Proclamation 209/2000 established the Authority for Research and Conservation of Cultural Heritage and prohibits export, except temporarily with the Minister's approval. Aksum is managed "at three levels – the site; the region; and the Federal administration" (UNESCO). Lalibela is administered by the regional and the Lasta district culture and tourism offices. A management plan "has not yet been established", in UNESCO's words at the time of its text. Italy returned the stele in 2005 and paid for its re-erection. The British Museum and the Ashmolean hold the coins; the British Library and others hold the Maqdala books.

Cultural note: the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church holds Lalibela's churches and Maryam Tsion as places of worship. The Tegaru and the people of Lasta live beside the monuments. **12. What is argued**

- *When were Lalibela's churches cut, and by whom?* The Church's tradition and UNESCO attribute them to King Lalibela, in the twelfth or thirteenth century. Fauvelle-Aymar, Bosc-Tiessé, Derat and colleagues (2010, 2014) read the spoil heaps and cuts as "different cutting phases". They span "at least eleven centuries (from the 10th to the 21st century)". Derat (2021) adds that the churches "were not created ex nihilo" but grew from an earlier highland practice of cutting into rock. The Church's account and the archaeologists' are both on record; the page does not choose.
- *When did Aksum begin and end?* UNESCO's own text gives two spans; Wikidata a third; the canon a fourth. The page gives all four with their owners.
- *When did Endubis strike his coins?* The British Museum says c. 270–300; the Ashmolean says c. 295–310. Both are quoted.
- *Did the stele belong in Rome?* Italy's own 1947 peace treaty bound it to return the stele (canon OCC213; the treaty not opened). Italy returned it in April 2005 and funded the re-erection; UNESCO called the reinstallation "a source of pride". The page reports the return and does not rule on other objects, including the Maqdala books.

**13. Afterlives**

Scribes and parchment makers still prepare goatskin and bind codices for the Church (PRA008). The coffee ceremony named in F1.16 is a living practice beside this page, not inside it. Remembered: the listings of 1978 and 1980; the Maqdala returns of 2002, 2021 and 2023. The inauguration of 4 September 2008 was tied to the end of Ethiopia's calendar year 2000. Sold: the stele on Ethiopian banknotes and in tourism, and "Abyssinian" on cats and coffee; we report that and do not endorse it. UNESCO records a museum begun in the stelae field in 2011 and flooding in the Tomb of the Brick Arches. That is a conservation note, not a judgement.
