## Civilisation page: al-Andalus, as commonly named

Five ivory carvers signed a casket in 1004–05; the label that gathers their work, "al-Andalus", is the makers' own, and what it hides is who was expelled.

**3. A place to stand**

A cylindrical ivory box, 11.7 cm high, carved in Córdoba in about 950–75. Birds, lions and gazelles sit inside vine scrolls. Its lid is missing; the Met reads that lost lid as the place where an owner's name and a date would have been carved. The box is Met 1970.324.5, bought by The Cloisters in 1970. The Met's label calls its carver "a master from the palace" of ʿAbd al-Rahman III. The record does not give his name. On a related casket, now in Pamplona, five carvers did sign (§6).

**4. The name, and who gave it**

"al-Andalus" is the makers' own name, not an outsider's. Arabic coins and inscriptions used it for the lands under Muslim rule from 711. Wikidata keeps it as the label, with "Muslim Spain" and "Islamic Iberia" as aliases. The outside names came later. Latin chroniclers wrote "Mauri", which became "Moors" and "Moorish" (CIV084), a word that flattens Arabs, Imazighen, converts, Jews and Christians into one racialised term. The museum trade coined "Hispano-Moresque" for the pottery and ivories. The Getty AAT files it as a style of "the Iberian peninsula under Muslim rule, roughly between the eighth and the 15th centuries". "Reconquista", the name Western historiography gave the Christian conquests, is a nineteenth-century label and is quoted here as theirs. This page uses the makers' name and reports the others.

**5. Span and phases**

Every definer gives a different span, and the facts block lists each with its owner. Wikidata runs 711 to 1492, from the crossing to the handover of Granada. The canon's "Moorish" row runs to 1614, the last Morisco expulsions, because makers under Christian rule kept working. Beneath the label sit polities with their own bounds. The Emirate of Córdoba begins in 756, the Caliphate in 929, and the Caliphate ends in 1031 after a civil war of 1009–10. The taifa kingdoms follow; then Almoravid and Almohad rule from Marrakesh; then the Nasrids of Granada from 1237 or 1238 to 1492. The Patronato de la Alhambra gives both years on two of its own pages. No PeriodO definition was found this pass.

**6. Extent**

The map draws three soft-edged areas from the site list, each captioned "sites attested; borders inferred". For about 1000 the attested sites are Córdoba, Madinat al-Zahra, Toledo, Zaragoza and Valencia; the northern edge is argued and drawn soft. For about 1150 the area reaches across the strait to the Maghrib, because the Almohads ruled both shores. That edge is inferred from the dynasty rows, not from sites. For about 1400 only Granada, Málaga and Almería are attested. Wikidata gives one point, 37°N 4°W, which is no border at all. The morph redraws the polygon across the three dates; it shrinks south and then to the Sierra Nevada.

**7. Its making**

Ivory carvers in the palace workshops cut boxes from elephant tusk. The Leyre casket is dated to the hijra year 395 (1004–05). Its inscription names the patron, ʿAbd al-Malik, son of al-Mansur, and the official who directed the work, "Zuhayr ibn Muhammad al-Amiri, his slave". Inside the lid a second inscription reads "made by Faray with his apprentices". The museum's record names four of them on the panels: Rasid, Jair, Sa'abada and Misbah. Where it was carved is argued (§12). A pyxis in the Hispanic Society is signed by Khalaf, who also signed a casket dated 966 at Fitero. For the mosque, Ibn Idhari records that al-Hakam II ordered the enlargement on 16 October 961. A mosaicist sent from Byzantium trained the caliph's enslaved apprentices, who then worked alone.

Weavers in Granada made lampas silks with Arabic inscriptions. The Met's fragment 29.22 wishes its owner "happiness, good fortune and prosperity". Potters at Manises, near Valencia, painted lustre on tin glaze through several firings, under Christian rule. The Met reads its dish 94.4.362 as made for the Buyl family, lords of Manises. Stucco carvers, tile cutters and carpenters made the Alhambra's rooms. The twelve lions of the fountain were, in the Patronato's words, "possibly executed by several artists". Their names are not recorded. Paper came from the Islamic world's mills (TEC185). No record names the weavers, potters or stucco carvers.

**9. What the name hides**

The label hides the Christians, Jews and converts who made things under it. It hides the Imazighen, whom the Latin sources call Berbers, who crossed in 711 and ruled from Marrakesh after 1086. It hides the saqaliba, enslaved men from Europe's edges; Zuhayr, "his slave" in the casket's own words, directed the palace workshop. It hides women, who appear as patrons (Subh, for whom the Zamora pyxis was made in 964) and never as makers. It hides the Mudéjar potters of Manises and the Moriscos, who kept making under Christian rule until the expulsions of 1609–14. It hides the expelled of 1492 and 1498, whose descendants Spain's 2015 law calls "injustamente expulsados". It hides the Calé, in Iberia from about 1425 (COM246). And it hides the present: the Jews and Muslims of Spain today, whose institutions are named in §11.

**10. Neighbours and counterparts**

Beside al-Andalus, not inside it, stood the Christian kingdoms of the north. They bought, took and reused its ivories as reliquaries. The Leyre casket reached a Navarrese monastery "as war booty or in payment of parias", the museum says, and held two saints' relics until 1862. Across the strait: the Idrisids of Fez, the Almoravids and Almohads of Marrakesh, the Hafsids of Tunis, who hosted Andalusi refugees (DYN029). Across the Mediterranean: Fatimid Egypt and Byzantium, whose emperor sent a mosaicist and "three hundred and twenty quintals" of tesserae in 965. South across the Sahara: Gao, where marble stelae cut in Almería stood in the royal cemetery (PLC030; origin to verify). No counterpart is ranked.

**11. Who speaks for it now**

Two communities have published bodies that speak for themselves. The Federación de Comunidades Judías de España issues the certificate that Ley 12/2015 names for proving Sephardic origin. The Junta Islámica wrote to the Pope in December 2006 asking to pray before the mihrab, "to bury past confrontations". The bishop of Córdoba refused the next day. The State holds the law: Ley 16/1985 bars export of any Bien de Interés Cultural and requires authorisation for the rest. The Junta de Andalucía runs Madinat al-Zahra through its Conjunto Arqueológico and the Alhambra through its Patronato. The Cabildo Catedral de Córdoba holds and runs the Mezquita-Catedral, and says it has done so since 1236. The Museo de Navarra holds the Leyre casket; the Met, the V&A and the Hispanic Society hold ivories and silks abroad.

**12. What is argued**

- *Was al-Andalus a culture of tolerance?* Américo Castro found Spain's culture in its medieval mixing. Claudio Sánchez-Albornoz traced it to pre-Roman times and denied the conquerors a crucial part (Akasoy's summary). María Rosa Menocal (2002) presents the diversity "commonly referred to as convivencia" as a defining feature of Andalusi making. Darío Fernández-Morera reads the same sources for "the otherness of al-Andalus" and the Maliki law's limits on Christians. Akasoy (2010) and the Claremont essay warn against romance in either direction. We do not choose.
- *Who owns the Mezquita-Catedral, and what is it called?* The Diocese registered it on 2 March 2006. It says it has owned the building "since 1236", citing a 2015 court order and a 2014 Treasury report. A committee for the city council concluded in 2018 that it "mantuvo durante 12 siglos su titularidad pública". The civic platform collected over 200,000 signatures for public ownership. Both are on record.
- *Should Muslims pray in it?* The Junta Islámica asked in 2004 and 2006. The bishop answered that shared use "sólo generaría confusión en los fieles". The page reports the request and the refusal and rules on neither.
- *Where was the Leyre casket carved?* The Museo de Navarra's record says the Madinat al-Zahra workshops. Jorge de Navascués argued for al-Zahira, al-Mansur's own palace city, from the marble basins that share its epigraphy. Both readings stand.
- *When was Madinat al-Zahra founded?* The Junta's 1997 decree says 936; its 2019 decree says building began in 940. The museum directory says founded 936, court moved 945. We give the range.

**13. Afterlives**

Lustre is still made at Manises. The Met's label says Christian potters re-established the industry after 1609; that is dated to the label, not to a maker. "Moorish Revival" (STY032) turned the Alhambra into cast iron, tile and synagogues from 1830 to 1930. Owen Jones's plates are the carrier. Remembered: the listings of 1984, 1994 and 2018; the 2015 law and its 2019 deadline; the 2006 registration and the arguments it started. Sold: the Alhambra's patterns on tiles, textiles and tourist goods, and the "Moorish" of estate agents and bars. We report that and do not endorse it. In 2016, 1.8 million people paid to enter the Mezquita-Catedral (El País).
