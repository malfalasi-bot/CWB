**3. A place to stand**

A sandstone lintel, 52.6 cm high, carved between about 875 and 925. Garuda, half man and half eagle, holds the centre; foliage turns into women and serpent heads at the edges. The stone is the grey-yellowish sandstone of the Angkor temples. It is Cleveland 1967.37, bought from the dealer Robert Rousset in 1967. Which temple it came from, and when it left Cambodia, the record does not say. That gap is the subject of this page's second chapter.

**4. The name, and who gave it**

"Khmer" is the people's own name, written in their inscriptions and spoken in Cambodia today. "Kambuja" is the kingdom's name in those inscriptions. "Angkor" is Khmer for "city", from Sanskrit nagara. The outside frames came later. French scholars of the École française d'Extrême-Orient made "Angkorian" a period. They turned a sequence of carved ornament into the chronology; Philippe Stern and Gilberte de Coral-Rémusat named the styles after temples (STY129). George Coedès gathered the region's polities as "les États hindouisés" in 1944, a frame the canon files as "Indianised states" (CIV054). UNESCO wrote the park into its list in 1992 as "Angkor". This page uses the people's name and the city's, and reports the frames.

**5. Span and phases**

The conventional span is 802 to 1431. It begins with Jayavarman II's consecration on Phnom Kulen and ends when the court left Angkor for the south. Wikidata and the canon both use it. Beneath it sit the capitals: Hariharalaya, then Yasodharapura from the late ninth century, Koh Ker from 928 to 944, then Yasodharapura again. The style phases run from Koh Ker through Baphuon and Angkor Wat to Bayon and post-Bayon. The ends are soft. Evans and colleagues write "9th–16th centuries". Buckley and colleagues date the droughts to the fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries and note that Angkor Wat stayed a monastery. The Post-Angkorian courts at Longvek and Oudong carry the label into the nineteenth century (PRD158). No PeriodO definition was found this pass.

**6. Extent**

The map draws at two scales, each captioned "sites attested; borders inferred". At the city's scale, the Greater Angkor Project's map covers nearly 3,000 km2 and shows a water network of more than 1,000 km2. We draw the mapped area as attested, and its edge as the survey's edge, not the city's. At the polity's scale, the attested points are Angkor, Koh Ker, Banteay Chhmar and Preah Khan of Kompong Svay. Phimai, at the end of the north-west road, is the fifth. Every edge is inferred. The reach into today's Thailand, Laos and Vietnam is drawn as a soft wash from the inscriptions' claims and the Cham war of 1177. The morph redraws the wash from 802 to 1431 and then shrinks the attested points to the Tonle Sap and the Mekong.

**7. Its making**

Content note: this section names the khñum, people bonded to temples. The inscriptions list them by name; they are people, not property, and both readings of the word are given. You can skip to §8.

Quarry workers cut sandstone in stepped benches at the foot of Phnom Kulen. The quarries lie about 30 to 35 km north-east of the temples. Uchida and Shimoda found more than 50 quarries there in 2013; a later survey counts 145. The blocks' magnetic susceptibility and thickness change with time, so each quarry can be dated to a building phase. A canal runs from the quarries toward Angkor; the field survey finds "a high probability" that it carried the stone. Carvers finished the blocks on site; the Royal Palace area holds unfinished sandstone images and carving waste.

Beside the Royal Palace of Angkor Thom, casters ran a bronze workshop. The EFEO, the University of Sydney and APSARA excavated it in 2012 and 2013. They found one forge and ten furnaces, and over 65 kg of clay mould fragments tempered with rice husk. Crucibles had been fired to 1,200 °C or more; the waste was copper, bronze and leaded bronze. The workshop dates to between the mid-eleventh and the twelfth century and was levelled in one event. No caster's name survives.

The temples themselves ran on people. Inscription K. 908, of 1191, records that people in 8,176 villages provided rice for 20,400 gods. Two of Jayavarman VII's temples employed over 30,000. The inscriptions list workers by name, sex, children and sometimes occupation; Aymonier called them "les interminables listes nominatives d'esclaves". The word khñum is read as "slaves" by some and as bonded people or servants by others (§12).

**9. What the name hides**

The label hides the makers' names. No inscription found this pass names a carver or a caster. It hides the khñum, whose names the inscriptions do record, in lists their early readers found too long to print. It hides the quarry workers and the canal. It hides the 8,176 villages. It hides the neighbours whose forces came and went: Champa, Ayutthaya, and the Burmese who later took Ayutthaya's Khmer bronzes to Mandalay. It hides the centuries after 1431, which the old label called a "dark age" (PRD158). It hides the people who live in the park now, more than 100,000 in 112 settlements. And it hides the trade: the dealers, the soldiers and the collectors of the 1990s, and the museums that bought from them.

**10. Neighbours and counterparts**

Beside the Khmer, not inside them, stood the Cham of Champa (CIV051). Their fleet reached Angkor in 1177, and their people still make pottery at Bàu Trúc (COM170). Đại Việt to the north-east (POL215). The Mon, and the Thai kingdoms of Sukhothai (POL212) and Ayutthaya, whose armies besieged Angkor in 1352–53 and 1393–94 (Wikipedia, coverage only). Java, named in the inscriptions as the power Jayavarman II broke from. China, whose envoy Zhou Daguan wrote the one outsider's account. Pagan in Burma and the kingdoms of Sri Lanka as parallel temple-builders. No counterpart is ranked; Champa sits beside Angkor, not under it.

**11. Who speaks for it now**

The Khmer speak for Angkor before anyone else, by their own name. The temples are Buddhist places in use; Angkor Wat has been a monastery since the fifteenth century. The APSARA National Authority was created by Royal Decree on 19 February 1995. It manages the park under the Ministry of Culture and Fine Arts, with almost 3,000 employees. Its missions include "dialogue with the communities living among traditional villages on site". The ICC-Angkor, set up on 13 October 1993, coordinates foreign teams. The 1996 Law prohibits export without a licence and seizes what is exported without one. The National Museum of Cambodia holds most of the statuary; the Conservation d'Angkor compound in Siem Reap was itself raided in the 1990s. Abroad, the Met returned 14 sculptures in 2023 and one more in June 2026, and says it is still reviewing its Khmer collection.

Cultural note: the Khmer are a living people, and Angkor's temples are places of worship. We show no act of worship, no monk, and no image taken from a shrine. The one object shown is a lintel whose findspot is not recorded. We name APSARA from its own pages and no other Khmer body, so this note is held. Ask us to change or remove this item.

**12. What is argued**

- *Was Angkor a "hydraulic city"?* Bernard-Philippe Groslier (1979) read Angkor as a city defined by its water network. He read its end as over-use of the soil. Van Liere (1980) and Acker (1998) disputed it. Evans and colleagues (2007) find a network over 1,000 km2 and changes "substantial enough to have created grave challenges". They call the debate unresolved before their map. Both readings stand on the same landscape.
- *What ended Angkor, and when?* The conventional date is 1431. Buckley and colleagues (2010) find two droughts, in the mid-fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries, that "contributed to its eventual demise" with other factors. Others stress the move of trade to the Mekong and Phnom Penh (Wikipedia, coverage only). Angkor Wat stayed a monastery, so "end" is itself argued.
- *Who were the khñum?* Aymonier and Coedès read the lists as "esclaves". Lustig and Lustig (2013) write that most of the population "was not exactly free, as we understand the term". People were bonded by inheritance, punishment, capture or debt. A 2013 account translates khñum vrah as "slaves of the god", and khñum as "slaves or bondsmen". The page writes them as bonded people and quotes the sources' words as theirs.
- *Which quarry fed which temple?* Uchida and Shimoda (2013) dated more than 50 quarries by magnetic susceptibility. They found none from the early Angkor period. The 2020 survey of 145 quarries reads the quarrying as moving counterclockwise. The method's assumptions are stated by its authors; the early quarries are not found.

**13. Afterlives**

Khmer weavers make weft-ikat hol silks in Takeo (PRA117). Sbek Thom puppeteers cut cowhide for the Reamker; UNESCO proclaimed Sbek Thom in 2005 and inscribed it in 2008 (PRA118). Both are dated to the canon's sources, not to a maker this page opened. The Khmer Rouge killed many of the artists who held these practices, and survivors rebuilt them after 1979 (OCC245). Remembered: the 1992 listing and the 1993 Santa Fe conditions; the returns of 2013, 2023 and 2026. Sold: Angkor Wat on the national flag, on beer labels and on the tickets that fund APSARA. Paris staged a replica in 1931 beside displayed people (EVT015). We report that and do not endorse it.
