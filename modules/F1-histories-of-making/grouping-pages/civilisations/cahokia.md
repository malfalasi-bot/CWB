**3. A place to stand**

Monks Mound rises in four terraces from the plain east of St Louis. Its base covers more than 14 acres. Its height is about 100 feet, or 30 m; measurements since 1882 run from 92 to 108 feet. Builders carried the earth in baskets, an estimated 22 million cubic feet, in stages over about three centuries. A building once stood on the top terrace. A concrete stair follows the line of a wooden one. The site calls it "the largest prehistoric earthen construction in the Americas". What its builders called it is not recorded. In March 2024 the Osage Nation's historic preservation office brought more than thirty Wahzhazhe people up it.

**4. The name, and who gave it**

"Cahokia" is a borrowed name. The State of Illinois named the site after the Cahokia, a tribe of the Illinois confederacy. They came to the area in the 1600s and, as the site says, "did not build the mounds". "Mississippian" is twentieth-century archaeology's label for the culture, after the river (CIV023). Before that, nineteenth-century European-American writers called the builders "Mound Builders", a lost race unrelated to the Native nations around them. The label served removal (CIV025). The Osage Nation calls the place part of its ancestral homeland and the people Wahzhazhe. This page uses the state's name as a label and reports the rest.

**5. Span and phases**

Every definer gives a different span, and the facts block lists each with its owner. The site's brochure runs from Late Woodland settlement about 700 to a Mississippian start in 800–1000. Then a regional centre after 1050, a peak in 1050–1200, decline from 1200 and abandonment by the late 1300s. UNESCO gives 800–1400 with a peak in 1050–1150. The canon's rows for the site and polity run c. 1050–1350. The AAT stretches "Mississippian" to the mid-eighteenth century and the Natchez. The Osage account places ancestral settlement at Cahokia from 900–1000 and a move west after 1300–1400. Beneath the label sit the phases archaeologists use: Lohmann, Stirling, Moorehead, Sand Prairie. Pauketat's "Big Bang" is the Lohmann phase, about 1050. No PeriodO definition was found this pass.

**6. Extent**

The map draws a soft-edged area for about 1100 and captions it "sites attested; borders inferred". The central site is attested: about 4,000 acres at its peak, of which Illinois protects 2,200. Some 120 mounds once stood; about 80 survive, and 72 are protected. Across the river stood the East St Louis group and St Louis's "Mound City". The Osage Nation's own account attests them; they were levelled by 1875. The farmsteads and satellite towns of the American Bottom are inferred. Beyond that, the map draws no border at all. Cahokian copper plates, engraved shell and Ramey jars reached Spiro, Etowah and Moundville, and those are drawn as find-spots, not as a reach of rule. Milner reads the polity as controlling "only within its immediate vicinity"; Pauketat reads a wider pull. The morph redraws the attested area from 1050 to 1350 and then empties it.

**7. Its making**

Content note: this section names Mound 72, a burial mound, in words only. No burial, grave good or human remain is shown or described. You can skip to §8.

Mound builders moved earth in baskets and laid it in stages. The mounds grew by accretion; the plaza itself was filled and levelled. Copper workers by Mound 34 took nuggets of native copper, brought from the Great Lakes or the south-east, and thinned them into sheet. Chastain and colleagues examined eight pieces from the workshop. They found them "thinned through repeated cycles of hammering and annealing" at temperatures "achievable in an open wood fire". No one melted copper there. Gregory Perino found the workshop in 1956; Kelly and Brown relocated it between 1998 and 2012, and it dates to about 1200. It is the only copper workshop found at a Mississippian site.

Shell carvers engraved cups from Gulf whelk. The earliest known engraved shell cup at Cahokia came from Mound 34 in 1950. Potters tempered clay with burnt shell and incised the shoulders of jars in the style archaeologists call Ramey Incised. Those designs, and the copper and shell imagery, carry meanings the descendant nations hold, so this page draws a jar's profile and not its motifs. Mound 72, a ridge-top mound, held burials; the canon names "the people sacrificed in Mound 72"; they are named here as people and nowhere shown. No record names any maker.

**9. What the name hides**

The label hides the builders' own name, because they left no writing. It hides the nations who descend from them. The Osage say the Cahokia area is ancestral Wahzhazhe land, and a national committee agreed in 2015. The Quapaw, Omaha, Ponca and Kaw share the Dhegiha story; other scholars name other nations too. It hides the Cahokia of the Illinois, whose name the site carries and who did not build it. It hides the people in Mound 72. It hides the "Mound Builder" myth and what it was for. It hides St Louis's own mounds, levelled by 1875, of which Sugarloaf alone survives. And it hides the museums: the Illinois State Museum holds the excavated collections and the ancestors, and NAGPRA now governs both.

**10. Neighbours and counterparts**

Before it came the Hopewell communities of Ohio and Illinois (ISP002). They moved copper, mica and obsidian across the continent five centuries earlier. Beside it, the other Mississippian centres: Moundville in Alabama, Etowah in Georgia, Spiro in Oklahoma, Angel and Kincaid on the Ohio. The copper plates at Spiro and Etowah are often attributed to Cahokian workshops; that is a reading of style, and the page marks it probable. Beside it too, the Oneota and Caddo peoples, and later the Illinois confederacy. Cahokia sits beside Moundville, not above it.

**11. Who speaks for it now**

The descendant nations speak for Cahokia by their own names. The Osage Nation Historic Preservation Office, in Pawhuska, Oklahoma, sets out an ancestral line from the Late Woodland through Cahokia to the Osage. It draws on oral tradition, linguistics and archaeology. In November 2015 the National NAGPRA Review Committee found the Osage culturally affiliated with the Mississippian people. The Quapaw, Omaha, Ponca and Kaw share that story as Dhegiha nations. The canon also names the Muscogee, Chickasaw and Choctaw as descendants of Mississippian peoples. The State of Illinois manages the site through its Department of Natural Resources and says it consults affiliated communities. The Illinois State Museum holds the collections. NAGPRA, since 1990, and its 2024 rule set the terms for what may be held and shown.

Cultural note: the Osage Nation (Wahzhazhe) describes Cahokia as ancestral homeland and a spiritual place. Other nations hold claims we have not read. We show no burial, no object from a grave, and no copper, shell or flint-clay image, because their meanings belong to the nations. We take the Osage's words from the Osage Nation's own pages and claim no permission from them or from any other nation. Ask us to change or remove this item.

**12. What is argued**

- *Was Cahokia a state, a chiefdom, or neither?* Milner (1998) reads a "complex chiefdom" with "only a few thousand inhabitants". It controlled territory "only within its immediate vicinity"; its remains are "essentially similar" to other chiefdoms except in earth moved. Pauketat reads a "Big Bang" around 1050 and, by 2004, "a city" engaged in "state making"; by 2007 he calls the chiefdom "an archaeological delusion". Holt (2009) offers a third reading, a "theater state" whose power "lies more in its ceremonies than its armies". Byers (2006) reads a heterarchy of clans and cults. None is chosen here.
- *How many people lived there?* The site and UNESCO say 10,000 to 20,000 at the peak. Milner says a few thousand. The Osage account says up to 25,000 for the wider area. The numbers rest on house counts and assumptions, and the page gives the range.
- *What ended it?* The site lists resource depletion, political challenge, conflict (the stockade), climate and disease, and says "probably a combination". Osborn (1998) raises catastrophic flooding. The Osage read the end as a move west, not an ending of a people; both readings are on record.
- *Who descends from the builders?* The Osage Nation's office argues an ancestral line and a federal committee accepted it in 2015. Earlier scholars disputed Dhegiha origins; Vehik and Henning (1993) reassessed them and let "the Dhegiha tribes speak for themselves". Other nations' claims were not opened this pass. The page reports the Osage's case in their own words and does not rule on anyone else's.

**13. Afterlives**

The Osage Nation reacquired Sugarloaf Mound in St Louis in stages from 2008. It completed the purchase on 22 September 2025. It plans an interpretive centre there; that is dated to the nation's announcement. Its heritage office takes Osage people to the mounds every year, by the nation's newspaper's account. Remembered: the 1982 listing; the 2015 ruling; the halls that closed in 2024 after the new NAGPRA rule, told in F1.24. Sold: Cahokia on tourist goods and in video games, where Wikidata notes it as a city-state in *Civilization V*. The site's own line says it was "larger than London at that time". We report that and do not endorse it.
