## Civilisation page: the Classic Maya, as commonly named

A painter signed a drinking cup "its'aat Aj Maxam" at Naranjo about 780–810; the Maya are a living people of more than twenty language communities, and the label "Classic" is a period historians drew.

**3. A place to stand**

A cylinder cup, 23.5 cm high, in the Art Institute of Chicago. A painter drew water lilies on it in black slip and wrote a line of glyphs along the rim. The line names him: "its'aat Aj Maxam", which the museum glosses "wise person; he from Maxam". It names his parents too, a king of Naranjo and a lady of Yaxha. The museum dates the cup 780–810 and places it "vicinity of Naranjo, Petén, Guatemala". Where it was dug is not recorded: the museum's record begins with a couple in Illinois who owned it by 1969. Cups of this kind were mostly placed in tombs. That is why this page shows no picture of it.

**4. The name, and who gave it**

"Maya" is a name the communities use. The Academia de las Lenguas Mayas de Guatemala was created by Decreto 65-90 of 18 October 1990. It writes of "el Pueblo Maya" and lists 22 Maya linguistic communities, each with its own name and elected board. In Guatemala the K'iche', Kaqchikel, Q'eqchi', Mam and others are Maya; in Mexico the Yucatec and Tzotzil are too. The Classic kingdoms left no collective name on record. Each wrote its own emblem: Mutul for Tikal, Oxwitik for Copán. Spanish colonial writers, then Stephens and Catherwood's book of 1841, then the Mayanists made "the Maya" a unit of study, as the canon lists them. "Classic" is a Mesoamericanist's period term, and PeriodO carries one handbook's bounds. "Mayan" belongs to the languages; UNESCO's listings use it for the civilisation, and we quote that as theirs.

**5. Span and phases**

The Classic is a period term, so its bounds belong to whoever defines it. PeriodO's definition from one handbook runs the Early Classic c. 250–600 and the Late Classic c. 600–900. The canon's phases end the Late Classic at 800 and add a Terminal Classic to 1000. UNESCO dates Tikal's occupation from the 6th century BCE to the 10th century CE. Its dated monuments run from Stela 29 in 292 to Stela 11 in 869. Copán's dynasty runs 426 to 822 in the canon. The canon's CIV018 runs c. 1000 BCE to 1697 and says the Maya continue. This page keeps the Classic as c. 250–900 and says whose each bound is. The Long Count gives the monuments their dates. The Tikal lintel's radiocarbon range of 658–696 is the check on the correlation (F1.30).

**6. Extent**

The map draws a soft-edged area for 600 to 800 CE from the site list. Its caption reads "sites attested; borders inferred". Tikal, Calakmul, Naranjo and El Perú-Waka' attest the central Petén; Caracol attests Belize. Palenque, Yaxchilan and Piedras Negras attest the Usumacinta. Copán attests the south-east edge, where UNESCO's listing names non-Maya neighbours to the east. The northern Yucatán is drawn with a thin band, because its cities grew as the southern ones emptied. No kingdom's border is attested by a text read this pass; the emblem glyphs name courts, not lines. The morph redraws the polygon by century; after 900 the attested dated monuments stop in the south.

**7. Its making**

Sculptors cut limestone and tuff into stelae and lintels and wrote on them. At Copán they carved the Hieroglyphic Stairway, which UNESCO calls "the longest inscribed text in the Maya region". The Met's record of a Puuc column reads two hands in one monument from the way the feathers are incised. Painters slipped cylinder cups and wrote along the rim. A few signed: Aj Maxam of Naranjo, whose cup the Art Institute holds; the "Metropolitan Painter" is a modern name for an unsigned hand. Scribes wrote on bark paper; four pre-conquest books survive. Jade workers sawed and drilled Motagua jadeite into masks and celts. Knappers pressed prismatic blades from obsidian cores that moved along the network the canon maps. Weavers worked cotton on the backstrap loom, as Maya women do today. Potters formed the cups the painters signed; their names are not recorded. Who organised the work is argued (claim 13).

**9. What the name hides**

The label hides twenty-two language communities in Guatemala alone, and the Maya of Mexico and Belize. It hides the potters who formed the cups a few painters signed. It hides the farmers who fed Tikal, whom UNESCO's listing reaches only as "residential areas". It hides the books: four survive, the rest burned at Maní in 1562. It hides the north, whose cities grew while the south emptied, and the word "collapse" hides that growth. It hides the looting that put a stela front in Cleveland and keeps Tikal's listing worried. And it hides the century: "Classic" borrows a European ranking word for a period of dated monuments.

**10. Neighbours and counterparts**

Beside the Maya, not inside them: Teotihuacan, whose contact with Tikal the monuments record. Monte Albán and the Zapotec. The Gulf lowlands, from which Teotihuacan drew cotton. The non-Maya peoples east of Copán, named in UNESCO's listing. Later, Tula and the Mexica, who told the Teotihuacan story in Nahuatl. Across the sea, nothing is attested. None is ranked.

**11. Who speaks for it now**

The Pueblo Maya speak for themselves. The Academia de las Lenguas Mayas de Guatemala is "la máxima entidad rectora" for the Maya languages, in its own words. Its 22 community boards are elected every four years. Its site prints the law that made it and says the recognition "no es una dádiva de algún gobierno" but the work of a people. The states speak next. Guatemala's Decreto 26-97 gives its Dirección General del Patrimonio Cultural y Natural the supervision of any temporary export. Mexico's INAH holds Palenque and Calakmul with CONANP and bars export under art. 16 of the 1972 law. Tikal's listing names looting as "critical" and says enforcement must grow. The Art Institute of Chicago, the Met and Cleveland hold painted cups and a stela front; the chain of each is on its record.

Cultural note: the Maya are a living people. The Academia de las Lenguas Mayas de Guatemala publishes how its 22 communities name themselves. We show no painted cup, stela, codex or tomb. We do not interpret the glyphs or the 260-day count beyond what the museums and the Academia print. The links go to the Academia and to the holders' own records. Ask us to change or remove this item.

**12. What is argued**

- *What happened after about 800?* UNESCO's Calakmul listing writes of "the dramatic population decline" and "the causes of its collapse". Its Tikal listing has "the decline and eventual collapse of the urban centre around 900 AD". The canon's PRD181 answers that northern growth is hidden by "collapse". CIV018 names "the 'collapse' narrative that erases continuity", and the Academia's own text counts the Pueblo Maya "A 500 años de la invasión". The drought studies (Kennett et al. 2012) were not opened. Both readings are on record.
- *Who organised the painting?* One reading: court workshops. Aj Maxam was a king's son, and the Met's cup carries a royal title. The other: painters moved between courts, and the Met records one vase as "perhaps created in the same workshop" as another without saying where. The potters who formed the cups appear in neither reading.
- *Is "Classic" a period or a rank?* PeriodO carries it as a dated period from one handbook. The canon and this page read the word as borrowed from a European ranking and keep it only as the field's term. We do not choose.
- *How did the Cleveland stela leave Guatemala?* Cleveland's record begins in 1967 with a New York collector. Trafficking Culture calls it "a different looted stela from El Perú-Waka'", and the reader pass marked it LINK-OUT. The law in force in the 1960s was not read.

**13. Afterlives**

Maya women weave huipiles on the backstrap loom in Guatemala and Chiapas. The canon notes a weavers' movement for collective rights. No page of theirs was opened this pass, so the practice is dated here to the canon's reading of 2026. The Academia's annual report for 2024 counts 27 linguistic studies across the 22 communities. Guatemala's law of 2024 (Decreto 03-2024) declares a National Day of Indigenous Languages, as the Academia reports. The 260-day count, the Cholq'ij, is kept by Ajq'ijab', and the F1.30 tool shows a day's name only (AR-106). Remembered: the listings of 1979, 1980, 1987 and 2002. Sold: "Maya" on resorts, tequila and a calendar craze in 2012; we report that and do not endorse it.
