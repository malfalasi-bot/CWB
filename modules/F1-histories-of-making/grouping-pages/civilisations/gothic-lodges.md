## Civilisation page: the Gothic building lodges of Europe, as commonly named

In the spring of 1459 masters of the stone lodges met at Regensburg and wrote a common order; the masons who rebuilt Chartres after 1194 are not named in any record, and the Strasbourg workshop still cuts stone by hand.

**3. A place to stand**

A sheet of parchment, 232 by 152 millimetres, in the Bibliothèque nationale de France. It is one of 33 leaves bound as a portfolio in leather, with drawings in lead and ink and notes in Picard French. The BnF's catalogue says more than 13 leaves are missing. On one opening the draughtsman drew the plan and elevation of a tower at Laon. Of a window at Reims he wrote that he drew it "because I liked it best". He greets his reader by name: "Wilars dehonnecourt". He says he has been in many lands and as far as Hungary. What his trade was, the record does not settle (claim 11). The leaves date, by the latest edition, to about 1220–1240.

**4. The name, and who gave it**

"Gothic" came from outside and from contempt. Renaissance writers used it for a German manner, and 19th-century historians turned it into a period. The AAT now files "Gothic (Medieval)" as a style that began in northern France in the mid-12th century. The lodges' own words were plainer. The 1459 order speaks of "masters and fellows" of "our whole common craft" and "the fraternity of Masonry". The German lodges called themselves Bauhütten, and the Strasbourg lodge belonged to the Œuvre Notre-Dame, the Opus Sancte Marie earliest recorded in 1224–28. Who made the lodges a unit of study is a tangled history. Freemasons did much of it. Heldmann's edition of the Strasbourg statutes (1819) and Krause's *Die drei ältesten Kunsturkunden* reach us with "Bro." before the editors' names. Bürger's recent study of the Simrock-Boisserée papers traces the "Bauhütten-Forschungen" of the 19th century. We use the lodges' words before any other, and "Gothic" after them, marked as a later label.

**5. Span and phases**

Every definer gives a different span. The canon runs the style 1140–1520 and the lodges 1200–1731. The Strasbourg Œuvre is attested from 1224–28 and has not stopped. The 1459 order is the earliest common text. It was drawn up at three meetings: Strasbourg from 9 April 1458, Regensburg four weeks after Easter 1459, Speyer on 9 April 1464. The end is the argued part. The Reichstag at Regensburg resolved on 12 August 1671 that Strasbourg's supremacy should end. A Reichsschluss of 16 March 1707 repeated it, and a ratification of 13 May 1727 withdrew the lodge's powers. Bürger cites an imperial order of 1731 to abolish all head lodges. The French inventory says 1721. Keller gives 15 July 1771 for the abolition of the whole organisation. The workshops re-founded in the 19th and 20th centuries carry the practice to 2020. PeriodO holds no Gothic period.

**6. Extent**

The map draws lodges as nodes and the 1459 provinces as soft regions, not a polygon. The caption reads "lodges attested; provinces inferred from the 1459 text". Strasbourg is the Oberhütte, whose master was supreme judge. Cologne, Vienna and Berne, later Zurich, are the head lodges under it, as the Fondation summarises the order. Regensburg and Speyer are meeting places. Prague is attested by its own workshop from 1344. Chartres, Laon, Reims, Cambrai, Vaucelles and Meaux are attested by Villard's drawings, and Hungary by his words. The French compagnonnages are drawn as a separate network. Norway is attested only by the 2020 element. The morph lights the nodes by date and draws the provinces only after 1459.

**7. Its making**

Masters set out plans with compass and straightedge; the geometry, not calculation, carried a vault. The Parlier turned the master's drawing into templates the cutters worked from. Stone cutters dressed ashlar and left their marks; Hültz's mark is still visible on a baldachin high on the Strasbourg spire. Quarry masters ran the Fondation's sandstone quarries. Hut servants carried tools and moved blocks; the winch men worked the cranes. At Prague, Parler's net vaults doubled the diagonal ribs across each bay. At Strasbourg, Hültz finished an openwork spire in 1439 that reached 142 metres. At Chartres, the masons after 1194 raised a nave whose builders the cathedral's own site cannot name. Villard drew what he saw and what he admired, in Picard, on parchment he later cut, folded and sewed into a book. The Strasbourg workshop today still cuts "exclusively" by hand, as the French inventory records it. Who organised the work at Strasbourg is documented: the Werkmeister under the city's magistrate from the 1280s. Elsewhere it is argued (claim 13).

**9. What the name hides**

"Gothic" hides the lodges' own words, and "the lodges" hide the people outside them. Chartres hides its masons entirely: "we do not know the names of the architects", its site says. The 1459 order names a supreme judge and says nothing of the quarry workers, hut servants and winch men who moved the stone. Women appear only as wives: Husa, Erwin's wife, died in 1316 and shares his epitaph. The canon names the Islamic pointed arch as a precedent the style's name hides. The record itself is hidden twice. The brotherhood kept its statutes "under triple enclosure" in 1817, and the Freemasons then edited and claimed them. And the end is hidden under five dates.

**10. Neighbours and counterparts**

Beside the lodges, not inside them: the compagnonnages of France, inscribed by UNESCO in 2010. Their journeymen still travel a Tour de France of workshops and finish with a masterwork. The Italian cathedral workshops, Pisa's attested from the 12th century. The English masons' lodges, not opened this pass. The Islamic builders of the pointed arch. The Bauhütten today number workshops in five countries; the Strasbourg atelier is "the only workshop attached to a cathedral still existing in France". None is ranked.

**11. Who speaks for it now**

The workshops speak for themselves. The Fondation de l'Œuvre Notre-Dame publishes its history, its masters and its plans. Its administrator is the mayor of Strasbourg, and its atelier cuts by hand. The Dombauhütte Köln and the other Bauhütten of Germany, Austria, Switzerland and Norway filed the 2020 nomination with France. UNESCO's text calls their network "an example to be adapted and implemented in other contexts worldwide". The Compagnons du Devoir speak for the journeymen. The states hold the buildings. France lists Chartres as a Historic Monument since 1862 and manages it with the diocese, the Centre des monuments nationaux and the city. The Czech state owns St Vitus within Prague Castle. The BnF holds Villard. Export of the drawings is governed by French and German law this pass did not verify.

Cultural note: the cathedral workshops of five countries are living institutions with their own customs. The 2020 nomination is their own account of them. We show no photograph of a living mason and do not describe the lodges' customs beyond what UNESCO and the Fondation print. The links go to the Fondation's site and the UNESCO element page. **12. What is argued**

- *Is the surviving order the text of 1459?* Heldmann's edition (1819) and the Krause text present it as the statutes of 1459, revised in 1563. Bürger argues the document Heldmann saw was "actually that of 1464", the Speyer revision, and that it was destroyed in 1870. The Regensburg historian reads the order as the work of three meetings, 1458 to 1464. Both are on record.
- *When did Strasbourg's supremacy end?* Bürger: the Reichstag's decisions of 1671 and 1707, ratified in 1727. He adds an imperial order of 1731 abolishing all head lodges. The French inventory: "arrêté impérial en 1721". Keller: 15 July 1771. The Fondation: "au début du XVIIIe siècle". The canon's MKR061 gives 1731. We list all five and date the end as argued.
- *Who organised the work where no city records survive?* At Strasbourg the record is full. The magistrate appointed the curators from the 1280s, and the Werkmeister served the city. At Chartres the cathedral's site reads "a collective process" of "probably several architects". John James's "contractors" model assigns campaigns to teams of his own naming; Aubert (1958) reads one design carried out in 25 years. We do not choose.
- *What was Villard?* The BnF calls him "constructeur, géomètre, inventeur, voyageur". Barnes (1981, 2009) argued from his drawing technique that he was trained as a metalworker, not an architect. The TMR review notes the subjects fit no single trade. The leaves "do not offer the detailed instructions" of a craft manual.

**13. Afterlives**

The Bauhütten's selection for UNESCO's Register in 2020 is the latest step. The element page says workshops "created or re-established in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries" now preserve the techniques. The Strasbourg atelier is on France's national inventory of intangible heritage, cutting by hand. About thirty medieval plans sit in the museum's reading room since 2015. The compagnons' Tour de France continues, inscribed in 2010. Freemasonry took the lodges' name, statutes and signs into its own origin story. Keller's history, written for a Masonic readership, says the brotherhood survived with about 100 brothers "to this day". That claim is Keller's. Remembered: Chartres's listing of 1979; Prague's consecration of 1929. Sold: "Gothic" on fonts, novels and a music scene that have nothing to do with lodges; we report that and do not endorse it. Gothic Revival (STY027) is the style's own afterlife, taught in F1.15.
