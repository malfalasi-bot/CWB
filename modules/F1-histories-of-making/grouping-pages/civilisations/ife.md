**3. A place to stand**

A house plot at Wunmonije Compound, Ilé-Ifẹ̀, a short walk from the Ooni's palace. In January 1938 workmen digging foundations struck metal. They had found cast heads. The British Museum's record of the find counts five in copper, twelve in brass and the top half of a figure. Fifteen of the heads are life-size and pierced round the neck; two are smaller and wear crowns. Most went to the Ooni, Sir Adesoji Aderemi, and later to the museum built at Ife. Fourteen of them and the half figure travelled to London for cleaning in 1947 and came back. One head left the country within a year. The plot itself is not a monument: the people living there in 1938 are not named in any record this pass found.

**4. The name, and who gave it**

The city's own name is Ilé-Ifẹ̀, and the Yorùbá have never stopped using it. "Ife" is its short form in English. "Ife art" is the museums' label, used since the heads went on show in London in 1947. The Getty AAT files "Ife" as a style with "Classical" and "Postclassical" phases. Neither phase name is a Yorùbá term. The oldest outside label was worse. Leo Frobenius came to Ife in November 1910 and read the sculpture as a relic of Atlantis: "Atlantic Africa's Poseidon", he called the Olokun head. By January 1911 the *New York Times* was reporting his lost continent. His reading rested on a racial claim that the people around him could not have made such work. Apter (2013) traces the argument through Frobenius's own book; Eyo and later Nigerian archaeologists answered it. We use the Yorùbá name before any other, "Ife" after it, and the museum phases only as theirs.

**5. Span and phases**

Every definer gives a different span, and the facts block lists each with its owner. The British Museum dates its head "probably" to the 14th or early 15th century. The Met's essay says "between ca. 1200 and 1500". The Ooni's site prints 1200–1400 CE; the Getty TGN, the 12th and 13th centuries. Thermoluminescence on the clay cores of the Ita Yemoo figures gave the 13th to 15th centuries. Radiocarbon at Igbo Olokun puts the glass-bead industry in the 11th to 15th centuries. Two dated layers fall at 1058–1264 and 1304–1423 cal CE. Garlake's shrine sites with potsherd pavements fall in the 12th to 14th centuries. The canon's 1000–1500 holds all of these. "Iron Age", which the TGN uses, is a European sequence and carries that region.

**6. Extent**

The map draws one city, not a polygon. Ilé-Ifẹ̀ sits at 7.48 N 4.56 E in today's Osun State. Its caption reads "sites attested; the city's medieval edge inferred". Wunmonije Compound and Ita Yemoo are attested near the centre. Igbo Olokun lies about two kilometres out by the outermost earthen wall. By the 1980s building had cut the grove to a plot of 21 by 48 metres, now enclosed by the NCMM. Obalara's Land and Woye Asiri attest shrines with pavements. The walls are the problem. A 2015 re-excavation at Ita Yemoo dated the ditch there to 1802–1938 cal CE. That embankment is 19th-century work, perhaps Ooni Abeweila's of about 1848. The medieval edge is therefore argued (claim 8), and the morph draws it with a wide soft band.

**7. Its making**

Casters built a core and modelled wax over it. They cast heads in copper or in leaded zinc-brass by the lost-wax method. The British Museum's analysis of its head reads "heavily leaded zinc-brass". The 2010 examination of the Olokun head found it "a hollow cire perdue or lost wax casting". The heads are near life-size, incised with fine vertical lines on the face, and pierced at the neck and hairline. What the holes held, and what the heads stood on, is not recorded. Modellers made terracotta heads in the same manner of face. Bead makers at Igbo Olokun melted their own glass from local materials, a high-lime high-alumina glass unlike Mediterranean or Egyptian glass. They drew it into canes, cut the canes and reheated the beads; crucibles, cullet and beads at every stage lie in the pits. Pavement layers set potsherds and quartz on edge in mortar across courtyards and shrine floors. No record names any of these makers. Who organised the work is argued (claim 13).

**9. What the name hides**

"Ife art" hides the casters, whose names are not recorded. It hides the bead makers of Igbo Olokun, whose industry was argued out of existence for decades. The beads were read as imports until the crucibles were dated. It hides the other Yorùbá kingdoms and the Edo court at Benin, whose oral history is heard here only through a museum. It hides the Yorùbá of Ilé-Ifẹ̀ now, for whom the groves are shrines and not sites. It hides the owners of the house plot of 1938. And it hides forty years in which a European read the heads as Greek. The museums dropped Atlantis by 1948; the label "classical", with its Greek echo, stayed.

**10. Neighbours and counterparts**

Beside Ife, not inside it: the Edo kingdom of Benin. One court oral history there says a caster came from Ife in the 13th century (claim 14). Igbo-Ukwu, where cast copper alloys are earlier and in another style. Nok, far to the north and a thousand years earlier, in terracotta. Esie, with its soapstone figures. The Yorùbá kingdoms of Ọ̀yọ́, Ìjẹ̀bú and Ekiti, whose own carvers and casters the units teach through Olowe of Ise. Osun-Osogbo, the grove UNESCO lists. None is ranked.

**11. Who speaks for it now**

The Yorùbá speak for Ilé-Ifẹ̀. The Ooni's site states the claim in its own words: the Ooni is "spiritual and cultural custodian of the Yoruba race worldwide". The site prints the city's history and the festival calendar. The Nigerian state speaks through the NCMM, created by Decree 77 of 1979. It runs 53 museums, holds the Wunmonije heads at the National Museum, Ife, and fences what is left of Igbo Olokun. Section 25 of its Act makes export without a permit an offence. The British Museum holds one Wunmonije head and the casts of 1947. Berlin holds terracottas Frobenius took in 1910. Which body a return request would go to, the palace or the Commission, is a question the Benin case has already raised (F1.24).

Cultural note: the Yorùbá of Ilé-Ifẹ̀ hold the groves, shrines and palace these objects came from. The Ooni's own site is the place to read about them. We show no image of any Ife head, figure or shrine, and do not say what the heads were for. The link goes to the Ooni's site and to the NCMM. Ask us to change or remove this item.

**12. What is argued**

- *Is the returned Olokun head the one of 1910?* Fagg and Underwood (1949) examined it and condemned it as a sand-cast copy. The story that Frobenius switched a fake is told from that. Craddock and colleagues (2013) examined it again in 2010. It is a lost-wax casting like the other heads, and "the balance of evidence suggests" it is the 1910 head. The head went missing between those examinations and its present whereabouts are not recorded in the sources read. We do not choose.
- *How old is the city's wall?* Ozanne (1969) and Willett read the walls as medieval. The 2015 re-excavation dated the Ita Yemoo ditch to 1802–1938 cal CE and read it as 19th-century work. The medieval extent is open.
- *Who organised the casting?* One reading infers a palace workshop from the crowns and the find beside the palace. The other is the Museums Journal's: "little is known about how the sculptures... were used, and who would have carved them". The FT adds that a 1940s idea of one workshop or one hand "still hasn't been ruled out". Both are on record.
- *Did Ife teach Benin to cast?* The Met reports one Benin court oral history: a caster from Ife, Ahammangiwa or Igueghae. The canon marks the account "one among several"; the F1.6 and F1.29 specs keep "foreign artisan" as the Met's phrase. The other reading rests on the metal. The Ife heads are leaded zinc-brass and copper, and the composition studies (Werner and Willett 1975, not opened) treat Ife and Benin as separate traditions.

**13. Afterlives**

The Ooni's site announces the Olojo festival at Ilé-Ifẹ̀ for 26 September to 5 October 2026. Ifa divination, a Yorùbá practice centred on the òrìṣà Orunmila, is on UNESCO's Representative List since 2008. The Ooni's site calls the heads "masterworks that challenged European assumptions". The British Museum's record calls them "one of the highest achievements of African art". Both are quoted as theirs. The 2010 exhibition toured Santander, London and New York with 109 loans from the NCMM. Remembered: the independence anniversary the exhibition marked. Sold: Ife heads on Nigerian stamps and in replica; the British Museum shop once sold a replica (FT), which we report and do not endorse.
