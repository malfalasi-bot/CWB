**3. A place to stand**

A covered drain runs beside a street at Mohenjo-daro, in Larkana district, Sindh. Bricklayers lined it in fired brick. UNESCO's listing records streets "intersecting each other at right angles", a citadel to the west, a lower town to the east. The site covers 240 hectares; about a third has been dug since 1922. Nobody has found a palace or a royal tomb there. That is an absence, not a proof of equality. Salt rising with the water table has eaten the exposed brick since the 1920s. In August 2022, 779.5 mm of rain fell, twice a normal year, and walls fell. The Government of Sindh and two UNESCO missions have been consolidating them since.

**4. The name, and who gave it**

The label came from outside, in English, in 1924. John Marshall, Director-General of Archaeology in India, wrote in the *Illustrated London News* of 20 September 1924. His headline called it a "long-forgotten civilisation"; his text named "the civilisation of the Indus Valley". Behind him lay Daya Ram Sahni's dig at Harappa, begun in January 1921, and Rakhal Das Banerji's at Mohenjo-daro, begun in 1922.

"Harappan" followed the usual rule of naming a culture after its type site. In 1937 Ernest Mackay wrote that it "should be renamed the 'Harappa culture'", and Indian archaeology mostly uses "Harappan" today. Jim Shaffer in 1992 recast the whole as an "Indus Tradition" of eras. A fourth name, "Indus–Sarasvati civilisation", was proposed by S. P. Gupta from 1989 and is politically contested; both readings are in §12. None of these is a name the makers used.

**5. Span and phases**

Every definer gives a different span, and the facts block lists each with its owner. The canon runs c. 3300–1300 BCE; Wikidata 3000–1800; UNESCO 2500–1500 for Mohenjo-daro and 3000–1500 for Dholavira. PeriodO holds one definition, "Harappan (ca. 2200–1900 BC)", from the Cuneiform Digital Library's list, which is Mesopotamia's narrow use. Harappa's excavators give five phases from the Ravi (c. 3300–2800 BCE) to the Late Harappan (c. 1800–1300 BCE). Specialists agree most on the middle: cities, seals and weights, about 2600–1900 BCE. "Bronze Age" is a European sequence, and the label carries that region.

**6. Extent**

The map draws a soft-edged area for 2600 to 1900 BCE from the site list. Its caption reads "sites attested; borders inferred". Mohenjo-daro, Harappa and Chanhu-daro attest the Indus plain. Rakhigarhi attests the Ghaggar–Hakra plain to the east, where the edge is argued. Dholavira and Lothal attest Kachchh and Saurashtra. The western edge comes from a bowl: the Met reads Damb Sadaat, in the Balochistan hills, as "outside the area controlled by the cities". One point lies far outside: Shortugai on the Oxus, where a French team dug Indus pottery, seals and a lapis workshop in 1976–79. It is drawn as a site, not an extent. UNESCO counts more than 1,000 Harappan sites. The morph redraws the polygon by phase; after 1900 BCE the attested sites thin in Sindh and thicken in the east.

**7. Its making**

Seal cutters carved square seals in steatite, a soft stone, and then fired them hard. The Met describes 49.40.1 as "burnt steatite". Cleveland reads its seal 1964.104 as "probably used to identify merchandise or property". At Harappa in 2000–01 excavators found a courtyard workshop that made steatite tablets, faience tablets and beads together. No kiln stood in it; the makers fired small objects inside clay canisters, and a replica firing reached 935 °C. Indus faience is denser and stronger than Egyptian or Mesopotamian faience of the same centuries.

Weight makers cut cubes of chert in a doubling series: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32. The smallest at Harappa weighs 0.856 g; the commonest, about 13.7 g, is the sixteenth step. Above that the series turns decimal. Bead drillers bored long carnelian beads with a constricted cylindrical drill of a rock Kenoyer and Vidale named "ernestite". Kenoyer estimates two to ten hours to drill one centimetre of agate. Mackay's team found beads at every stage at Chanhu-daro in 1935–36. In 2009 Good, Kenoyer and Meadow found wild silk inside microbeads from that dig and ornaments from Harappa, c. 2450–2000 BCE. Cotton cloth survived on a silver vase at Mohenjo-daro, analysed for Marshall's 1931 report. Shell cutters sawed conch into bangles. No record names any of these makers.

**9. What the name hides**

The label hides its makers' name, because the signs they cut are unread. It hides whether the signs are writing at all (§12). It hides the villages: hill potters whom the cities did not rule made the Damb Sadaat bowl. It hides the neighbours, Dilmun, Magan, the Helmand and the Oxus. It hides the people on the sites now: Rakhigarhi's farmers, whose fields were declared protected in 2024, and Harappa town on three mounds. It hides what happened after about 1900 BCE, which Giosan calls "collapse" and Shaffer "Localisation". And it hides the word's own history: "civilisation" was coined in French in 1757 to rank peoples (F1.3).

**10. Neighbours and counterparts**

Before it, Mehrgarh in Balochistan (ARC137), farmed from about 7000 BCE. Beside it, the Helmand towns (CIV070), the Oxus towns (CIV089) and the hill villages. Across the sea, Dilmun on Bahrain (CIV060), Magan on the Oman peninsula (CIV061) and Mesopotamia. An inscription attributed to Sargon of Akkad says "Ships from Meluhha, ships from Magan, (and) ships from Dilmun tie up alongside the quay of Agade". A carnelian bead catalogued as Indus work was excavated at Nippur in 1957–58 (Met 59.41.42). After it, the Cemetery H and Jhukar wares, and the Vedic period (PRD143), whose link to the Indus is argued and politically charged. No counterpart is ranked; Dilmun sits beside the Indus, not under it.

**11. Who speaks for it now**

No descendant community speaks for the Indus cities in any statement this pass found. Two states and their heritage bodies do. The Archaeological Survey of India protects Dholavira, Lothal and Rakhigarhi under its Act of 1958. The Government of Sindh's Directorate General of Antiquities has managed Mohenjo-daro since the 18th Amendment moved culture to the provinces. Harappa sits on Pakistan's Tentative List, submitted on 30 January 2004. Pakistan's Antiquities Act, 1975, section 26, bars export without a licence. India's Antiquities and Art Treasures Act, 1972, section 3, reserves export to the Central Government. The MFA Boston holds the Chanhu-daro finds under the 1935–36 agreement. Marshall himself took the Met's four seals out of British India and sold them to the Met in 1949.

Cultural note: bead-making families at Khambhat, Gujarat, work agate and carnelian today. Siddi and Bhil communities mine and supply their stone. We name them as their own sources do and show no photograph of a living maker. We do not say whether any of them count the Indus makers as ancestors; the sources that say so are not theirs. Ask us to change or remove this item.

**12. What is argued**

- *Are the signs writing?* Farmer, Sproat and Witzel (2004) argue not. The inscriptions are brief, they say, and "were not even evolving in linguistic directions". Rao and colleagues (2009) find their conditional entropy "closer to those of natural languages". Both cite the Dholavira board, ten signs each about 37 cm high. We do not choose.
- *What happened after about 1900 BCE?* Giosan and colleagues (2012) write of "the collapse of the Bronze Age Harappan". They tie settlement shrinkage and eastward movement to weakening monsoon rivers. Petrie (2019) stresses diversity and resilience, and Shaffer's scheme names the phase "Localisation". Both are on record.
- *Who ran the cities?* One reading infers a state from standard weights and bricks. The other is Petrie's: "no one elite group dominated any of the Indus cities". Kenoyer, Vidale and Bhan add that Khambhat's standard beads come from merchant families. Clear signs of state control, they note, are absent "in the case of the Harappan civilization".
- *Was Lothal's basin a dock?* S. R. Rao argued a dockyard. Shah (1959–60) and Leshnik (1968) argued an irrigation tank: the inlet is shallow, the rivers not navigable. Nigam (1988) reported foraminifera and salt as marine evidence; Leshnik's side answers that floods could leave them.
- *Is "Indus–Sarasvati" a name or a programme?* S. P. Gupta (1989) and Danino read the Ghaggar–Hakra as the Rigveda's Sarasvati. It belongs in the name, they argue. Chadha (2011) traces the Saraswati Heritage Project of 2002–04 to colonial Indology and postcolonial science as well as Hindutva. Kumar (2025) reads the claim as political, about who owns the nation. Giosan (2012) and Tripathi (2004) find only monsoon-fed rivers there. Danino himself writes that the term "did not really catch on". The page uses "Harappan" and reports the dispute.

**13. Afterlives**

Bead makers at Khambhat still heat, chip, saw, grind, drill and polish agate. Kenoyer, Vidale and Bhan recorded the workshops in 1989. Merchant families "monitor and control all stages"; state contractors hire Bhil labourers to mine at Ratanpura; men, women and children dig. In 2023 ThePrint profiled Anwar Husain Shaikh, a fifth-generation maker who hand-drills Harappan-style beads. It said the Siddi supply the stone. Its claim that today's makers descend from Harappan workers comes from S. R. Rao's report and is not evidenced; what continues is stone and technique. A 2008 Geographical Indication, "Agates of Cambay", rests on a blog not confirmed at the registry. Remembered: the listings of 1980 and 2021, the centenary pieces of September 2024. Sold: the one-horned seal on museum goods and brands; we report that and do not endorse it.
