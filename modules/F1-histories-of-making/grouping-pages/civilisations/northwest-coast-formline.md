## Civilisation page: Northwest Coast formline, as commonly named

"Formline" is a word Bill Holm invented in 1965 for a design system whose makers, Haida, Lingít, Ts'msyen and others, had no word for "art" and still carve, paint and weave it.

**3. A place to stand**

A cedar plank, kerfed three times, steamed and bent into four sides. One open corner is sewn or pegged. That is a bentwood box, and the Lingít, Haida and Ts'msyen made hundreds that Bill Holm measured in the 1950s. He recorded 392 specimens on 400 cards. From their painted sides he drew his "rules". This page shows no box. The boxes in museums came from families who were not asked, and the designs on them belong to clans and houses. The opening link goes to the U'mista Cultural Centre, which holds what came home.

**4. The name, and who gave it**

"Formline" came from outside, in 1965. Bill Holm, a painter and later a curator at the Burke Museum, published *Northwest Coast Indian Art: An Analysis of Form*. The University of Washington Press issued it. In his 2015 preface he writes: "My goal in inventing terminology was always to try for really descriptive words." He would now add "Northern" to the title. Readers, he says, took a Tlingit, Haida and Tsimshian analysis as "pan-coastal". One of his terms, "salmon-trout's head", he "lifted bodily" from a list of Tlingit weavers' terms that George Emmons recorded. Artists today, he notes, say "fine line" and "trigon" for his "tertiary line" and "T-shaped" relief. Sealaska Heritage Institute uses "formline design" and adds that the Tlingit, Haida and Tsimshian "had no term for 'art'". The nations' names for themselves come first on this page; Holm's word is kept as his, and used because the nations' institutions use it.

**5. Span and phases**

The canon gives STY076 a start of 1700 and marks it "much older; living tradition". Sealaska Heritage Institute says the oldest object found with formline design is 4,000 years old. The page keeps that as SHI's statement and did not open the object record. Holm's sample is nineteenth-century. The ban gives the tradition a documented interruption. The amendment of 19 April 1884 took effect on 1 January 1885; the 1951 revision of the Indian Act dropped the section. Chief Mungo Martin held what U'mista calls "the first public potlatch" after the ban, in Victoria in 1952. The practice continues, dated to its sources. SHI ran formline and Naaxein classes in 2025. It says Chilkat weaving, spruce-root weaving and canoe carving are "considered endangered". No PeriodO definition was found.

**6. Extent**

The map draws no polygon and no border. It names each nation's territory as the nation names it. Haida Gwaii for the Haida, with the Kaigani Haida in Alaska. The coast of south-east Alaska for the Lingít. The Skeena and Nass rivers and the coast between for the Ts'msyen. The inlets and islands of northern Vancouver Island, and the mainland opposite, for the Kwakwaka'wakw. The west coast of Vancouver Island for the Nuu-chah-nulth. Holm's "northern" limit is drawn as a note on the map, not a line. Where Aboriginal title has been recognised or is in negotiation, the page says so only by link to the nation. The morph does not run; the nations have not moved.

**7. Its making**

Content note: this section names the arrests of 1922 and the seizure of masks and regalia. The people arrested are people; the objects are the nations' property. You can skip to §8.

Carvers split planks and poles from western red cedar with adzes, in SHI's own account. They bent boxes by kerfing and steaming a single plank. Weavers twined mountain-goat wool and cedar bark into Chilkat and Ravenstail robes on a hanging warp, without a loom beam. One robe "can take years to complete". Painters and carvers laid the design system over boxes, house fronts, poles, masks and spoons. Which crest a maker may carve belongs to a clan or house, and the nations' institutions, not this page, say what those rights are.

In the record, makers are mostly unnamed until the late nineteenth century. Then names appear. Da.axiigang, Charles Edenshaw, carved argillite, engraved silver and painted spruce-root hats. His wife Kwii.aang, Isabella Edenshaw, wove the hats; museums long credited him alone (PER120). Hilamas, Willie Seaweed, kept carving through the ban. Jennie Thlunaut wove some fifty Chilkat robes and taught weavers in the 1980s (PER126). In 1921 ʼNa̱mǥis chief Dan Cranmer gave a potlatch at ʼMimkwa̱mlis. In early 1922 the Indian agent William Halliday and Sergeant Donald Angerman arrested 45 people; about half went to prison for two to three months. The rest kept out of prison by giving up their regalia. About 600 objects were taken, shown in the Anglican parish hall at Alert Bay, and sent to Ottawa and Toronto. Halliday sold 33 to George Heye of New York.

**9. What the name hides**

"Formline" hides that it is one man's word for several nations' systems. He himself would now call it "northern". It hides the rights: who may carve which crest is a clan's or house's to say, and the nations' institutions say so. It hides the women: Isabella Edenshaw's hats under her husband's name, and the weavers whose terms Emmons wrote down and Holm reused. It hides George Hunt, who wrote much of what was published as Boas's (PER127). It hides the ban, the 45 arrests of 1922, the parish hall where the regalia was shown for admission, and the sale to Heye. It hides the ancestors: over 600 returned to Haida Gwaii, and others still in Europe. It hides the central and southern nations, whose designs differ and whose practices the book's ubiquity "marginalized", in its reviewer's words. And it hides the market, which sells "Northwest Coast style" to anyone.

**10. Neighbours and counterparts**

Beside the northern nations, not inside them, are the Heiltsuk, Nuxalk, Haisla and Gitxsan. The Coast Salish peoples to the south have designs of their own. The Makah are relatives of the Nuu-chah-nulth across the strait. The Russian-American Company and the Hudson's Bay Company traded on the coast and are settings, not makers. Across the Pacific, Hawaiʻi's voyaging canoe Hawaiʻiloa was built on two Sitka spruce logs given by Sealaska Corporation (INS151). The Museum of Anthropology at UBC and the Burke Museum hold poles and boxes and have worked with nations on loans and co-management (PLC111). No nation is ranked against another.

**11. Who speaks for it now**

The nations speak for themselves. Their institutions are the sources this page opens before any other. The Haida Gwaii Museum at Kay Llnagaay tells the repatriation story in its own exhibition: fifteen years of requests from 1995, over 600 ancestors returned. The Haida Repatriation Committee works from Skidegate and Old Massett. It writes: "Our ancestors are our relatives and we have a deep connection to them." The U'mista Cultural Centre holds the Potlatch Collection and still seeks "the remaining 24 artifacts". Sealaska Heritage Institute, founded in 1980 after elders passed the "container of wisdom" to Sealaska, runs the classes and the Native Artists Committee. Canada's export act requires a permit for ethnographic objects; the United States' NAGPRA governs returns from its museums. The Burke Museum keeps Holm's archive and the Bill Holm Center.

Cultural note: the Haida, Lingít, Ts'msyen, Kwakwaka'wakw and Nuu-chah-nulth are living nations. The rights in their designs belong to their clans, houses and families. We show no mask, pole, robe, box or copper, and we draw no crest. We link to U'mista, the Haida Gwaii Museum and Sealaska Heritage Institute and take their words from their own pages. **12. What is argued**

- *Is "formline" one system or several?* Holm's book found "rules" that "transcended tribal and linguistic boundaries on the northern coast". He would now add "Northern" to its title. Its 2015 reviewer writes that southern and central practices "have been marginalized in the literature and within the art market". The book's ubiquity is part of the cause, the reviewer says. Both are on record, and the nations' institutions use the word for their own purposes.
- *How old is it?* The canon starts the style at 1700 and calls it "much older". SHI says the oldest object with formline design is 4,000 years old. The page gives both dates with their owners and opened no object record for the older one.
- *Whose terms?* Holm coined most of the vocabulary, by his own account. He borrowed "salmon-trout's head" from Tlingit weavers through Emmons. Artists use "fine line" and "trigon"; Holm keeps "tertiary line" and "T-shaped" as "more descriptive". The page reports the three vocabularies and uses the nations' institutions' words where they publish them.
- *Who wrote the Kwakwaka'wakw ethnography?* The canon records George Hunt as the uncredited co-author of much published under Boas's name (PER127, PER128). This page did not open Boas or Hunt and reports the canon's row.

**13. Afterlives**

The practice is living and dated here to its sources. SHI ran an intermediate formline class called Naaxein in May 2025 and a Ravenstail workshop in July 2025. It is building an arts campus in Juneau. The U'mista Cultural Centre has operated since November 1980 and the Nuyumbalees centre beside it. The Haida Gwaii Museum's repatriation exhibition is open. Remembered: the 1981 listing; the 1952 potlatch; the 2005 End of Mourning. Sold: "Northwest Coast style" on everything from blankets to logos. Holm's book itself has sold more than 100,000 copies by the Burke's count, 130,000 printed by the reviewer's. Whether outsiders may make the designs is argued in British Columbia; the page reports that there is an argument and does not take a side.
