**3. A place to stand**

A silver plate in the Met, 4.6 cm high and 21.9 cm across. A silversmith hammered it into shape and cut lips up from the surface. Into them he set separate pieces of silver to raise the king and the rams in high relief. Then the plate was gilded with an amalgam of mercury and gold and inlaid with niello. The king wears a crown, a fillet, a covered globe and a beaded halter with ribbons flying. The Met says his crown makes him Peroz or Kavad I, so the plate is mid-5th to mid-6th century. It was "said at the time of the acquisition to have been found in Qazvin"; the Met bought it in 1934. The smith's name is not recorded.

**4. The name, and who gave it**

The rulers' own name for the realm survives in stone. Ardashir I's investiture relief at Naqsh-e Rostam calls him king of kings of ērān. His son Shapur I, in the trilingual inscription on the Ka'ba-ye Zardosht of about 262, says he is lord of Ērānšahr and lists its provinces. Iranica reads ērānšahr as the empire and ērān as "of the Iranians". The title "king of kings of Ērān and Anērān", Iran and non-Iran, came with Shapur's conquests. "Sasanian" is the dynasty's name from its claimed ancestor Sāsān, and the AAT prefers "Sassanian"; UNESCO's listing says "Sassanid". None of these is a name a weaver or a smith would have used of their work. We use the rulers' own word for the realm and "Sasanian" for the dynasty, as the Met does.

**5. Span and phases**

The start is one date in every source: 224, when Ardashir I defeated the Parthians. The end is three. Wikidata, the Met, the canon and UNESCO's brief synthesis end the empire in 651, the year Yazdgard III died. UNESCO's short description for the same listing prints 658. The Getty AAT's note has the empire fall "to the Arabs in 631", a date no other source read gives. The reigns beneath are the phases the sources use. Shapur I ruled alone from 12 April 240 to May 270. Under Shapur II, 310–379, the hunting plates began. The Great Grotto is usually ascribed to the late court of Khosrow II, 591–628. PeriodO holds two "Sasanian" definitions, one for eastern Arabia from 300.

**6. Extent**

The map draws the provinces as Shapur I lists them in ŠKZ. Its caption reads "provinces as the king lists them; borders inferred". In the west: Asūrestān, Adiabene, Armenia, Iberia, Albania "up to the Caucasus mountains and the Gates of the Alans". In the centre: Pārs, Media, Khuzestan, Mesene. In the east: Gurgān, Marw, Herat, Abaršahr, Kirmān, Sakastān, Makurān, "the Kushanshahr up to Peshawar", Sogdiana. "On that side of the sea", Mazūnšahr: the Oman peninsula. Iranica notes that Kerdīr's later inscriptions place Armenia and the Caucasus lands in Anērānšahr, so the edge moved with the writer. Ctesiphon, on the Tigris 32 km south of Baghdad, is the capital the Met's record places. Firuzabad and Bishapur in Fars are the earliest cities, in UNESCO's listing. The Gulf is drawn as the Gulf.

**7. Its making**

Silversmiths hammered plates and raised figures with inserted pieces. They chased the detail, gilded with mercury amalgam and inlaid niello; the Met describes the method on 34.33. The Freer says about thirty such royal hunting plates have been found. The kings are known by their crowns, matched to coins. Stucco moulders cast repeating panels of birds, vines and pomegranates for the reception rooms of houses at Ctesiphon. The Met's panel 32.150.20 came from a house at Ma'aridh IV excavated in 1931–32. Carvers cut ayvāns and reliefs into the cliff at Taq-e Bostan. There is an investiture on the open rock and a small ayvān with Shapur II and III named by inscription. The Great Grotto is carved on every surface: an investiture above, an armoured horseman below, hunts on the side walls. Weavers made silks with winged horses in pearl roundels; the Met owns a strip and says the centres of production "remain unknown". Die cutters put each king's own crown on the drachms. Roman captives settled after 260 are credited by tradition with dams and bridges (OCC018). No record names a maker. The silver was a royal product; who organised the silk is argued (claim 13).

**9. What the name hides**

"Sasanian" hides the makers: no silversmith, weaver, moulder or carver is named on any record read. It hides the deportees of 260, people the sources count in tens of thousands and the tradition credits with dams. It hides the subjects who were not Zoroastrian, whom the canon names. It hides where the silks were woven, since "Sasanian" silks in museums are placed in Central Asia and their centres "remain unknown". It hides the Parthians before, told as defeat, and the conquest after, told as "fall" in three different years. It hides the Zoroastrians now, for whom Takht-e Soleyman is a place of prayer and not only a listing. And it hides that the court's own word was Ērānšahr.

**10. Neighbours and counterparts**

Beside the Sasanians, not inside them: Rome and Byzantium. ŠKZ names their emperors, and the Met describes the plates' Dionysiac borrowings. The Kushans, whose country ŠKZ claims "up to Peshawar". The Sogdians, through whom the roundel silks travelled east. Arabia, across the Gulf, where Mazūnšahr lies "on that side of the sea". Armenia and Iberia, inside or outside Ērānšahr depending on who wrote. Later, the Islamic courts that kept the plates' imagery (F1.10). None is ranked.

**11. Who speaks for it now**

Zoroastrian communities speak before any state, by their own names. The Bombay Parsi Punchayet calls itself "the apex body representing the interest of the Parsi/Irani Zoroastrian Community in India". It traces its roots to 1673. FEZANA, registered in Illinois on 2 June 1987, coordinates 26 or 27 associations in the United States and Canada and writes of a "Zarathushti community". At Takht-e Soleyman, UNESCO says, the fire temple "still serves pilgrims performing Zoroastrian ceremonies". The states hold the sites. Iran registered Firuzabad and Bishapur in 1931 under the 1930 Act. The Iranian Cultural Heritage, Handicrafts and Tourism Organization manages both listings in UNESCO's texts. Iraq holds Ctesiphon under Law 55 of 2002. Iran's export rules run from the 1930 Act through the bans of 1979 and 1980 to the Penal Code of 1996. The Met, the Freer, the British Museum and Berlin hold the silver and stucco abroad; their chains are on their records.

Cultural note: Zoroastrians, Parsi and Irani, keep the religion whose fires the Sasanian kings founded. The Bombay Parsi Punchayet and FEZANA are their own bodies to read. We show no fire temple interior, rite or image of a deity, and we do not interpret the investiture reliefs beyond what the sources print. The links go to the communities' own sites. **12. What is argued**

Content note: this section names the forced movement of people. After 260 Shapur I deported the emperor Valerian's army and the people of captured cities and settled them in Iran. Iranica gives the army as 70,000. They are named here as people; the canon's OCC018 says their own names and lives are lost. You can skip this section; the storyboard carries one line of it at station 7.

- *Whose investiture at Taq-e Bostan?* The king in the open-air relief wears the crown of Ardashir II (379–383). One reading: Ardashir II invested by Ohrmazd and Mithra over a defeated enemy. The other: a posthumous commemoration of Shapur II's victory over Julian, with Shapur as the figure on the right. Iranica gives both.
- *Whose Great Grotto?* "Usually ascribed to Ḵosrow II and occasionally to Pērōz" (Iranica). Tanabe argues from the necklace and earring that the king is Ardashir III (628–630), with the work continued to the Arab conquest. We do not choose.
- *Who made the silks?* The Met places its roundel silk in "Central Asia" and calls the motif "typically Sasanian". It says the centres of production "remain unknown". The court style's reach and the Sogdian weavers' part are both on record (STY096; F1.13).
- *When did it end?* 651 (Wikidata, the Met, UNESCO's synthesis), 658 (UNESCO's short text), 631 (the AAT). The year is a register's, not ours.
- *Did the Roman captives build the dams?* The canon's OCC018 says the attribution "is traditional". Iranica says the deportees "helped to revitalize Persia's urban centers, industries and agriculture". Both readings stand; the people are people in either.

**13. Afterlives**

Parsi and Irani Zoroastrians keep the religion; their bodies are dated above to their own sites. The Pahlavi dynasty of 1925–79 took its name from the Middle Persian script and language (DYN023), which we report and do not endorse. The royal hunt passed into the Shahnama, whose Bahram Gur story the Met's plate 1994.402 is "the earliest known representation" of. The plates' imagery passed to Islamic, Byzantine and Chinese workshops, as the canon's STY096 and the Met's essay say. Remembered: the listings of 2003 and 2018; Taq-e Bostan's nomination to the World Heritage tentative process, reported in 2021. Sold: "Sasanian" on the auction market, where the BM notes copies of its plate "made in Rawalpindi and Peshawar". We report that and do not endorse it.
