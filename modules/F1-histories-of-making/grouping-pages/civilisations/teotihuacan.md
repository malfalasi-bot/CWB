## Civilisation page: Teotihuacan, as commonly named

A tunnel 103 metres long runs under the Temple of the Feathered Serpent, sealed about 250 CE; the city's own name is not recorded, and "Teotihuacan" is what Nahuatl speakers called the ruins centuries later.

**3. A place to stand**

A shaft 0.83 metres wide, in front of the Temple of the Feathered Serpent. Heavy rain opened it on 2 October 2003 while INAH's team was repairing the temple. Sergio Gómez Chávez went down on a rope to 13 metres. He found a tunnel running east under the temple, filled with stones and earth by the people who built it. Excavation began in 2009. The tunnel runs almost 103 metres, drops to about 17 metres, and ends in three chambers under the temple's vertical axis. The builders blocked it with walls, opened it again at least twice, and sealed it for good between 250 and 300 CE. Someone cut back in during the 6th century and found it already full.

**4. The name, and who gave it**

The name is Nahuatl and later. UNESCO prints it as "the place where gods were created"; the canon says Nahua speakers gave it to ruins they found. The Mexica told the legend in the 16th century, and the Nahua authors of the Florentine Codex wrote it down (MKR103). What the city's own people called it is not recorded: their signs are not read as a language. The Getty AAT files "Teotihuacán" as a style and borrows five Nahuatl place-names of the valley (Tzacualli, Miccaotli, Tlamimilolpa, Xolalpan, Metepec) for its phases; those too are later names. Archaeologists made the city a unit of study from the surveys of 1864 and the excavations of 1884, as UNESCO dates them. Leopoldo Batres restored the Pyramid of the Sun in 1905–10 and, UNESCO says, "arbitrarily reconstituted a fifth tier". We use "Teotihuacan" as the only name there is, and say whose it is.

**5. Span and phases**

Every definer gives a different end. The canon ends the city about 550. UNESCO writes of fire and abandonment "during the 7th century" and "towards 650". The TGN has it "sacked by the Toltecs" in 750, a reading no other source read this pass repeats. The start is firmer: INAH dates the tunnel's digging between 100 BCE and the start of our era, and UNESCO's "1st century" matches. Manzanilla's phases for Teopancazco run 150 to 650 CE in three steps. PeriodO holds no Teotihuacan period; its "Early Classic" for Mesoamerica runs c. 250–600. The canon's 100 BCE–550 CE is kept as this page's span, with the end marked argued (claim 12).

**6. Extent**

The map draws one city, not a state. UNESCO gives 36 km² at the city's peak and a ceremonial centre of a tenth of that. The listed property is 250 ha, and the 1988 decree protects more than 3,381 ha. The caption reads "sites attested; the city's edge inferred". The Avenue of the Dead, the three pyramids and the Ciudadela are attested. So are the compounds UNESCO names: La Ventilla, Tetitla, Zacuala and Yayahuala to the west, Xala and Tepantitla to the east. The barrios of outsiders sit on the fringe: Tlailotlacan in the south-west, the Merchants' barrio in the east, a Michoacán group in the west. The city's reach beyond the valley, to Kaminaljuyú and Tikal, is drawn as points joined by dotted lines, because objects attest contact and not rule. The mapping project that drew the whole city was not opened this pass.

**7. Its making**

Knappers pressed prismatic blades from obsidian cores. The canon's network traces the cores from Pachuca and other sources. Mask carvers cut greenstone and onyx marble into faces with a horizontal brow and oval eyes, drilled for inlays of shell and pyrite. The Met reads its large mask as too heavy to wear, "attached to larger, perishable sculptures" or placed in bundles. Builders raised platforms in talud-tablero, a slope under a framed panel, and painters covered compound walls in lime plaster and mineral colour. Potters moulded figurines and Storm God jars; the tunnel holds dozens of them. The tunnel's makers dusted its walls with magnetite and its floor with cinnabar. They cut the floor into a landscape and set drops of mercury in the hollows, 50 grams in all. Manzanilla reads the barrios as workshops of lapidaries, garment makers and headdress makers organised by intermediate elites. No record names a maker. Who organised the work is argued (claim 13).

**9. What the name hides**

The name hides the city's own name, because its signs are not read. It hides the people who lived on its fringe from Oaxaca, the Gulf and Michoacán, whose burials and pottery tell their origins. It hides the people whose remains lie in the tunnel's offerings and the Ciudadela's drains; §12 names them as people. It hides the makers by name, and gives us only roles. It hides the farmers whose fields the 3,381-hectare decree covers, and the two municipalities that share the plans. It hides the end: fire, abandonment, sack and "collapse" are four words from four sources. And it hides that "City of the Gods" is a legend the Mexica told about a ruin they did not build.

**10. Neighbours and counterparts**

Beside Teotihuacan, not inside it: the Maya kingdoms. Their Motagua jade and their pottery lie in the tunnel, and their monuments at Tikal record contact. Monte Albán and the Zapotec, some of whom lived in Tlailotlacan for centuries. The Gulf lowlands, which sent cotton and people to Teopancazco. Kaminaljuyú, which UNESCO names as the reach of "Teotihuacan III". Later, Tula and the Mexica. The turquoise in the tunnel points north, to sources in northern Mexico and the south-western United States, as INAH's Gazzola notes. None is ranked.

**11. Who speaks for it now**

No descendant community speaks for the city in any statement this pass found. The canon says the same. The Nahua, whose language gave the name, are a living people, and the Florentine Codex's authors were Nahua. The Mexican state speaks through INAH, which has coordinated research since 1962, runs the Tlalocan project and published its book in March 2026. The municipalities of Teotihuacán de Arista and San Martín de las Pirámides share the development plan of 2008 and the image regulations of 2009. Article 16 of the 1972 law bars export; the law makes archaeological property public. The Met holds masks and figures with no findspot, and a marker bought in 1980.

Cultural note: the Nahua gave this city its name. The people of two municipalities live beside and on its protected zones. We show no burial, offering, tunnel chamber or mask that may have come from a bundle. We do not say what the city meant to the Mexica beyond what UNESCO and INAH print. The links go to INAH's pages. Ask us to change or remove this item.

**12. What is argued**

Content note: this section names the burial of people killed in the city. INAH's team reports "approximately 50 individuals" placed as offerings in a Ciudadela drain. Manzanilla reports 116 burials at Teopancazco, 32 per cent of them decapitated. They are named here as people; no image is shown. You can skip this section; the storyboard carries none of it.

- *When and how did the city end?* UNESCO: razed by fire and abandoned in the 7th century, "towards 650". The canon: the centre burned about 550, "internal revolt or attack" unknown. The TGN: sacked by the Toltecs in 750. Manzanilla reads tensions between corporate rule and competing barrios as "setting the stage for the collapse". Four readings, four dates; we do not choose.
- *Who organised the crafts?* Manzanilla's reading: intermediate elites in neighbourhood centres drew in foreign workers. They competed in garments and headdresses, while the ruling elite controlled jade and mica. The older reading, in the canon's rows, places obsidian and other workshops in the apartment compounds. Whether the obsidian "workshops" counted in the 1980s were workshops at all was argued by Clark (1986), not opened; marked unverified.
- *What was the tunnel for?* Gómez Chávez's hypothesis: a burial place of an early ruler and a site of investiture rites. Gazzola calls the mercury part of a cosmogram of the underworld's waters. INAH's own 2019 page calls these "hypotheses" being tested. No ruler's remains are reported in the sources read.
- *What was the Ciudadela?* Gómez and Gazzola argue a sanctuary flooded in the rains for thousands of people. It was "lejos de ser un espacio de acceso restringido"; other authors, they say, read it as the seat of power or the ruler's residence. Both are on record.

**13. Afterlives**

INAH's book on the tunnel, presented on 17 March 2026, closes over fifteen years of work. The site's Management Plan 2010–2015 set research, conservation and community involvement as goals, in UNESCO's words. UNESCO's 2012 session asked for conservation guidelines after concrete and polymers damaged some elements. Visitors climb the pyramids by the hundreds of thousands; the listing names development pressure as a rising threat. Remembered: the Mexica legend, told every twenty days in Moctezuma's rites according to 16th-century writings UNESCO cites. Sold: the Pyramid of the Sun on tourism, beer and a thousand souvenirs; the AAT's "Teotihuacán" as a style name on auction lots. We report that and do not endorse it.
