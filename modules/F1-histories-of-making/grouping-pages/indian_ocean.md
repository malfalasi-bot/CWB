## Grouping page: The Indian Ocean

A wind that reverses twice a year let printers in Gujarat, potters in Zhejiang and builders at Kilwa work for buyers they never met.

| 1 | The wind that turns | Sailors timed their year to a wind that blows one way, then the other. Over the Arabian Sea the southwest monsoon runs from about June to September; the northeast monsoon blows in winter. The *Periplus* credits a pilot, Hippalus, with laying a course "straight across the ocean" (§57). | NET005, NET006, TXT073 | Wind timing: documented. | 2 | A ship sewn together | Shipwrights stitched this hull plank to plank with cord, without nails. It sank off Belitung, Indonesia, in about the 830s. Michael Flecker found "every timber was fastened with stitching", in African *Afzelia* planks with teak through-beams from India. | PLC248, TEC143, OBT295, MAT095 | Construction and timbers: documented (Flecker). | 3 | Seven ports, seven towns | Ports were towns where people settled, married and prayed, not only stops. Siraf served the Abbasid Gulf; excavations from 1966 found a "massive haul" of Chinese and Islamic pottery. Aden, Calicut, Hormuz and Malacca each held one sea gate; Quanzhou's Qingjing Mosque and a statue of Mani show a mixed merchant town. | PLC277, PLC294, PLC221, PLC278, PLC245, PLC190, PLC076 | Documented (Whitehouse's excavations; UNESCO 2021 for Quanzhou). | 7 | Families across the water | Merchant families lived on both shores for generations. Gujarati traders ran cloth, beads and credit and settled in Malacca, Mombasa and Zanzibar; Tomé Pires recorded them in Malacca c. 1512–15. The *Periplus* lists enslaved people beside cloth and grain (§13, §31, §36); we name them as people. **Members.** This walk lights 10 networks and diasporas, 11 places, 3 people, 5 texts, 5 techniques and object types, 3 materials, 2 communities, 2 practices and 4 open objects. Networks and diasporas: the Indian Ocean world (NET005), the Arabian Sea circuit (NET006), the Nanhai route (NET009), the Kilwa gold route (NET011), Swahili dhow trade (NET012), the porcelain trade (NET054), the Indian cotton textile trade (NET055), Geniza merchants (DIA004), the Hadrami diaspora (DIA005) and Gujarati merchant communities (DIA006). Places: Siraf, Aden, Calicut, Hormuz, Malacca, Quanzhou, Kilwa Kisiwani, Fustat, Zanzibar Stone Town, the Belitung wreck and, through the letters, Mangalore (no canon row). People: Abraham Ben Yiju (PER326); Ashu, named in his deed but with no row of her own; Ibn Battuta (PER325), who visited Kilwa in 1331. Texts: the *Periplus* (TXT073), the Geniza documents (TXT066), Ben Yiju's letters and accounts (TXT067, project DOC-001), the Kilwa Chronicle (TXT048) and the *Rihla* (TXT072). Techniques and types: sewn-plank boat building (TEC143), dhow building (TEC144), celadon (TEC034), the dhow (OBT295) and the celadon bowl (OBT070). Materials: cotton (MAT056), teak (MAT095), coir (MAT069, for stitching; not confirmed for Belitung). Communities: Waswahili (COM061) and Siddi (COM203). Practices: lenj building and sailing (PRA143) and Swahili dhow building (PRA023). The Swahili coast (CIV004) is a civilisation label that opens onto these rows; it is not a member ranked above them.

**Neighbours.** Overland, the Silk Roads (NET001) carried some of the same silk and ceramics by relay. The Red Sea network (NET032) and medieval Mediterranean trade (NET023) joined it at Aden and Fustat; the Geniza merchants worked in both. At its eastern end it meets the Nanhai route (NET009) and the Chinese maritime diaspora (DIA007). From 1498 the Cape Route and the East India companies (NET033) cut into it by force. Vasco da Gama reached Calicut that year (OCC053); Portuguese forces sacked Kilwa and Mombasa in 1505 (OCC054). Parallels elsewhere: trans-Saharan trade (NET010), which also moved gold, cloth and cowries (NET013); the transatlantic trafficking of enslaved Africans (NET035), which was larger in scale and shorter in span; Austronesian voyaging (NET045), which reached Madagascar across the same ocean.

**Afterlives.** The word "calico" is named after Calicut (canon PLC221). Kilwa Kisiwani and Songo Mnara were inscribed as World Heritage in 1981; Quanzhou followed in 2021 as "Emporium of the World in Song-Yuan China". The Belitung cargo was sold to Singapore's Sentosa Leisure Group in 2005 for US$32 million; the Smithsonian cancelled its exhibition in 2011. Siraf is now under the village of Taheri and near oil and gas works (Current World Archaeology, undated web page). The Siddi descend from East Africans who came as soldiers, sailors, merchants and enslaved people. The 2011 census counted 10,477 in Karnataka and 8,661 in Gujarat. In 2011 UNESCO recorded that fibreglass boats were replacing wooden lenj and that yards were turning to repair work. "Maritime Silk Road" is the name many governments and tourist boards now use for these routes (canon other names).

**What is argued.**

- *Who found the monsoon route?* The *Periplus* (§57) credits the Greek pilot Hippalus with learning to cross open water. Other readings hold that Arabian Sea sailors trading between Africa, India and Southeast Asia knew the pattern long before (Wikipedia, "Monsoon of South Asia"). We show the Greek text as one outsider's claim.
- *Whose coast?* Colonial-era writers framed the Swahili towns as "Arab" or "Persian" colonies. African archaeology since the 1960s reads them as African towns working in Indian Ocean trade (canon CIV004, naming Chittick, Horton and Chami). The Chinese bowls in tomb façades support the second reading: local builders chose where they went (Carfantan).
- *What was the revolt of 869–883?* One reading: a rising of enslaved East Africans, "Zanj", against Abbasid landowners. Ghada Talhami and M. A. Shaban argue it was a wider uprising of the poor, with free Africans, Bedouin and peasants (Wikipedia, "Zanj Rebellion"). Both are shown.
- *Should a salvaged cargo be shown?* The Smithsonian judged that showing the Belitung finds would condone "commercial exploitation of unscientifically excavated objects". Singapore and Indonesia read the cancellation as a slight (Trafficking Culture). The Atlas links out and states the salvage history.

**Apply** (F1.13 Networks; links to F1.6, F1.9 and F1.10)

- **Maker (M).** The Met's 14th-century Gujarati print (30.112.28) copies woven silk patterns from Iran and Egypt in stamped, mordant-dyed cotton. Design up to three blocks that give a costly surface through a cheaper process, for a buyer in another country. Note what your process can and cannot copy, and write a maker's credit for the printer.
- **Briefer (B).** Write a one-page order from Madmun in Aden to a workshop in Mangalore for bronze bowls. Set the deadline by the sailing season, not a calendar date. Name who carries the risk if the ship is lost. Name the workshop's makers, even as "the casters of Ben Yiju's workshop", and state what you do not know about them.
