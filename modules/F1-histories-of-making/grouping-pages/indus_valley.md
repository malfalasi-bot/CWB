## Grouping page: Indus Valley civilisation

Brick drains, chert weights and carved seals, about 2600–1900 BCE: a modern name gathers them, and nobody knows what their makers called themselves.

**Card**

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| Kind | Civilisation, as commonly named (CIV048). It is a search label that opens onto the Harappan archaeological cultures beneath it. It is not a self-name, and the Atlas does not rank it against other civilisations. |
| Defined by (who, when) | John Marshall and the Archaeological Survey of India, announced in the *Illustrated London News* in September 1924. "Harappan" comes from the type site, Harappa. Archaeologists and museums in Pakistan, India and elsewhere use both names. "Sindhu–Sarasvati civilisation" is used mainly by supporters of Indigenous Aryanism and is politically contested. Jim Shaffer (1992) recast the whole as an "Indus Tradition" of eras (PRD142). |
| Span | About 3300–1300 BCE (canon, approximate). Beneath it: Early Harappan about 3300–2600 BCE (ARC138), Mature Harappan about 2600–1900 BCE (ARC139), Late Harappan about 1900–1300 BCE (ARC140). Earlier farming villages such as Mehrgarh (ARC137) sit before it, not inside it. |
| Places | The Indus and Ghaggar–Hakra plains, with Gujarat and the Balochistan edges; today Pakistan and northwest India. Cities: Harappa (PLC215), Mohenjo-daro, Dholavira, Rakhigarhi, Lothal (PLC216); craft town Chanhu-daro. By 2002 over 1,000 Mature Harappan settlements had been reported, just under a hundred excavated. |
| Membership confidence | Documented for finds excavated at named sites. Probable for museum seals labelled only "Indus Valley", which are attributed by style. Contested at the edges: hinterland villages, and whether Late Harappan communities still belong. |
| Leaves out | Its makers' own name and language, since the signs are unread. Whether the signs are writing at all. The farmers, herders and hill communities of Balochistan and the hinterland. Its neighbours in Dilmun, Magan, the Helmand and the Oxus. Present-day people of Sindh, Punjab, Gujarat and Haryana. The Late phases, often told as "collapse". |

Harappa.com images are © Harappa.com: link out only. Objects in Pakistan's and India's national collections: link out unless the holder licenses. **Members.** In the canon: 1 civilisation-name (CIV048); 3 archaeological cultures (ARC138 Early, ARC139 Mature, ARC140 Late Harappan); 1 period scheme (PRD142, Shaffer's eras); 2 places (PLC215 Harappa, PLC216 Lothal), with 4 more proposed (Mohenjo-daro, Dholavira, Rakhigarhi, Chanhu-daro); 2 object types (OBT126 stamp seal, OBT165 Indus weights); 3 "earliest known" rows (INV006 Indus script, INV173 standard weights, INV099 wild silk); 4 materials (MAT006 carnelian, MAT009 steatite, MAT082 conch shell, MAT022 faience); 2 techniques (TEC155 fired brick, TEC043 faience). Eight verified objects: four Met seals, one Cleveland seal, the Nippur bead, the Damb Sadaat bowl and a photographed set of weights. The key members are the seals, the weights and the carnelian beads, because they travel between every city.

**Neighbours.** Before it: the farming village of Mehrgarh (ARC137, PLC214). Around it: hill communities of Balochistan (Damb Sadaat), the Helmand craft town of Shahr-i Sokhta (CIV070, PLC273), and the Oxus towns (CIV089, ARC104). Across the sea, through NET016: Dilmun (CIV060, POL099, PLC289), Magan and the Umm an-Nar communities (CIV061, ARC061, PLC292), and Mesopotamia, where Indus carnelian reached Ur (PLC265, PER279). After it: Late Harappan regional crafts (ARC140) and the Vedic period (PRD143), whose link to the Indus heritage is argued and politically charged. Parallel names elsewhere, with no ranking: Dilmun, Magan, Helmand and Oxus are also "civilisations as commonly named" in the canon.

**Afterlives.** Bead-making in Khambhat (Cambay), Gujarat, still works agate and carnelian. Kenoyer, Vidale and Bhan recorded it in 1989 and 1990: heating nodules, chipping, sawing, grinding, diamond-tipped drilling and polishing. Merchant families ran the workshops; Bhil labourers mined the stone; women strung the beads. They used this to argue that standard products need not mean state control. "Agates of Cambay" was registered as an Indian Geographical Indication in 2008 (Culture and Heritage, 2023). That page also reports silicosis among workers and dates the Muslim bead trade to Baba Ghor, about 1500 CE. In 2023 ThePrint profiled Anwar Husain Shaikh, a fifth-generation bead-maker who hand-drills Harappan-style beads. Its claim that today's makers descend from Harappan craftsmen is not evidenced; the continuity shown is of stone and technique. Remembered: Mohenjo-daro became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1980, Dholavira in 2021. Sold: museum shops and brands reuse the "unicorn" seal; Cleveland's own wall text still calls the sites "mysterious", which this page does not repeat.

**What is argued.**
- Are the signs writing? Farmer, Sproat and Witzel (2004) argue they are non-linguistic emblems: texts average 4.6 signs, the longest single surface has 17, and frequent signs rarely repeat within a text. Rao and colleagues (2009) argue the signs' conditional entropy is closer to natural languages than to non-linguistic systems. Both sides still publish; no decipherment is accepted.
- What happened after about 1900 BCE? Giosan and colleagues (2012) link shrinking and eastward-moving settlement to river and monsoon change, and use the word "collapse". Petrie (2019) stresses the cities' long resilience, notes that "different social, economic and political structures" were in place by about 1900 BCE, and calls the causal data inconclusive. Shaffer's scheme (PRD142) names it "Localisation", not collapse.
- Was Lothal's basin a dock? S. R. Rao, its excavator, argued a dockyard. Leshnik (1968) and Yule (1982) argued a water tank, since the inlet seems too narrow for sea-going boats. Later finds of foraminifera and salt crystals suggest seawater once filled it.
- Who ran the cities? No palaces or royal tombs are identified. Petrie argues several elite groups of roughly equal standing, with ways of limiting concentrated power. Kenoyer, Vidale and Bhan's Khambhat study warns that standard beads and weights can come from many workshops, not only from a state.

**Apply.** F1.5 (where the canon routes INV006 and INV173), with the seals and the weights.
- Maker (M): write the label for Met 49.40.1 in three tiers, each true on its own. Show the signs but do not translate them; say who made the seal, what is known, and what is argued.
- Briefer (B): write the brief clause for a brand borrowing Indus motifs. Ban "lost civilisation" and invented translations; require the holder, licence and 1970 status of any seal image; name Pakistan and India as the source countries.
