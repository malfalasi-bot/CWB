## Grouping page: The Middle Horizon (Andes)

Tapestry tunics, a staff-holding god and knotted cords spread across the Andes about 600–1000 CE; whether an empire spread them is argued.

**Card**

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| Kind | Horizon (HOR002), and the period of the same name (PRD189). It is an archaeologists' time term, not a people or a state. It opens onto two polities, Wari (POL058) and Tiwanaku (POL059), and one shared style (STY065). |
| Defined by (who, when) | John H. Rowe's Andean chronology of the 1960s, which alternates "horizons" of wide-spread style with "intermediate periods" (canon HOR001–HOR003). Dorothy Menzel's 1964 monograph split it into ceramic epochs (Middle Horizon 1–4, with sub-phases such as 1A and 1B). Ann Pollard Rowe (2024) re-dates the ceramics Menzel gave to Middle Horizon 3, so the end of the horizon is itself being redrawn. Luis Lumbreras's rival scheme (PRD192) calls the same centuries the "Wari Empire" stage. |
| Span | About 600–1000 CE, the conventional range (Jennings 2006; Conlee and others 2021; the Met). Tiwanaku starts earlier: UNESCO gives 400–900 CE for its flourishing and a collapse in the first half of the 12th century. |
| Places | Wari: the capital near Ayacucho, Peru; Conchopata nearby; Pikillacta near Cusco; Cerro Baúl in Moquegua; Huaca del Loro in Nasca; El Castillo de Huarmey on the north coast. Tiwanaku: the capital by Lake Titicaca, Bolivia, with colonies in Moquegua. Many museum textiles come from dry south-coast graves in Ica and Nasca. None of these places has a canon ID yet. |
| Membership confidence | Documented for excavated finds at Wari and Tiwanaku sites. Probable for museum textiles labelled "Wari" by style, with no findspot. Contested at the edges: museums label the same hat type "Wari", "Tiwanaku style" or "Tiwanaku or Wari", and Cleveland keeps modern fakes of Wari-style pots. |
| Leaves out | The makers' own names for their polities: "Wari" and "Tiwanaku" are modern site names. The weavers, spinners and dyers, none named. Coastal and highland communities who lived alongside or under Wari. The Aymara (COM138), for whom Tiwanaku is ancestral. What the staff-holding figure meant; the Met says the role of such beings "remains unknown". |
| Rights line | Met, Cleveland and Art Institute of Chicago records listed below are CC0 or public domain and may be shown. Many Wari textiles came from burials: show them only with origin and a content note. Never show human remains, mummy bundles or tomb photographs, including El Castillo de Huarmey. Tiwanaku site and Gateway of the Sun: link out to UNESCO. Taquile and Chinchero weavers: link out to their own published pages; photographs only with the weavers' permission. |

**Members.** In the canon: 1 horizon (HOR002) and its period (PRD189); 1 style (STY065); 2 polities (POL058 Wari, POL059 Tiwanaku) and their 2 civilisation-names (CIV036, CIV035), both under the Andean label (CIV021); 3 object types (OBT075 kero, OBT139 khipu, OBT048 feather cloak, for Wari feathered panels); 4 techniques (TEC092 tapestry weaving, TEC104 khipu construction, TEC122 tie-resist dyeing, TEC109 featherwork); 3 materials (MAT064 camelid fibre, MAT056 cotton, MAT043 cochineal); 1 "earliest known" row (INV005 khipu). That is 18 canon nodes. No place is in the canon yet; six are proposed: Huari (Ayacucho), Tiwanaku, Conchopata, Pikillacta, Cerro Baúl and El Castillo de Huarmey. Eight open objects are verified below. The key members are the tapestry tunics and four-cornered hats, because they carry the shared style and the labour argument together.

**Neighbours.** Before it: the Early Horizon of Chavín (HOR001, STY064) and the Early Intermediate Period of Nasca (ARC206) and Moche (ARC207, POL057); Moche-Wari style pieces sit on that seam. After it: the Late Intermediate Period, with Chimor (POL060) and Sicán (ARC209), then the Late Horizon of the Inca (HOR003, POL061, STY066). The Inca state's cloth-makers, the Aqllakuna (MKR023), and its khipu keepers (MKR024) inherit two Middle Horizon crafts. Rival frame: Lumbreras's stage scheme (PRD192) names the same centuries by a Wari empire, not by a style. Parallel elsewhere, with no ranking: the Mississippian Ideological Interaction Sphere (HOR007), another shared iconography whose political meaning is argued.

**Afterlives.** Taquile weaving was proclaimed by UNESCO in 2005 and inscribed in 2008. Men there knit chullo hats and weavers make calendar belts that record the ritual and farming year (PRA051). The Centre for Traditional Textiles of Cusco was founded in 1996 by Andean weavers and supporters. In September 2026 its site listed ten communities, including Chinchero, "the birthplace of the CTTC" (PRA053). Raised fields came back in the 1980s: Erickson's project rebuilt fields with Quechua communities at Huatta, Coata and Capachica from 1981 to 1986. By 1989 hundreds of families had adopted them, Erickson reports (1993). Bandy (2005, as summarised on Wikipedia) reports that farmers stopped once projects ended in the 1990s. Remembered: Tiwanaku became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2000. Cleveland's 2012–13 exhibition called Wari "widely regarded today as ancient Peru's first empire". The Met showed WS-007 in "Weaving Abstraction in Ancient and Modern Art" (5 March–16 June 2024). Sold: Wari cloth has long fed the art market, and so have fakes.

**What is argued.**
- Was Wari an empire? One reading says yes: an expansive state with planned administrative centres such as Pikillacta and Viracochapampa, and roads (Schreiber, Isbell and McEwan, as summarised by Jennings 2024). Conlee and colleagues (2021) read compounds, a D-shaped temple and non-local burials at Huaca del Loro as Wari colonisation of Nasca. Moseley, Williams and Nash (2005) call Cerro Baúl a Wari colony and read its brewery as state feasting. The other reading says the evidence was fitted to expectation. Jennings (2006) argues the model of administrative centres ruling through reciprocity "fit[s] our expectations" more than the data. Jennings (2024) keeps the word "empire" but describes low power: rival elite houses and small gatherings, and a heterogeneous horizon of shared ideas, objects and people.
- Was Tiwanaku a state that ran its fields? Kolata (1991) argues state authority organised raised-field farming, with hierarchical ties between city and countryside; UNESCO calls Tiwanaku "the capital of a powerful empire". Erickson (1993) argues farming was organised "at the local level": the fields predate the state and continued after it. Wikipedia's summary of later work (Janusek 2004; Bandy 2013) describes a federation of communities or a network of powerful lineages. It notes no defensive architecture, state roads or princely burials.
- Did the state weave the tunics? The Met's label says scholars believe production "was supervised by imperial administrators"; canon STY065 reads the standard designs the same way. Jennings's low-power model would place fine cloth among competing elite houses and gift-giving instead. That extension to cloth is our reading, not his stated claim.
- Where did the shared image come from, and does sharing mean rule? The Met says Ayacucho elites adopted Tiwanaku images and beliefs; Isbell dates this at Conchopata to Middle Horizon 1A. At Cerro Baúl, Moseley says the two polities "are not borrowing anything from each other". Shared iconography alone does not show imperial control; both sides accept this and argue from other evidence.
