## Movement page: Art Deco (named 1966 to 1968 after the Paris exposition of 1925)

A style nobody called Art Deco while it was made took its name forty years later, and its lacquer came from workers in Paris whom the record calls "Indochinese".

**3. Before the name**

A lacquer writing box, made in Japan in the 1800s, lies in a Met drawer. Gold takamaki-e on black lacquer; the maker is not recorded. Boxes like it reached Paris after 1868. Seizo Sugawara came to Paris in 1900 with Japan's delegation to the exposition, to look after its lacquer, and stayed. In 1912 Jean Dunand, a coppersmith, took thirteen lessons from him; Eileen Gray was his other known student. Sugawara is the named maker behind two of the style's names. After the war of 1914 to 1918, Dunand set up a lacquer studio at the rue Hallé. He bought urushi, the tree sap, from French Indochina. Most of the workers who built up the layers were Indochinese.

**4. The name**

The name came forty years after the things. The Exposition internationale des arts décoratifs et industriels modernes opened in Paris in April 1925 and closed on 8 November. It had been planned since 1912 and postponed by war. The French state ran it; about twenty countries sent pavilions; the MAD counts some 15 million visitors. Nobody there said "Art Deco". Hillier writes that the work was called "jazz modern" or "moderne". In 1966 the Musée des Arts Décoratifs staged "Les années 1925: Art déco, Bauhaus, Stijl, Esprit nouveau". The museum says that show "introduced the term". In 1968 Bevis Hillier, aged 28, published "Art Deco of the 20s and 30s" in London. He says dealers were already using the word. The name came from curators, dealers and a historian, from outside and after.

**5. The makers it championed**

The style praised handwork and credited one name per workshop. Baumeister, a Met conservator, counts 40 to 60 employees in Dunand's studio in the 1920s and 1930s, "nearly half of them lacquer workers from Indochina". For the liner Normandie he employed over a hundred. The Saint Louis Art Museum's 2025 account says his Asian assistants "produced most of the lacquerware". It names two: Madame Nam, "the best poseuse in the studio" (Marcilhac's words), and Madame Ho, photographed applying eggshell. Many returned to Hanoi and opened workshops. Dunand told a dealer in 1920 that curing lacquer was "positively Oriental labor" (his words, as SLAM quotes them). The record keeps his name on every piece and theirs on none. In Mumbai, ICOMOS credits the 1930s buildings to an exchange of "European and Indian" values; the contractors are not recorded.

**6. Named hands**

Ruhlmann, Dunand, Lalique and Le Corbusier are the names the label kept. Jacques-Émile Ruhlmann furnished the "Hôtel du Collectionneur" at the 1925 exposition; Dunand lacquered the embassy pavilion's smoking room and Ruhlmann's furniture. Pierre Chareau's library from that pavilion is rebuilt at the MAD. Le Corbusier built the Pavillon de l'Esprit Nouveau and attacked the rest. Eileen Gray, trained by Sugawara, made lacquer screens. In Shanghai, László Hudec designed the Park Hotel for the Joint Savings Society of four Chinese banks. It opened on 1 December 1934 at 83.8 metres. In Havana, Esteban Rodríguez-Castells and Rafael Fernández Ruenes won the Bacardí competition; the building went up in 1930. In Miami Beach, Henry Hohauser and Albert Anis designed the hotels of Ocean Drive from 1933. Barbara Baer Capitman formed the Miami Design Preservation League in 1976 to save them.

**7. Institutions**

The style ran on expositions, department stores, liners and cinemas. The 1925 exposition stretched from the Invalides to the Grand Palais. The Union centrale des arts décoratifs bought from it; the pieces are the core of the MAD's collection today. The 1931 Exposition coloniale commissioned Dunand's panels "Les peuples d'Asie et d'Afrique" for its permanent palace. The French Line's Île de France (1927), L'Atlantique (1931) and Normandie (1935) carried his lacquer walls. The École des Beaux-Arts de l'Indochine opened in Hanoi in 1925 under Victor Tardieu and Nam Son. In Bombay, the Backbay Reclamation Scheme of the 1920s made the land for Marine Drive. In Miami Beach, the League's Art Deco Weekend has run since 1977.

**8. Reach**

The style went where capital and empire went. In Bombay, under British rule, the reclaimed land west of the Oval Maidan filled with cinemas and flats in the 1930s and 1940s. UNESCO inscribed them with the Victorian Gothic buildings in 2018 as "Indo-Deco". In Shanghai's International Settlement, Hudec's Park Hotel was Asia's tallest building in 1934, built by the Voh Kee Construction Company for Chinese banks. In Havana, the Bacardí rum company's headquarters rose in 1930. In Miami Beach, after the 1926 hurricane, hotels went up from 1933 in what the city calls "tropical art deco". The materials went the other way. Urushi came from Indochina; the canon lists ivory, ebony and macassar from colonies. The Indochinese workers came to Paris within an empire; the state segregated such workers from white workers during the war, SLAM reports.

Baschet, in L'Illustration in 1927, described Dunand's workers as "silent and diligent Asians" with "a resignation" fitting slow labour. SLAM reads this as "diminutive and dehumanizing". **9. Against**

The exposition set itself against the past and against Art Nouveau's curve. Its programme, as coverage reports it, was "open to all manufacturers whose products are artistic in character and show clearly modern tendencies". In our words: the counter-position was the revival style and the whiplash line of 1900. The counter-movement sat inside the fair. Le Corbusier's Pavillon de l'Esprit Nouveau and Melnikov's Soviet pavilion, the Paris libraries say, were "hostile to ornament and the decorative". The Bauhaus was absent; Germany was not invited. The later name "Art Deco" took the side of the ornament. The fight over the word is in section 10.

**10. What is argued**

- *Whose work*. One reading: Baumeister calls Dunand's lacquer oeuvre "remarkable" for its ingenuity, founded on a two-month course. The other: SLAM's 2025 account reads the same studio through its Indochinese workers. They "produced most of the lacquerware", and the credit left their names out. Both are museum readings; we do not choose.
- *One style, or a museum word*. One reading: Hillier's "the last of the total styles", stretching from Ruhlmann's furniture to Los Angeles's streamlined auditorium. The 2003 V&A catalogue, he says, endorsed it. The other: the Paris libraries' account, in which the 1925 pavilions were "coffrets à bijoux" of luxury craft that neglected the manufactured object. On this reading Miami's hotels and Mumbai's cinemas share a word and not a style. The AAT's alternate terms (Jazz Modern, Moderne, Streamline) carry the disagreement.
- *The start*. The MAD says the 1910s; the canon says 1920; the name says 1925. The page keeps all three.

**11. Neighbours and counterparts**

It followed Art Nouveau (STY036). The Wiener Werkstätte's geometry (MKR077) showed in Paris in 1925. Its opponents at the fair were Le Corbusier and the absent Bauhaus (PLC342; MOV011). Its parallel in the north is Scandinavian Modern (STY048), which took the later export route. Its Egyptian motifs followed the 1922 opening of Tutankhamun's tomb (OCC195). Its lacquer line runs to Japan and Vietnam (F1.8; F1.13).

**12. Afterlives**

The listings made the word official. On 14 May 1979 the Miami Beach Architectural District went onto the National Register of Historic Places. The League calls it "the nation's first urban 20th century Historic District" (its words). The Park Hotel became a key national heritage site of China in 2006. UNESCO inscribed Mumbai's Victorian Gothic and Art Deco ensembles in 2018. The Musée des Arts Décoratifs marked the centenary with "1925–2025. Cent ans d'Art déco" from 22 October 2025 to 26 April 2026. Hillier, writing in March 2025, still calls American architecture the style's "finest flowering" (his words). "Art Deco" sells hotels, flats and auction lots in 2026; we report that and do not endorse it.
