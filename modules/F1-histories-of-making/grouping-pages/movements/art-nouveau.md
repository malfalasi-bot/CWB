## Movement page: Art Nouveau (Jugendstil · Stile Liberty · Modernisme · Sezessionstil)

A shop name in Paris stuck to a style that had a dozen names, and in Brussels the ivory and wood came from the Congo.

**3. Before the name**

A woodblock print of a wave, cut in Edo in about 1830, hung in Paris studios. By the 1880s dealers sold such prints across the city. Hokusai designed it; the block cutters and printers are not recorded. Prints like it reached western markets after trading rights with Japan opened in the 1860s. Dealers sold them, and Siegfried Bing was one. Designers in Brussels and Paris read the flat colour and the curling line as a way out of the past. The Met's essay names two more sources. One was English design reform, the Arts and Crafts and Aesthetic movements. The other was the study of plants and sea creatures, in books by Pugin, Owen Jones and Haeckel.

The second thing came from the Congo. In 1893 Victor Horta began the Hôtel Tassel in Brussels. In 1895 he began a house for Edmond van Eetvelde, the Congo Free State's administrator-general in Brussels. The Free State was Leopold II's private possession from 1885. Its exports were ivory and rubber, extracted by Congolese people under force. For the van Eetvelde interior, Horta used wood and ivory from the colony and elephant motifs. Both readings of that fact are in section 10.

Content note: this paragraph names deaths at Tervuren in 1897. The exposition's colonial section exhibited 267 Congolese men, women and children, taken to Belgium by force. Seven died: Sambo, Mpemba, Ngemba, Ekia, Nzau, Kitukwa and Mibange, as the museum records their names. You can skip this paragraph; the dates stay in the claims.

**4. The name**

The words came from a journal and then a shop. In 1884 the Brussels journal L'Art Moderne wrote of "art nouveau" for Les Vingt, twenty painters and sculptors. In December 1895 Bing, a German-born dealer in Paris, opened a gallery he called L'Art Nouveau. The name came from critics and from trade, not from the makers. Elsewhere the same work had other names. Munich called it Jugendstil, after the magazine Jugend, founded there in 1896. Italy called it stile Liberty, after the London shop Liberty & Co, or stile floreale. Catalonia called it Modernisme; Vienna, Sezessionstil. Critics in Belgium and France called it style nouille, noodle style, and style coup de fouet, whiplash. One Belgian name is harder: "Style Congo". A single label for all of it is recent. Delevoy, in Universalis, dates the shared term to the 1970s and 1980s.

**5. The makers it championed**

The style praised nature and copied makers it did not name. Its printed sources were Japanese: woodblock prints and katagami, the stencils cut from mulberry paper for dyeing cloth. The Met holds a stencil of parallel waving lines, cut in Japan in the 1800s. The record does not give the cutter's name. Its material sources were colonial. The AfricaMuseum's own account says the Colonial Palace at Tervuren was "fitted out in art nouveau style" and held the Congo's "economic products". Ivory, hardwoods and rubber filled a hall. The tappers, porters and carvers are not recorded. Closer to home, the record keeps firms and not hands. The Met credits a cabinet to Émile Gallé and its manufacture to Établissements Gallé. The cabinetmakers of Nancy, the glassblowers, the ironworkers who forged Horta's stair rails: role known, names not.

**6. Named hands**

Architects and dealers are the names the label kept. Horta built the Hôtel Tassel in 1893 to 1895; UNESCO now lists four of his houses. Henry van de Velde designed interiors, silver and the Tropon poster of 1898, then taught in Weimar. Hector Guimard's iron and glass entrances for the Paris Métro, from 1900, gave the French their "style Métro". Gallé led Nancy's glassmakers and cabinetmakers and died in 1904. In Glasgow, Charles Rennie Mackintosh and Margaret Macdonald designed the Hill House in 1902 to 1904. The V&A says many of his designs were "often executed or inspired by his wife". Frances Macdonald and Herbert MacNair made up the rest of "the Four". In Barcelona, Antoni Gaudí worked on the Sagrada Família from 1882. In New York, Louis Comfort Tiffany's name sat on lamps that Clara Driscoll's women's glass-cutting department designed. Her letters, found in 2005, gave her the credit.

**7. Institutions**

The style ran on magazines, shops, schools and fairs. Jugend began in Munich in 1896. The Studio, Pan, La Plume and Ver Sacrum carried the graphics across borders. Bing's shop opened in December 1895 and showed six interiors at the Paris exposition of 1900. In Nancy, a show of 1894 drew over seventy exhibitors. The Alliance provinciale des industries d'art followed in 1901, with Gallé as its president. The Glasgow School of Art bought its Renfrew Street site in 1897 and finished Mackintosh's building in 1909. The Vienna Secession opened its own hall in 1898. Expositions did the selling: Brussels 1897, Paris 1900, Glasgow 1901, Turin 1902.

**8. Reach**

The style sold where the empires traded. Japanese prints came in through dealers after the 1860s. In Belgium the material reach was the Congo Free State, Leopold II's private possession from 1885 to 1908. The 1897 exposition ran from 10 May to 8 November. Its colonial section at Tervuren drew over a million visitors, up to 40,000 a day. Paul Hankar designed rooms there; Georges Hobé built a hall of Congolese bilinga wood. The display became the Congo Museum in 1898 and the AfricaMuseum today. In the Netherlands, Nieuwe Kunst took up Javanese batik through colonial rule (STY039). Liberty & Co sold Japanese and other Asian goods in London from the 1870s. Russia, Finland, Latvia and Estonia had their own words for the style.

Cultural note: Congolese makers carved the ivory and tapped the rubber that Belgian Art Nouveau used. In 1897, 267 Congolese people were exhibited at Tervuren. We show no Congolese object and no image of the 1897 villages; the link goes to the AfricaMuseum's own account. We do not say how Congolese communities regard this history or these objects.

**9. Against**

The makers opposed the copying of past styles. The V&A puts it as a wish to revolutionise "the tired historicism" of nineteenth-century design. Les Vingt called for ending the division between fine art and the "so-called lesser decorative arts". In Munich, August Endell claimed a new art of forms that "signify nothing, represent nothing and recall nothing". In our words: the counter-position was the academy and the historical revival, the Beaux-Arts habit of building in borrowed costume. The next generation turned the same charge on Art Nouveau itself. Adolf Loos in Vienna and the Deutscher Werkbund in Munich called its ornament waste (their pages carry the words).

**10. What is argued**

- *Style Congo*. One reading: Debora Silverman argues that Belgian Art Nouveau was "a distinctively Congo style". It was made, she says, from Congo materials and motifs, with van Eetvelde as patron. Brussels's 2023 programme and the CIVA show "Style Congo. Heritage & Heresy" take this up. The other reading: the Met's essay traces the style to nature study, English design reform and Japanese prints, and does not mention the Congo. Belgian makers of the 1890s did not call their work colonial. We do not choose; the claims carry both.
- *One movement or many*. One reading: the V&A calls it "a thoroughly 'international' movement" with shared traits. The other: Delevoy lists a dozen local names and dates the shared label to the 1970s and 1980s. The AAT keeps Jugendstil and Modernisme as narrower terms. On this reading Art Nouveau is a museum category, not a movement its members joined.
- *The span*. The AAT says about 1890 to 1910; the Met essay runs from the 1880s to 1914; the canon says 1890 to 1914. The end depends on whether the Secession and the Wiener Werkstätte count.

**11. Neighbours and counterparts**

Its acknowledged sources are the Arts and Crafts movement (MOV005) and Symbolism (GAP009). Its sibling in Vienna is the Secession (MOV009), with the Wiener Werkstätte (MKR077). Its Nordic cousin is National Romanticism (STY040), where Frida Hansen wove in the style. Its heirs and opponents are the Deutscher Werkbund (MOV011) and, after 1925, Art Deco (STY042). In the United States, Tiffany Studios (MKR032).

**12. Afterlives**

The listings came late. UNESCO inscribed three works by Gaudí in 1984 and extended the listing in 2005. It inscribed Horta's four town houses in 2000 and the Stoclet House in 2009. The Met says the graphics returned in the 1960s. Brussels named 2023 its Year of Art Nouveau and used it to face the Congo. The Musée de l'École de Nancy keeps the Alliance's archive and is open in 2026. Mackintosh's Glasgow School of Art burned in 2014 and again in 2018; in July 2026 the Guardian reported the building still unrestored. "Art nouveau" sells as a dealers' and auction word in 2026; we report that and do not endorse it.
