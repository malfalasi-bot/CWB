## Movement page: Italian postwar design

A department store named the quality in 1954, a New York museum named the country in 1972, and the label still leaves out the people who made the things.

**3. Before the name**

A portable typewriter in a flat metal housing left Ivrea in 1950. Giuseppe Beccio engineered it and Marcello Nizzoli gave it its shape; Olivetti called it the Lettera 22. Camillo Olivetti had founded the firm at Ivrea in 1908, the year Treccani and UNESCO both give. From the 1930s to the 1960s his son Adriano had the town built out by architects and planners, with factory, offices, social services and housing. UNESCO lists that ensemble as "Ivrea, industrial city of the 20th century". The archive that holds the firm's papers began collecting only in 1984 to 1985 and says part of the record is lost.

In Meda, the brothers Cesare and Umberto Cassina founded a cabinet-making firm in 1927. The town lies north of Milan, in the Brianza. Its early designs were drawn in-house. In 1948 the firm separated design from production and began to work with outside architects, Gio Ponti among them. Joiners in the Brianza and machinists at Ivrea made these things before anyone had a word for them. The word came later.

**4. The name**

The quality was named by a shop before the country was named by a museum. In 1954 the La Rinascente department store in Milan set up a prize, the Compasso d'Oro, from an idea of Gio Ponti and Alberto Rosselli. Its earliest edition went to the Lettera 22. The jury's citation said the award "can be considered as being symbolically extended" to the whole of Olivetti's work. ADI, the Association for Industrial Design, was founded in 1956 and took the prize over. ADI's own site says from 1958. The ADI Design Museum says 1959, with full ownership in 1967.

The country was named in New York. From 26 May to 11 September 1972 the Museum of Modern Art showed "Italy: The New Domestic Landscape". Emilio Ambasz directed it. It held 180 objects "produced in Italy during the last decade" and eleven commissioned environments. The press release called Italy "the dominant product design force in the world". The name came from outside, from a museum and a prize, and from the trade's own association. No maker coined "Italian postwar design"; historians draw its span.

**5. The makers it championed**

The prize went to the product. People the citation did not name had made it. Cassina's own history says the Superleggera of 1957 came from Ponti's design and "the experimentation and skills of the Cassina artisan joiners". The firm's word for them is the trade's; ours is joiners, and the record does not give their names. The Valentine typewriter that Cooper Hewitt holds carries a stamp: "made in Olivetti plant/Barcelona - Spain". The assemblers in that plant are not named either. The 1972 show thanked Abet Print, a laminate maker, among its sponsors. The laminate workers whose sheets covered the radicals' furniture are not in the catalogue. The gap sits in the archive, not on the makers.

**6. Named hands**

The names that carry the label are designers and a few engineers. Nizzoli and Beccio made the Lettera 22; the Olivetti archive credits both, the ADI record credits Nizzoli alone. Ettore Sottsass joined Olivetti in 1958 and designed the housing of the Elea 9003 computer. It took the Compasso d'Oro in 1959. Mario Tchou led the engineers who built it. The Valentine of 1968 to 1969 is credited by MoMA and Cooper Hewitt to Sottsass and Perry King. Gio Ponti designed the Superleggera for Cassina. Eleven designers and groups made the environments of 1972. They were Sottsass, Joe Colombo, Gae Aulenti, Alberto Rosselli, Zanuso with Sapper, Mario Bellini, Ugo La Pietra and Gaetano Pesce. Superstudio, Archizoom and the Turin group of Derossi, Ceretti and Rosso made the rest. One of the eleven was a woman.

**7. Institutions**

Four institutions held the system together: a prize, a fair, an association and a museum abroad. The Triennale began as a biennial at Monza in 1923 and moved to Milan in 1933. The Compasso d'Oro ran under La Rinascente from 1954 to 1957 and then under ADI, which was founded in 1956. The prize's historical collection passed to the Fondazione ADI in 2001. On 22 April 2004 the Ministry of Cultural Heritage declared that collection "of exceptional artistic and historical interest". The ADI Design Museum in Milan shows it today. In New York, MoMA took the Lettera 22 and the Lexikon 80 into its collection in 1952, by the Olivetti archive's account. It staged the 1972 show. Cassina bought the worldwide rights to the Le Corbusier, Jeanneret and Perriand models in 1964. It named the I Maestri collection in 1973.

**8. Reach**

The system sold outward, and the state helped it. The 1972 show was presented "under the sponsorship of the Ministry of Foreign Trade of Italy" and the Institute of Foreign Trade. Gruppo ENI, Fiat, Olivetti and Alitalia paid too. We quote MoMA's own sponsorship line because it shows the entanglement plainly. An exhibition that staged a "growing distrust of objects of consumption" was paid for by an export ministry. The firms whose objects it showed paid as well. Olivetti bought the American typewriter maker Underwood in 1959, Treccani records. The Valentine was assembled in Spain. Cassina's I Maestri took designs made in France in the 1920s and sold them from Meda. The reach ran out through exports and museums and ran in through rights bought abroad.

**9. What is argued**

- *Who designed the Lettera 22 and the Valentine.* One reading: the designer's name is the credit. The ADI record (Nizzoli) and most accounts (Sottsass) have it so. The other: the credit is shared. The Olivetti archive has it so for Beccio; MoMA and Cooper Hewitt have it so for Perry King. Both readings stand in holder records. We do not choose.
- *Memphis as end or continuation.* One reading is Memphis's own. Its 1981 debut was "disobedience" against "good design", a break with the system the prize had built. The other is Alessandro Mendini's, quoted by Domus. Alchimia worked "in a problematic, multidisciplinary manner". Memphis took "the path of the strong, calm, reassuring image of the establishment". On that reading Memphis is the system's next product, not its end.
- *Whether the 1972 show reported or made the category.* One reading: Ambasz reported "current design developments in Italy", as the museum's words say. The other: the museum called a country "the dominant product design force in the world" while that country's trade ministry paid. On this reading it was naming a market. The press release supports both; we hold both.

**10. Neighbours and counterparts**

Its neighbours are selection systems. The "Good Design" shows at MoMA and the Chicago Merchandise Mart (MOV024, 1950 to 1955) are one. Britain's Council of Industrial Design (INS061, 1944) is another; Japan's G-Mark copied it (grouping-pages/movements/japanese-postwar-design.md). Its counter-movement inside the label is Italian Radical Design (MOV028). Its hinge is Memphis (MOV029) and Studio Alchimia, which open the Postmodernism page (grouping-pages/movements/postmodernism-in-design.md). Its parallel in state-run selection is Japan's Good Design Award (GAP081, 1957).

**11. Afterlives**

The label is now a museum, a heritage listing and a brand. ADI's collection has been national cultural heritage since 2004 and the ADI Design Museum in Milan shows it. UNESCO inscribed Ivrea in 2018 under criterion (iv) as "the testing ground for Olivetti". Cassina sells the I Maestri re-editions with the heirs' foundations in 2026. Memphis Milano trades under its name in 2026 and calls itself a "non-historic" brand. "Italian design" and "Made in Italy" sell as marketing words in 2026; we report that and do not endorse it.
