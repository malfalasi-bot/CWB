A state set up a mark in 1957 to answer a charge of copying, and the son of the Mingei movement's founder made its stool.

**3. Before the name**

A protest reached the occupation's headquarters in Tokyo in 1949. The United Kingdom complained that Japan was exporting textiles that copied British designs; Germany and the United States followed, the award's own history says. The government revised its export inspections and design registrations. In 1956 a Council of Design Promotion was formed inside the Patent Office. The award traces its own origin to that charge of copying.

Two other things came before the word. On 7 May 1946 Masaru Ibuka and Akio Morita founded Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo in a bombed department store. It had ¥190,000 of capital and about twenty people. In 1955 it sold the TR-55, which the company calls "Japan's first transistor radio"; the words are Sony's. And in 1952 Yanagi Sori opened the Yanagi Industrial Design Institute in Tokyo. He was the son of Yanagi Sōetsu, who had coined "mingei" in 1925. He had worked as Charlotte Perriand's assistant while she travelled Japan in 1940 to 1942.

**4. The name**

The mark was named by the state in 1957. A Special Section for Good Design inside the Council for Design Promotion chose products that year. The architect Junzo Sakakura chaired its committee of 42 experts, the award's history says. The Ministry of International Trade and Industry formed a Design Section in 1958. It ran the "Good Design Products Selection System", the G Mark system, for forty years. The model was Britain's Council of Industrial Design of 1944, which the award names. In 1963 the system opened to public submissions. In 1984 it took in all industrial products. In 1998 the ministry privatised it as the Good Design Award.

The movement named itself. In 1960, at the World Design Conference in Tokyo, a critic and four architects issued a bilingual booklet, "Metabolism 1960: The Proposals for New Urbanism". Its authors were Kawazoe Noboru, Kikutake Kiyonori, Kurokawa Kisho, Maki Fumihiko and Otaka Masato. The designer Awazu Kiyoshi drew its symbol. The company named itself too: Mujirushi Ryōhin, "no-brand quality goods", began in 1980 as a store's own brand. The label "Japanese postwar design" is none of theirs; it is historians'.

**5. The makers it championed**

The stool's record keeps its makers by institution, not by name. The V&A says Yanagi developed the Butterfly stool "in collaboration with the Sendai Research Institute". That was the Miyagi prefectural industrial arts institute, which had moulded plywood aircraft fuselages during the war. Isamu Kenmochi directed it in the mid-1950s. The prototype was launched in 1956 at the Matsuya store in Tokyo. Tendo Mokko then made it commercially, 20 to 30 a year until the 1990s, in a wooden press. Those pressers are not named. Sony's history names its founders and the president, Tamon Maeda, and no assembler. Muji's policy, as Cooper Hewitt's record puts it, is that products "are generally not attributed to individual designers"; its suppliers are unnamed by design. The gap sits in the archive, not on the makers.

**6. Named hands**

The public faces are designers, founders and a group of architects. Yanagi Sori designed the stool, kitchenware and, by MoMA's record, "automobiles, and monuments". Ibuka and Morita built Sony; the name was adopted in 1958. The Metabolists built little of what they drew. Kurokawa's Nakagin Capsule Tower in Ginza, completed in 1972, was the one capsule building realised. Expo '70 at Osaka carried their pavilions. Tange Kenzo taught them and employed some; whether he was a member is argued in section 9. Tanaka Ikko, Koike Kazuko and Sugimoto Takashi gave Muji its look and its name, with the retailer Tsutsumi Seiji. Issey Miyake founded his studio in 1970 and showed in Paris from 1973. His studio's own text credits "the 'we' rather than 'I'" and names the textile designer Makiko Minagawa. Pleats Please launched in 1993; A-POC began in 1998 with Dai Fujiwara.

**7. Institutions**

A ministry, a conference, a store and a museum carried the label. The G Mark passed from the Patent Office to MITI in 1958, with the Japan Chamber of Commerce and Industry as secretariat. JIDPO, founded in 1969, took the trademark in 1970 and the selection duties in 1974. The World Design Conference of 1960 was Japan's earliest postwar international design meeting, a conference paper says. Seiyu launched Muji in 1980 and Ryohin Keikaku became its company in 1989. MoMA took the Butterfly stool in 1958 as a gift of the designer, and the V&A added one in 2017 (W.5-2017). The Japan Folk Crafts Museum, founded by Yanagi's father, had the son as its director from 1977.

**8. Reach**

The system was built to export, and it says so. The award's history states that from the 1960s "the early objective of 'design to promote exports' came into clear focus". Sony licensed the transistor from the United States and set up Sony Corporation of America in 1960. Muji opened its earliest overseas store in London ten years after its 1983 Tokyo store, Cooper Hewitt's record says. Miyake showed in Paris. The reach ran outward on licences, stores and shows. It also ran inward. The model for the mark was British and the plywood technique was the Eameses'. The stool's form, the V&A says, "recalls Japanese architecture". The neighbour page records where Mingei's collecting reached: Korea, Okinawa, Hokkaido and Taiwan under Japanese rule. This page does not repeat those holds; it links to them.

**9. What is argued**

- *Shield or programme.* One reading is in the award's own history. The mark began as a response to protests about "imitation products", and its early objective was export promotion. The other is in the same body's academic paper. The reform of 1984 made it a system "to appeal the charm the design" and to make a "Japan Original". Both are JDP's own words from two documents. We hold both.
- *Mingei's heir or an engineer's stool.* One reading is the V&A's label. The stool's simplicity "reflects an inheritance from the Japanese Mingei movement". The other is the same record's technical history. The prototype came off wartime fuselage presses at a prefectural institute, with the Eameses' moulded plywood as the model. Japan House London adds the plywood researcher Inui Saburo. The stool is both; the readings weight it differently.
- *A Japanese idea or a shared moment.* One reading: Kawazoe tied Metabolism to the periodic rebuilding of Ise shrine. The booklet presented it as Japanese, as Interstices reports. The other: its forms followed the megastructure projects then "dominating global architecture discourse", with Archigram as a parallel. Rem Koolhaas's phrase about "the only non-white avant-garde" is quoted there as a provocation. Both readings are in the same article; we do not choose.
- *Tange as member.* One reading: Tange was the group's teacher. He also made its two bracketing projects, the Tokyo Bay plan of 1960 and the Expo roof of 1970. The other: the 1960 booklet's authors do not include him. Contested.

**10. Neighbours and counterparts**

Its parent is Mingei (MOV096; grouping-pages/movements/mingei.md). The stool's designer was Yanagi Sōetsu's son and the museum's later director. Muji's "no brand" echoes Mingei's "unknown craftsman", whom that page shows was never unnamed by accident. Its models were Britain's Council of Industrial Design (INS061) and the "Good Design" shows in the United States (MOV024). Its Italian parallel is the Compasso d'Oro (grouping-pages/movements/italian-postwar-design.md). Its late neighbour is Postmodernism (STY051), which took Kuramata and Isozaki into Memphis and Kawakubo into the V&A's 2011 show (grouping-pages/movements/postmodernism-in-design.md).

**11. Afterlives**

The mark outlived the ministry that made it. Since 1998 the Good Design Award has been a private, fee-paying system run by JDP, and it runs in 2026. The Butterfly stool is still made. The V&A records production rising to 3,000 to 4,000 a year from the 1990s, on a steel press. Muji sells in 2026 as a "no-brand" brand across many countries. Nakagin was demolished from 12 April 2022. Its owners and management company had agreed in March 2021 to sell the site. Of its 140 capsules, 23 were preserved, and one is shown at MoMA in New York. Fukasawa Naoto, a designer for Muji, has directed the Japan Folk Crafts Museum since 2012. "Japanese design" sells as a retail word in 2026; we report that and do not endorse it.
