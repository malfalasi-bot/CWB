## Movement page: Mingei (民藝)

A word coined by three men on a journey in 1925 praised makers it never named, and its collecting followed the map of an empire.

**3. Before the name**

A faceted white jar, painted in underglaze blue, sat in a house at Abiko in 1914. Asakawa Noritaka, who had lived in Korea since 1913, had brought it to Yanagi Sōetsu, then an editor of the magazine *Shirakaba*. Joseon potters had made it; the record does not give their names. The museum Yanagi later founded dates his turn to craft from that jar. In 1916 he travelled to Korea and stayed with Asakawa Takumi, Noritaka's brother, who spoke Korean and studied Joseon wares. By 1940 he had been there twenty-one times.

Korea under Japanese rule was the place where the ideas were formed. After the March First protests of 1919, Yanagi published an essay on Koreans. In July 1922 he wrote against the planned demolition of Gwanghwamun, the gate of Gyeongbokgung palace; the gate was spared. In 1924 he and the Asakawa brothers opened a Korean Folk Art Museum in a hall of that palace. The Governor-General let them use the building. Nine years earlier the colonial government had cleared palace grounds for its 1915 exposition (EVT011). The museum was Japanese-run, in a Korean palace, under Japanese rule. Both readings of that fact are in section 9.

Content note: in September 1923, after the Kantō earthquake, Koreans in Japan were killed on rumours that they planned to riot. Yanagi wrote to Bernard Leach calling it a "Great massacre of Korean people". You can skip this paragraph; the dates stay in the claims.

**4. The name**

The word came in December 1925, on a road in Kishū. Yanagi, the potter Kawai Kanjirō and the potter Hamada Shōji shortened minshūteki kōgei, "crafts of the common people", to mingei. Yanagi put it into English as "folk craft" and refused "folk art", because art meant a named artist. In April 1926 four men signed and circulated a prospectus for a Japan Folk Art Museum: Tomimoto Kenkichi, Kawai, Hamada and Yanagi. The name came from inside, from the people who would run the movement.

**5. The makers it championed**

The things the word named were cheap and used. A rice bowl, a water jar, a stencilled cloth, a painted scroll sold at Ōtsu to travellers. Yanagi's theory held that the best of them were made in quantity by makers who did not sign, and that their beauty lay in that. The museum's own history still speaks of "unknown craftspeople". The record keeps their roles, their kilns and their districts, and rarely a name. Minagawa Masu, who painted teapots at Mashiko, is one of the few a London show named in 2024. Boro, the patched cloth of the poor, was left out: Yanagi's circle thought it too close to poverty.

**6. Named hands**

The movement that praised the unnamed became known through names. Hamada had built a kiln at St Ives with Bernard Leach in 1920 and settled at Mashiko in 1924. Kawai fired a climbing kiln at Gojōzaka in Kyoto and left his pots unsigned. Serizawa Keisuke learned stencil dyeing from Okinawan bingata and made it his own. The museum's opening show in October 1936 was of named makers. Kawai, Hamada, Tomimoto, Serizawa, Tonomura, Kawakami Sumio, Munakata Shikō and Yanagi Yoshitaka were listed. In 1955 the state named Hamada a holder of an Important Intangible Cultural Property, in the year the system began. Kawai refused the title. A reviewer of Kim Brandt's book asked what the book did not. How did a movement built on the unknown maker live with "celebrity 'artist' potters like Hamada Shōji"?

**7. Institutions**

Mingei built a pavilion, a magazine, a shop, an association and a museum. A pavilion called "Mingeikan" stood at the Ueno exposition in March 1928, designed by the members and moved afterwards to Yamamoto Tamesaburō's grounds in Osaka. The magazine *Kōgei* began in January 1931; Yanagi edited it from the third number to the last, number 120. A short-lived museum opened in Hamamatsu in April 1931 and closed in 1933. Yoshida Shōya opened the Takumi shop in Tottori in June 1932 and a Tokyo branch in Ginza in December 1933. The Japan Mingei Association was founded in June 1934 with Yanagi as chairman. In May 1935 the Kurashiki industrialist Ōhara Magosaburō offered ¥100,000 for a museum. The Japan Folk Crafts Museum opened at Komaba in October 1936, in Oya stone and timber, to Yanagi's design. He became its founding director. Tokyo listed the old wing as a cultural property in 2021.

**8. Reach**

The movement collected where the empire reached. The Association staged a "contemporary Korean folk crafts" show at Takashimaya in 1938. From late December 1938 Yanagi, Kawai and Hamada spent a month in Okinawa. In March 1939 a larger party stayed three to six months, making and teaching pottery and dyeing. They bought textiles, dyed cloth, pots and lacquer. A third trip in December 1939 went as a "Ryukyu tourist group", a fourth in July 1940. The museum's own history adds Tōhoku, Kyūshū, and the craft of Ainu makers and of Indigenous peoples in Taiwan, which it calls "Taiwan tribes". In January 1942 the Association wrote its purpose as the creation of a "simple, healthy, national life culture" (our translation). In January 1943 it joined the state's wartime craft control body, the Dai Nippon Kōgeikai, without dissolving. Brandt reads the early 1940s as the movement's alignment with "fascist aspects of Japanese cultural policy".

Cultural note: Uchinanchu (Okinawan) makers made the textiles, pots and lacquer bought in 1938 to 1940, and Ainu women made the robes shown. We show none of these objects and link to the holders; we do not say how the makers' descendants regard the collecting. Korean wares collected under Japanese rule are treated the same way until a reader from each community has read this page.

**9. What is argued**

- *Mingei and empire*. One reading: Brandt argues the movement was "crafted in the context of efforts by Japanese state and society" to manage industry and empire. She follows it into Korea, north China, Manchukuo and Okinawa. Ch'oe Harim (1974) called Yanagi's view of Korea an "aesthetics of colonialism" (quoted through Kikuchi). The other reading: Kikuchi shows Yanagi's protest after 1919, his defence of Gwanghwamun and his letter on the 1923 killings. She also notes he did not question colonial rule itself. We do not choose.
- *The Okinawa dialect debate, 1940*. One reading: in January 1940 Yanagi objected to the prefecture's standard-language campaign and its punishments. He argued Okinawan was a national language of Japan. The prefecture answered in print on 11 January: "do not be misled by the mingei movement". Hanada (1993) calls the Mingei group's position the settled view. The other reading, from Okinawan writers: the push for standard Japanese also came from Okinawans who met discrimination on the mainland. Outsiders praising the island's difference overrode its people's own needs. Both readings are shown.
- *The named and the unnamed*. One reading: the famous potters betrayed the theory. The other: they financed and carried it, and the museum's collection is the theory's proof. Lee (2008) and Inglesby (2024) both raise it; neither settles it.
- *Arts and Crafts as source*. Kikuchi (2004) argues Mingei theory was eclectic, with strong influences from Ruskin and Morris. The movement presented itself as Japanese in origin. The William Morris Gallery says it "developed in dialogue with the work of William Morris". Jones (2023) stresses the differences: no programme for industry or architecture, and Pure Land Buddhism after 1945. Contested.

**10. Neighbours and counterparts**

It set itself against the Tokyo art world of named artists (bijutsu). It set itself against machine goods, and later against the iemoto houses of tea. Its acknowledged neighbour is the Arts and Crafts movement (MOV005). Its parallels are Swadeshi in India (MOV081), the Craftsman style in the United States (STY035), and Sōsaku-hanga and Shin-hanga in Japan (MOV095, MOV094). It fed Studio pottery (MOV041) through Leach and Hamada. The state's Living National Treasures system (INS028) is the counterpart that absorbed it.

**11. Afterlives**

The word outlived the circle. From about 1960 to the mid-1970s a "mingei boom" sold handmade goods, and new kilns opened to meet it (Moeran, 1981). The Japanese government used "mingei" in official documents in the 1950s as a category of craft (Jones, 2023). Martha Longenecker, who had studied pottery in Japan, opened Mingei International Museum in San Diego in May 1978. Yanagi's son Sōri, a designer, was among the Komaba museum's later directors. Since 2012 it has been run by Fukasawa Naoto, a product designer. In 2024 the William Morris Gallery staged *Art Without Heroes: Mingei*. Its Korean, Okinawan and Ainu objects were chosen with the Ainu artist Mayunkiki; Theaster Gates showed "Afro-Mingei" work. "Mingei" sells as a shop word and a design word in 2026; we report that and do not endorse it.

**18. Success criteria**
