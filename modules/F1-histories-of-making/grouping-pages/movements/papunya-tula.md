## Movement page: Papunya Tula and Western Desert painting

Men at a government settlement painted a school wall in 1971, set up their own company in 1972, and have decided ever since what the public may see.

**Cultural note:** Pintupi, Luritja, Anmatyerr, Warlpiri and Arrernte people made the paintings this page describes, and the company they own, Papunya Tula Artists Pty Ltd, sets what may be shown. **3. Before the name**

A wall at a government school stood blank in Papunya in 1971. The government had built the settlement in 1959, 240 kilometres north-west of Alice Springs. It was "an administrative centre" for Aboriginal people who had moved in from the desert, the company's own history says. Pintupi, Luritja, Anmatyerr, Warlpiri and Kukatja people lived there. Some Pintupi had met Europeans only in the 1960s, the S.H. Ervin Gallery's text adds. The designs the men carried were older than the settlement by far. They belonged to body and sand painting "associated with ceremony", in the company's words, and to the rules about who may make and see them.

Anmatyerr men at Papunya had another model. Kaapa Tjampitjinpa and his countrymen had worked on cattle stations. They knew the watercolours of the Hermannsburg school, begun by the Western Arrernte painter Albert Namatjira (SCH042). The NGV's curators set the two groups apart. The Anmatyerr had that model. The Pintupi had not painted in watercolour; in April 1971 they began drawing in pencil and crayon on paper on the teacher's verandah.

**4. The name**

The painters named their company; museums named the movement. The company's history says the movement "began in 1971 when a school teacher, Geoffrey Bardon, encouraged some of the men" to paint a school wall. The NGV names seven men of the Honey Ant mural of late July 1971. The painters were Kaapa Tjampitjinpa, Billy Stockman Tjapaltjarri, Long Jack Phillipus Tjakamarra, Johnny Warangkula Tjupurrula and Don Ellis Tjapanangka. They worked "under the direction of custodians" Mick Wallangkarri Tjakamarra and Tom Onion Tjapangati. The mural has since been destroyed, the NGV records. In August 1971 Kaapa's painting won the Caltex Art Award in Alice Springs.

In June 1972 the painters met at Charley Creek near Alice Springs. Asked what to call the company, a Pintupi man proposed "Papunya Tula". It is the name of a hill near the settlement and a Honey Ant Dreaming site. The NMA gives his name as Charlie Tarawa (Tjaruru), the NGV as Charlie Wartuma Tjungurrayi. The company was established in 1972, "entirely owned and directed by traditional Aboriginal people from the Western Desert". The NMA calls it the earliest Aboriginal-owned arts company in Australia; its own word is "first", quoted here as the museum's. The name came from inside.

**5. The makers it championed**

The paintings derive from makers the paintings do not name. The company says its painting style "derives directly from the artists' knowledge of traditional body and sand painting associated with ceremony". The dancers, singers and ground-painters who hold those designs are not credited on any canvas, and their names are theirs to give. Within the painting rooms there were other hands. The company's 50-year project with the Kluge-Ruhe collection records that wives and daughters "would sometimes assist their husbands and fathers with dotting backgrounds". Their names are not recorded on the works. The gap sits in the record, and in some cases it is the communities' own rule, which this page does not override.

**6. Named hands**

The public faces are the painters the galleries name. We name them as the company and the galleries do. Kaapa Tjampitjinpa was the mural's principal painter. Johnny Warangkula Tjupurrula over-dotted his water Dreamings. Uta Uta Tjangala, Mick Namarari Tjapaltjarri, Tim Leura Tjapaltjarri and Clifford Possum Tjapaltjarri painted the large canvases of 1974 to 1994. The Paris exhibition showed them. From 1996 women painted for the company as independent artists, after the Minyma Tjukurrpa canvases of 1994 and 1995. Makinti Napanangka, the AGNSW says, was "the most senior woman painting" with it. Geoffrey Bardon, the teacher, is the one non-Indigenous name the company's history gives. The canon records that his name is often made to stand for the men's.

Content note: some Aboriginal communities do not speak the names of the dead. This page names deceased painters only as the company and public galleries publish them. A reader named by the company decides whether any name stays. You can skip this section; the dates stay in the claims.

**7. Institutions**

The company is the institution, and the painters own it. Papunya Tula Artists Pty Ltd has 49 shareholders; its board "change[s] every year and rotate[s] between shareholders and established artists" (its FAQ). After the homelands movement of the 1980s it built studios at Walungurru (Kintore) and Kiwirrkurra, up to 700 kilometres west of Alice Springs. It asks that visits to the studios be arranged with it beforehand. The Aboriginal Arts Board of the Australia Council was created in 1973 with all-Indigenous members and bought many early works. Tim Leura Tjapaltjarri and Billy Stockman Tjapaltjarri represented Papunya on it. Its collection passed to the National Museum of Australia in 1990. The Australian Museum bought 94 early paintings, the Papunya Permanent Collection, in 1983. The Indigenous Art Code, a voluntary code of conduct for dealers, was launched in 2009 after a 2007 Senate inquiry. Indigenous Art Code Ltd was set up in 2010 to run it.

**8. Reach**

The paintings travelled; the designs did not. The company's work is "represented in most public galleries, major museums, institutions and many large private collections" in Australia and abroad, its history says. Two hundred of the earliest boards went to Melbourne in 2011 to 2012. They then went to the musée du quai Branly in Paris in 2012 to 2013. An exhibition of early boards toured the United States in 2009. The company's 50-year project with the Kluge-Ruhe collection in Virginia opened in 2021. Reach ran the other way too. In 1994 the Federal Court found that carpets made in Vietnam, copying Aboriginal artists' paintings, infringed copyright and caused cultural harm (OCC258). The company's own line on reach is a rule. Showing the stories "has required the removal of sacred symbols and the careful monitoring of ancestral designs".

**9. What is argued**

- *What may be shown, and to whom.* One reading: the early boards are art and belong in galleries. The NGV's curators in 2011 hung 200 of them by artist and were "committed to a 'fine art' or 'art historical' display", Myers reports. The other: some of the paintings' makers and their relatives have asked that boards with ceremonial content not be seen by uninitiated people. Kimber's 2009 consultations found most relatives content for the boards to be shown in the United States, but not to vulnerable people at home. In 2009 the Northern Territory's arts minister, Alison Anderson, a descendant of Papunya painters, halted an international tour of MAGNT's boards. Myers calls the positions "not constant; they are unstable". After the 2012 assessment by the Aboriginal Areas Protection Authority, the Australian Museum restricted 40 of its 94 paintings from view. Permission to see their documents can be had "only through the senior men of Papunya Tula Artists". This page does not rule; it follows the company's rule and shows nothing.
- *Who began it.* One reading is the company's own sentence: a teacher "encouraged some of the men". The other is in the NGV's account and the canon. Kaapa had painted before, the Anmatyerr men had the Namatjira school as a model, and the senior men asked for materials. The canon's row for Bardon lists "the Papunya men who painted, who led the movement" as what his name leaves out. Both readings are in published texts. We hold both.
- *Whose stories.* One reading: the painters painted their own Tjukurrpa, within their rights. The other is Kimber's, as Myers reports it. Some Dreaming tracks crossed the countries of other people "who had not been consulted", and compensation had to be made. At the Yuendumu sports weekend in August 1972, men who shared the traditions objected. The painters then dropped restricted stories and disguised ritual objects. Both readings concern the communities' own law. We report what is published and do not interpret any design.

**10. Neighbours and counterparts**

Its neighbours are the other art centres of the desert, each owned by its painters. Ikuntji Artists at Haasts Bluff worked with its painters from 1993. Warlukurlangu Artists at Yuendumu holds the Warlpiri painters of the Yuendumu Doors of 1983 (COM259). Ernabella Arts (MKR081, 1948) and Tjanpi Desert Weavers (MKR087) are Anangu centres. Its counterparts in the law are the carpets case (OCC258) and the Indigenous Art Code. Its parallel on this program is Mingei (grouping-pages/movements/mingei.md). That movement praised unnamed makers from outside; Papunya Tula's makers own the company that names them.

**11. Afterlives**

The company is the afterlife, and it is the present. Papunya Tula Artists trades in 2026 from Alice Springs, Walungurru and Kiwirrkurra. It says that its success funds health, education, recreation and ceremonial events for Western Desert people. The Indigenous Art Code is under a government review announced in Australia's national cultural policy, with its consultation closed by 2026. The NMA holds more than 200 Papunya works and showed them in 2007 to 2008. The Australian Museum keeps 40 of its 94 boards from view. "Dot painting" sells as a market word and is copied on goods, which the Code and the courts have addressed. We report that and do not endorse it.
