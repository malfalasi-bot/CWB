## Movement page: Postmodernism in design

A word borrowed from a poet, a death announced to the minute, and a museum that defined the movement twenty years after it ended.

**3. Before the name**

A book of 1966 argued for buildings that were "both-and" rather than "either-or". Robert Venturi wrote *Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture* and the Museum of Modern Art published it in New York. It does not use the word postmodern. In 1972 Venturi, Denise Scott Brown and Steven Izenour published *Learning from Las Vegas* with MIT Press. It grew from a Yale studio of 1970 that Scott Brown taught with Venturi. The same year, in St Louis, the city's housing authority began to dynamite the Pruitt-Igoe blocks. Its own chronology gives 16 March 1972 for the test demolition and 21 April for the second, the one the press photographed.

Content note: Pruitt-Igoe's remaining tenants were moved out before the demolitions. A 1977 text blamed its Black residents for the project's failure. This page quotes the opening line of that text and describes the next; it does not reproduce it. You can skip the opening argument of section 9; the dates stay in the claims.

In Milan, Alessandro and Adriana Guerriero founded Studio Alchimia in 1976. Alessandro Mendini joined in 1978 and hand-painted a period armchair in a pointillist pattern, the Proust chair. The group's furniture shows carried the parodic titles "Bau.Haus uno" and "Bau.Haus due". None of these people called what they did postmodern.

**4. The name**

The word came from critics, and the AAT traces it before them. Its scope note records the Spanish poet Federico de Onís using "postmodern" in 1934 and Arnold Toynbee in 1938. Wide use for architecture came only in the 1970s. In 1977 Charles Jencks published *The Language of Post-Modern Architecture* in London. Its opening claim fixed a date: modern architecture "died in St Louis, Missouri on July 15, 1972 at 3.32 pm (or thereabouts)". The date does not match the housing authority's record of March and April 1972; Jencks himself wrote "or thereabouts". The name came from outside, from a critic, and the makers it covered mostly did not take it. In 2011 the Victoria and Albert Museum fixed the span: "from about 1970 to 1990".

**5. The makers it championed**

The label names authors and leaves out hands. Memphis's own history lists designers and architects and no maker of the 55 pieces shown on 19 September 1981. Alchimia's Proust chairs were "individually painted", as a secondary source puts it; the painters are not named. The Portland Building's record names its architect, two contractors as firms and an engineer, and no trade. Scott Brown wrote in 1975 that the star system "defines our associates as 'second bananas' and our staff as pencils". The gap sits in the archive, not on the makers.

**6. Named hands**

The public faces are an author, a critic, a group and a building. Venturi and Scott Brown ran a Philadelphia firm; Scott Brown led its planning and urban design. Jencks wrote the book that named the style and curated a "Critics' Corner" at Venice in 1980. On 11 December 1980 Ettore Sottsass gathered six colleagues in his Milan flat. They were Martine Bedin, Aldo Cibic, Michele De Lucchi, Nathalie Du Pasquier, Matteo Thun and George Sowden. Barbara Radice directed the group's image. Shiro Kuramata, Arata Isozaki, Hans Hollein, Michael Graves and Javier Mariscal joined later, by the group's own list. Graves won Portland's competition of 1979, chaired by Philip Johnson, and the building opened in October 1982. Mendini wrote Alchimia's manifesto of 1984.

**7. Institutions**

A publisher, a biennale, a showroom and a museum carried the word. MoMA published Venturi in 1966; Academy Editions published Jencks in 1977. Paolo Portoghesi's architecture exhibition at the Venice Biennale of 1980 built the Strada Novissima, a street of façades. Hollein's columns ran from a garden to a modernist tower. Memphis showed at the Arc '74 gallery in Milan in September 1981, during the furniture fair. The City of Portland commissioned Graves's building and still owns it. The V&A staged "Postmodernism: Style and Subversion 1970–1990" from 24 September 2011 to 15 January 2012. Glenn Adamson and Jane Pavitt curated it, and it toured to Rovereto and Zurich.

**8. Reach**

The style moved along the routes of publishing, fairs and cities' budgets. Within three months of the 1981 show, Memphis's history says, "over 400 periodicals" had covered it. The V&A's own text says the movement "was meant to resist authority". It then "became enmeshed in the very circuits of money and influence" it had set out to dismantle. Portland's budget records show the other side of reach. A city owned a building whose exterior leaked from the mid-1980s. It considered demolition in 2013 and 2014, then spent up to $195 million rebuilding it by 2020. The style's Japanese and Spanish members are listed by Milan; the page does not yet have their own records.

**9. What is argued**

- *What died at Pruitt-Igoe.* One reading is Jencks's. Modern architecture failed there, on "a social and linguistic level", and the dynamite proved it. The other reading starts from the project's record. It shows a rent strike in 1969, a housing authority with no reserves, and tenants moved out in March 1972 before any charge was set. Katharine Bristol called the architectural story "the Pruitt-Igoe myth" in 1991. Ekin Pınar reads Jencks's next sentence, which blamed the "black inhabitants", as a racial framing that the later myth carried. We quote only the opening sentence, attribute it, and give the reason here. We do not choose which reading is right; we show both.
- *Resistance or market.* One reading is Memphis's own: "disobedience" against "good design". The other is the V&A's: a movement "meant to resist authority" that ended "enmeshed" in money and influence. A fashion designer's flat in Monte Carlo was furnished in its pieces by 1982. Both readings are in published texts.
- *One name or two.* One reading: the texts are Venturi's, as most citations treated them. The other is Scott Brown's. Critics "make a pro forma attribution in an inconspicuous place". They then give "the design of the work and the ideas in the writing" to her husband. Her essay was written in 1975 and held back until 1989. Both readings stand; the canon's own row records the gap.

**10. Neighbours and counterparts**

Its parent system is Italian postwar design (grouping-pages/movements/italian-postwar-design.md), through Alchimia and Memphis. Its counter-position is Modernism, and within design the "Good Design" selections (MOV024) and the Bauhaus, parodied in Alchimia's titles. Its Japanese contemporaries are Metabolism's heirs (MOV026, Isozaki) and the Tokyo designers the V&A showed (grouping-pages/movements/japanese-postwar-design.md). Its parallel in architecture criticism is Jencks's "Post-Modern Classicism". Italian Radical Design (MOV028) is its earlier neighbour in Milan.

**11. Afterlives**

A style that announced the death of its predecessor became a museum period. The V&A's show of 2011 to 2012 and its catalogue gave the label a span and a canon. The museum's text calls it "perhaps the most controversial" of modern movements. The Portland Building entered the US National Register of Historic Places in 2011. That was "decades ahead of the usual 50-year threshold", by the city's monograph. It was rebuilt in 2017 to 2020 with new cladding, clear glass and a seismic upgrade. Memphis Milano trades as a brand in 2026 and calls its 40 years "disobedience". The Jencks Foundation publishes his slides and essays in 2026. The word sells as a style name in furniture and graphics in 2026; we report that and do not endorse it.
