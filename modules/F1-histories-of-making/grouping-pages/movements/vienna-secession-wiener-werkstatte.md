## Movement page: the Vienna Secession (1897 to now) and the Wiener Werkstätte (1903 to 1932)

An artists' association that still runs its own hall, and a workshop company that went bankrupt: two bodies, two spans, one word "Vienna".

**3. Before the name**

A paper stencil, cut in Japan in the 1800s, lies flat in a Met drawer. Katagami like it were sold in Europe by the 1890s. The Leopold Museum says Moser's cover for Ver Sacrum of 1899 shows the stencil's "strong impact" on his work. The cutter's name is not recorded. Vienna's makers had also looked at Munich, where a Secession formed in 1892, and at Glasgow. The Künstlerhaus, the artists' society of the Ringstraße, controlled which paintings showed in Vienna and abroad. In 1896 the younger members lost a vote there. Josef Engelhart, Carl Moll and Gustav Klimt talked over what to do next.

**4. The name**

The name came from inside, and it was borrowed. On 27 March 1897 the critic Ludwig Hevesi reported a new "Vereinigung bildender Künstler Österreichs – Wiener Secession". On 3 April Klimt told the Künstlerhaus it had been founded. Forty names stood on the founding list, half of them Künstlerhaus members. After a stormy general meeting on 22 May, Klimt wrote the resignations on 24 May. "Secession" copied Munich's secession of 1892; "Ver Sacrum", sacred spring, named the magazine. The city's own wiki carries these dates. The Werkstätte took its name in 1903: Wiener Werkstätte, Vienna workshops, written WW on its goods.

**5. The makers it championed**

Both bodies praised craft and printed designers' names. The Secession's statutes put "Kunsthandwerk", craft, beside painting and architecture as equals. The Werkstätte's mark was a guarantee of handwork. Yet a Hoffmann tea set in the MAK is captioned "execution: Wiener Werkstätte (Konrad Koch)", and a Moser tray "(Alfred Mayer)". Most executing silversmiths, bookbinders and cabinetmakers have no such line. The MAK says many women employees "remain of which we know little to almost nothing". The health-insurance registrations of the late 1920s are their only trace. Grete Blatny, born 1908, designed textiles; the MAK records nothing more. The Japanese stencil cutters are not recorded at all.

**6. Named hands**

Klimt, Moser, Olbrich and Hoffmann are the names the label kept. Klimt was the Secession's president from 1897 to 1899. Joseph Maria Olbrich, under thirty, designed its hall; Moser drew the owls and the façade fresco. Otto Wagner joined in 1899. In 1903 Hoffmann, Moser and the industrialist Fritz Waerndorfer set up the Werkstätte. Waerndorfer paid for eleven years and left in 1914. The MAK's 2021 research identified about 180 women who worked for the firm. Mathilde Flögl, Maria Likarz, Felice Rix and Vally Wieselthier "defined the look" of the interwar WW, the MAK says. Wieselthier headed a department, the earliest documented woman to lead one there. Dagobert Peche joined in 1915. Alfred Hofmann was the last financier, from 1930. In 1939 the Nazis forced him, because he was Jewish, to sell the archive far below its value. He emigrated and survived.

**7. Institutions**

The Secession built a hall before it had a home. The city council allowed "a provisional exhibition pavilion" for at most ten years on 17 November 1897. Karl Wittgenstein and the takings of the opening show paid for it. The cornerstone went down on 28 April 1898; the building was finished on 29 October; it opened on 12 November 1898. Ver Sacrum ran from 1898 to December 1903. The Werkstätte took over a building at Neustiftgasse 32–34 in 1904. It added fabrics in 1910, fashion in 1911 and the Artists' Workshops in November 1916. Seven women and one man had founded Wiener Kunst im Hause in 1901, which the MAK calls the firm's direct forerunner. In 1907 the Werkstätte signed the Deutscher Werkbund's founding call as one of twelve firms.

**8. Reach**

The firm sold where the money was. Its clients, a secondary account says, were artists and Vienna's Jewish upper middle class. Its largest job was abroad: Adolphe Stoclet, a Brussels banker, commissioned a house from Hoffmann in 1905 with no limit on cost. It was finished in 1911 with work by Moser, Klimt, Metzner, Luksch and Powolny. Branches opened in Karlsbad (1909), Zürich (1916 to 1917), New York (1922) and Berlin (1929). The Werkstätte showed at the Deutsche Gewerbeschau in Munich in 1922 and the Paris exposition of 1925. Zürich, New York and Berlin did not pay. After 1929 the firm cut sections and sold other makers' goods, and in 1932 it filed for bankruptcy.

Content note: this section names the Nazi years. On 10 November 1939 the Secession's last assembly voted for a forced merger into one Nazi-run artists' union. The party named three liquidators, Hoffmann among them. In 1939 the state museum bought the Werkstätte archive from Alfred Hofmann under duress. The MAK says some of the WW's women "presumably became victims of the Nazis". You can skip this paragraph; the dates stay in the claims.

**9. Against**

Both bodies set themselves against the Künstlerhaus and the past. Just and Pangerl, from the state archives, give the aims. They "richteten sich gegen die konservativen historisierenden Strömungen" of the academies: in our translation, they were directed against the conservative historicising currents. Its statutes put the Gesamtkunstwerk, the total work of art, at the centre. In our words: the counter-position was the Ringstraße's borrowed styles and the society that chose what Vienna saw. The counter-movement then came from inside. Adolf Loos gave the lecture "Ornament und Verbrechen" in Vienna in 1910. It was printed in French in 1913 and in German in 1929; the date 1908 is a later error. His charge: ornament wastes the maker's labour and the buyer's money. The Werkstätte was his target.

**10. What is argued**

- *Who made the Werkstätte's look*. One reading: Hoffmann, Moser and Peche, the names that "enjoy global fame" (the MAK's words). The other: the MAK's 2021 research credits Flögl, Likarz, Rix and Wieselthier with defining the interwar WW, and counts about 180 women. The press of 1916 called the Artists' Workshops "a gaggle of laughing young girls". We do not choose; the Nodes list both.
- *A movement, or an exhibiting society*. One reading: Wikipedia and the canon treat the Secession as a movement of about 1897 to 1914 or 1939. The other: the city's wiki says the Klimt group left in 1905. After that the association "lost importance rapidly" (our translation), yet the Verein never ended. On this reading the "movement" is the eight years to 1905, and the association is a gallery that has run itself since.
- *Ornament*. Loos read ornament as wasted labour; the Werkstätte read handwork as its value. The UNESCO text calls the Stoclet House's "austere geometry" a turning point toward Art Deco and the Modern Movement. Both readings are on record.

**11. Neighbours and counterparts**

Its parent label is Art Nouveau (STY036) and Jugendstil (STY037). Its models were the Munich Secession, the Arts and Crafts movement (MOV005) and Mackintosh's Glasgow (PER422), probably shown at the Secession in 1900. Its counter-voice is Loos (PER425). Its heirs and partners are the Deutscher Werkbund (MOV011), which it joined in 1907, and the Austrian Werkbund of 1930. The Stoclet House links it to Brussels and to Art Deco (STY042).

**12. Afterlives**

The archive outlived the firm. Alfred Hofmann and the museum signed a deed of gift in 1955; the MAK holds about 16,000 design sketches and 20,000 fabric designs. Its 1967 exhibition began what it calls a "still-booming renaissance". The Secession was refounded by decree on 31 October 1945 and exhibits in its hall in 2026. UNESCO inscribed the Stoclet House in 2009. The MAK showed "Women Artists of the Wiener Werkstätte" in 2021 and Felice Rix-Ueno in 2023 to 2024. The Jewish Museum, New York, shows "Modernity and Opulence: Women of the Wiener Werkstätte" from 17 July to 15 November 2026. "Wiener Werkstätte" sells as a dealers' and reproduction word in 2026; we report that and do not endorse it.

**Members.** Secession, documented: 40 on the founding list of 3 April 1897; Klimt, president 1897 to 1899; 23 exhibitions to 1905; a Klimt group of 18 left in 1905. Werkstätte, documented: 3 founders (1903); about 180 women artists (MAK, 2021); Peche from 1915; Hofmann from 1930. Contested: Klimt (WW), Mackintosh (Secession), Loos (WW). Institutions: Ver Sacrum (1898 to 1903), Wiener Kunst im Hause (1901), the Kunstgewerbeschule, the Deutscher Werkbund (1907), the MAK (archive, 1955). Places: Vienna, Brussels, Karlsbad, Zürich, New York, Berlin. Objects: two open (NGA 2017.60.1.1-8; Met 42.91.188); the rest link-out.
