## Grouping page: The waqf (pious endowment)

A founder gave property away forever, and its rents paid for buildings, lamps, books and their repair for centuries.

**Card**

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| Kind | Institution: a commissioning regime in Islamic law (INS044). A founder (*wāqif*) dedicates property in perpetuity; its income pays for a named charitable purpose |
| Defined by (who, when) | Muslim jurists, from hadith about perpetual charity; the full legal form probably dates from the 9th century (Wikipedia, "Waqf"). Each foundation is defined by its own deed (*waqfiyya*, Ottoman *vakfiye*), validated by a qadi and two witnesses (Isoldi 2025) |

**Members.** 1 institution (INS044), with 10 people, 4 places and makers, 5 object types and 3 styles or techniques lit by the walk. People: Hürrem Sultan (PER317), Suleiman I (PER316), Mimar Sinan (PER319), Sultan Hasan (PER308), Fatima al-Fihri (PER302, contested), and, without canon IDs yet, Khawand Baraka, Ahmad al-Mihmandar, Amir Aydakin, Sultan Barquq and the maker Hasan ibn Sulaiman al-Isfahani. Places and makers: Iznik and its potteries (PLC305, MKR057), the court studio (INS042), Kairouan (PLC023); Mihrimah Sultan (PER318) commissioned two Sinan mosques, but our sources did not open her deeds; the Süleymaniye, Sultan Hasan complex and Qarawiyyin need place IDs. Object types: mosque lamp (OBT024), Qur'an stand (OBT183), illuminated Qur'an (GAP024), tile, carpet (OBT184 prayer rug as the nearest). Styles and techniques: Mamluk style (STY104), Ottoman classical (STY109), enamelled and gilded glass (TEC049). Polity: Mamluk Sultanate (POL042). Canon links not yet made: PER231 Jahanara Begum and PER301 Zubaidah bint Ja'far funded caravanserais and pilgrim wells, but our sources did not say whether through waqf.

**Neighbours.** It defined itself against inheritance and the ruler's treasury: property made waqf could not be sold, inherited or seized, and founders used it partly to protect wealth from confiscation (Hesperis-Tamuda 2020, citing Saidouni). Wikipedia ("Waqf") notes its close resemblance to the English trust. Parallel groupings elsewhere: the Christian chantry, monastic and hospital foundations, whose English property the crown seized in 1536–41 (OCC059), a mirror of the takeovers in step 8. The Khmer king Jayavarman VII's temples, hospitals and rest houses are listed in inscriptions (PER273). Hindu temple endowments under the Chola kings (POL192; Thanjavur temple, 1010) are a third neighbour; their endowment records still need a source here. Isoldi (2025) notes that scholars link the waqf's development to Byzantine *piae causae* and the Jewish *heqdesh*. We read all of these as parallels of form, not of descent, unless a source shows contact.

**Afterlives.** Waqf is living law, run in most Muslim-majority states by a ministry (Britannica). In 2007–2010 Turkey's General Directorate of Foundations (Vakıflar Genel Müdürlüğü) spent 21 million lira on the Süleymaniye. Its regional office said earlier restorations ran in 1847–49 and 1959–60, and that the 1960s cement had trapped damp and salts (Hürriyet, 9 November 2010). The mosque reopened for prayer at Eid al-Adha in November 2010 (Dünya, 16 November 2010). In Fez the Qarawiyyin library was restored from 2012 by the architect Aziza Chaouni, approached by Morocco's Culture Ministry, and reopened in 2016 (AP, 19 April 2016). The lamps became collectors' prizes. In the late 1800s the Parisian craftsman Philippe-Joseph Brocard revived Mamluk enamelling and made many lamp copies (Cleveland 1981.10).

**What is argued.**
- *Who founded the Qarawiyyin?* One reading: Fatima al-Fihri, a woman from Kairouan, spent her inheritance on the mosque from 859; this is Ibn Abi Zar's account (d. 1310–20), repeated by UNESCO and Guinness. The other: Roger Le Tourneau (1949) and Jonathan Bloom find the two-sisters, two-mosques story too neat. Chafik Benchekroun (2011) reads a Kufic inscription naming Dawud ibn Idris, dated 877, as the mosque's own foundation text, and calls Fatima possibly legendary (Wikipedia, "Fatima al-Fihriya").
- *Colonial takeover: plan or muddle?* The classic account (Ruedy 1967; Powers 1989) has France set out to dismantle habous in Algeria, climaxing in the 1844 law. Saidouni and Saidouni (2009, 2023) find two French camps, one for seizure and one for gradual reform, until the 1873 seizure (Hesperis-Tamuda 2020; Rivisteweb abstract).
- *Why did Egypt abolish family waqf in 1952?* The official reason was misuse and bad management by trustees. Ibrahim al-Bayoumi Ghanem (2021) argues the decree-law, issued a week after the land-reform law, mainly served land reform and weakened the old landowning class (ida2at; Persée 1953).
- *Conservation or reinvention?* The committee inside Egypt's waqf ministry (1881–1936 under Awqaf) saved Mamluk buildings. It also rebuilt minarets, domes and minbars to its own idea of them; Bakhoum (2021) asks whether this was "conservation" or "reinvention".

**Apply** (F1.17 Who commissions; also used in F1.10 The Islamic world as a network)
- **M (Maker):** Design one object for a public building that must still work in 200 years: a lamp, a bench or a sign. Write its inscription, what it is made of, and the one repair it will need most. Say who is paid to do that repair.
- **B (Briefer):** Write a one-page "deed" for a commission. List the income that pays for it, the order in which money is spent (repair first, as in the Ottoman deeds), the staff, and what happens if the purpose ends. Then name one way a later state or owner could override it.
