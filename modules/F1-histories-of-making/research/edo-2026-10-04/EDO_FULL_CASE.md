# Edo and the Floating World — the full case

Oct 4, 2026 · @Mohammad AlFalasi

Edo Japan, 1603 to 1868, taught as the earliest mass creative industry we can document in full: a city of a million, a four-trade production line, a censor, a market priced in bowls of noodles, and an afterlife that reaches a 2024 banknote. This is the model for every civilisation and movement in F1: the whole cast, the objects, the events, eight lectures, ten interactives, and how they hold together.

## 1. The concept: a creative industry read whole

**The essential question.** How does a city turn pictures into a mass market, and who gets named when it does?

**The thesis.** A nishiki-e print was never one hand. A publisher planned it, paid for it, applied to the censor for it and sold it from his own shop; a designer drew it; a carver cut it; a printer pulled it, two hundred a day; a buyer paid a bowl of noodles for it. The subjects came from licensed businesses (three theatres, the Yoshiwara), the paper from Echizen, the blue from a Dutch ship at Dejima, and the market from a city of a million held together by the shogun's order that every lord live in Edo every other year. The state banned the two best-selling genres in 1842 and cut the price by a third; the trade answered with landscapes, warriors, cats and earthquake catfish. When the country opened, the same prints were bought by the hundred thousand for Paris, copied by Van Gogh, dealt by Frank Lloyd Wright, and in 2024 printed on the thousand-yen note. Everything a learner needs to understand a creative industry is here: roles, money, licence, materials, censorship, celebrity, fashion cycles, export and afterlife. What a learner must unlearn is the single genius: the Met's own spectroscopy shows that the Great Wave's depth was invented by Eijudō's unnamed printers, mixing Prussian blue with indigo and printing the hollow twice.

Read it as one machine. Three inputs at the top, a production line with the publisher in charge, a market priced in mon, and an afterlife that remade the product as "Japanese art". Every lecture in section 5 walks one part of this picture; every interactive in section 6 lets the learner operate one of its joints.

**Why this case is the model.** It is a civilisation (Edo Japan), a movement (ukiyo-e and its schools) and a network (Dejima and the Dutch trade) at once, so one case tests all three page types. It has a named cast in every role, including the publisher, which most histories of making lack. It has dated edicts whose wording survives, prices and print runs from a publisher's diary, and thousands of open objects at the Met, the Art Institute of Chicago, Cleveland and the Library of Congress. Its arguments are live and well documented on both sides. And its lessons transfer directly to the programme's own subject: how a creative industry is organised, licensed, priced and exported.

**What it is not.** It is not a survey of Japanese art. Painting, Buddhist sculpture, Nō, tea and the Kanō school appear only where the print trade touched them. The other makings of Edo (kimono and Edo komon, lacquer, netsuke, Arita porcelain, the sword, carpentry) get one lecture as the trades the same city and the same buyers sustained.

What the spine teaches by itself: the trade's great works cluster after the censor arrived, not before it; the colour revolution of the 1830s was a pigment's price curve; the ban of 1842 produced the landscapes and warriors the West later took for the essence of Japan; and the afterlife was a trade too, with its own publishers, dealers and scandals.

## 4. The object set: 52 open objects and what each teaches

Every object below was read at its holder's record on 4 October 2026, with the licence as the record states it (the Met's `isPublicDomain: true`, the Art Institute's `is_public_domain: true`, Cleveland's `CC0`, the Library of Congress's "free to use"). The set is chosen to teach, not to represent: each object carries one idea the lectures need. The working file holds the full list with image URLs.

**The icon, as an edition.** Met JP1847 and JP10, two of the Met's four impressions of *Under the Wave off Kanagawa*; AIC 1925.3245, whose record states that the Institute's three impressions are all later than the first state. Three impressions of one design, side by side, teach that "the" Great Wave is an edition with states, wear and cost-cutting, which no single image can. With them: Met JP9 *Red Fuji* (93 impressions and five states known) and AIC 1925.3244 *Shower below the Summit*, the "three famous Fujis".

**Hokusai beyond the wave.** AIC 2007.628, eleven volumes of the Manga (1812–78); Met 2013.732, the three-volume Hundred Views of Fuji in a c. 1849 edition; AIC 1943.602, the ghost Kohada Koheiji; AIC 1962.1002, Hokusai at thirty as an actor-print designer under his Katsukawa name. These stop the learner thinking of one picture.

**Hiroshige and the road.** Met JP471 Nihonbashi, station one; AIC 1939.1362 Hakone; AIC 1925.3517 Kanbara's evening snow; AIC 1932.170 Mishima's mist, which shows the gradation blocks; Met JP526 Nihonbashi again from a different publisher's series; AIC 1925.3752 the Plum Garden at Kameido, with Frank Lloyd Wright in its provenance line; Met JP2522 the sudden shower over Shin-Ōhashi, the second print Van Gogh copied, with its date and aratame seals in the margin; AIC 1925.3318, Kunisada's memorial portrait of Hiroshige, 1858.

**The eighteenth century.** AIC 1957.556, a Harunobu dated 1765, the year full colour arrived; Cleveland 1930.197, Kiyonaga's diptych of 1783; AIC 1925.3030, Utamaro's *Takashima Ohisa*, whose record transcribes the publisher's seal and the kiwame censor's seal, the print on which the learner reads the seals; AIC 1958.165, a Yoshiwara courtesan named with her house (content note); Met 2013.768, Kyōden's 1784 album of courtesans' handwriting; AIC 1934.207, Sharaku's *Ōtani Oniji III*; AIC 1934.239, a Sharaku with Tsutaya named in the record as publisher; Met 2013.851, an illustrated playbill for the Nakamura theatre.

**The Utagawa century.** Met JP1563, Kuniyoshi's cat demon of Okazaki; Met JP1115, the Taira ghosts triptych, the warrior genre that replaced banned actors; AIC 1975.477, Yoshitsune and Benkei; Met JP1252, a Kuniyoshi surimono; Cleveland 1930.202, the New Yoshiwara as a famous place; AIC 2004.241–243, a Kunisada actor triptych with Danjūrō VII, the star exiled in 1842.

**Process.** Met 2007.49.284, a key-block proof by Sadahide, the black-line pull from which colour blocks were cut. The one complete process set in a public collection, the Tokunō gift at the Smithsonian (a carved double-sided block, 23 progressive proofs, a dismantled baren, the pigments), is CC0 in its data and "usage conditions apply" on its images: it is linked, not shown, until the Smithsonian is asked.

## 5. The lecture series: eight lectures, about an hour

Each lecture is a station tour on one lens of the Atlas, six to ten stations of 60 to 90 seconds, one idea and one visual per station, drawn live from the data (the Motion Canvas template proved on F1.13). Each has one one-tap prediction per chapter, one "argued" station where both readings are shown, and six closing one-tap questions whose answers appear on the map or timeline. Narration is 800 to 900 words per five minutes, every factual sentence carrying its claim id. Together they run about 62 minutes; the learner can take them in any order after the first.

**Lecture 1 · A city of a million** (8 min, Place lens)

*Thesis:* the market came before the pictures. *Stations:* (1) Edo in 1603, a castle town on a marsh: the map fades up from nothing. (2) The lords' order: from 1635 every daimyō lives in Edo in alternate years, his family stays; arcs draw the retinues' roads converging on the city; the samurai half of the population appears as a band on the city plan. (3) The fire of January 1657 at the scale of the city: six tenths burn; the castle keep never rebuilt; Ryōgoku Bridge and the eastern districts appear in the rebuilding. (4) The 1677 map (LoC) laid over the 1859 map (LoC), a transparency scrub: the learner sees what two centuries added. (5) The census of 1721: over a million, the largest city in the world, shown against London and Beijing from sourced figures, as bars. (6) Who lived where: a block of chōnin land against a daimyō mansion of equal footprint, the Edo-Tokyo Museum's own comparison, as two rectangles; commoner wards held about a fifth of the ground. (7) Nihonbashi: the bridge, the fish market, the publishers' streets named and pinned (Tōri Abura-chō, Bakuro-chō, Kodenma-chō). (8) The licensed pleasures: three theatres and the Yoshiwara pinned, with the content note spoken here, once. *Prediction (one tap on the map):* where did the print publishers cluster? Reveal: Nihonbashi, beside the fish market and the bridge where the five highways began. *Argued:* was Edo a city of a million, or of 1.3 million? The city's own count against the modern estimate, both shown. *Closing:* six taps on the map and the timeline.

**Lecture 2 · Four hands, one sheet** (9 min, Objects lens)

*Thesis:* a print is a team product, and the team's names are mostly lost. *Stations:* (1) The hanshita-e: a brush drawing pasted face down on cherry and cut through, so the original is destroyed; the British Museum's 2021 find of 103 uncut drawings is why we can see one at all. (2) The key block: Met 2007.49.284, Sadahide's black-line proof, with its registration notches outside the picture. (3) The colour blocks: the Great Wave peeled layer by layer, our reconstruction from the Met's analysis, labelled as such; then the Smithsonian's real 23-stage proof set, linked. (4) The baren and the day's work: two hundred impressions a day, water-based pigment and rice paste, the printer's sloped table. (5) The materials: cherry for the blocks, Echizen hōsho of pure paper mulberry, sumi, safflower pink that fades, dayflower blue, orpiment yellow; the chain drawn on the map from the paper villages to Edo. (6) The publisher: he owned the blocks, chose the subject, paid everyone per job, and reprinted until the lines wore. (7) Who was named: the law of 1790 named designer, writer and publisher; the carver appears only from the 19th century as a seal (Hori Take, ten seals recorded); the printer almost never. Hokusai's 1835 letter about Egawa Tomekichi's fee is the one pay record. (8) The Met's finding: the Great Wave's blue is two blues, mixed and double-printed by "Eijudō's anonymous printing masters". *Prediction:* how many people made a print, one, four or ten? Reveal: four trades, and a workshop behind each. *Argued:* Hokusai the genius against the system; the BM's "intensely individual" against Davis, Marks and the Met's own spectroscopy. *Closing:* order the four stages by one drag; name the one trade that was never named.

**Lecture 3 · Colour, calendars and poets** (7 min, Time lens)

*Thesis:* full colour was invented by amateurs for a party. *Stations:* (1) Black ink, hand colour, two colours by 1744: the timeline bands light up as the palette grows, each with an open print. (2) New Year 1765: picture calendars hiding the long and short months, commissioned by two poetry circles, exchanged at parties; the Met's Harunobu with "Meiwa 2" on the neckband, AIC 1957.556 dated 1765. (3) Over a hundred designs in a year; the blocks recut for sale with the patron's name replaced by the artist's. (4) Harunobu's five years. (5) The kyōka circles and surimono: privately commissioned, exempt from the sumptuary rules, thick paper, embossing, metal powders; Met JP1252. (6) Tsutaya turns the circle into a business: the 1788 advertisement inviting poets to submit verses for picture books; Utamaro's insect, shell and bird books. *Prediction:* who paid for the first full-colour prints, publishers, the shogun or poets? *Argued:* "first" against "earliest known": benizuri-e by 1744, nishiki-e 1765, and what "perfected" means. *Closing:* six taps on the timeline.

**Lecture 4 · The publisher's gamble** (9 min, People and Objects lenses)

*Thesis:* Tsutaya Jūzaburō shows what a creative-industry entrepreneur was. *Stations:* (1) Born in the Yoshiwara, 1750; a bookshop at the quarter's gate; the Yoshiwara guides as his monopoly. (2) 1783: the move to Tōri Abura-chō, shown on the map; the shop illustrated in the trade's own prints. (3) The stable: Kyōden, Utamaro, Bakin apprenticed in the shop, Ikku. (4) 1790–91: the Kansei edicts; the kiwame seal; Kyōden manacled fifty days; Tsutaya fined half of what he owned. The seal shown on AIC 1925.3030, the learner's first reading of a print's margin. (5) The answer to the fine: Utamaro's bust portraits, the most expensive prints of the decade, with mica grounds that were later banned. (6) 1794–95: Sharaku's ten months, 140-odd designs, all for Tsutaya, then nothing; AIC 1934.207 and 1934.239 with the publisher's name in the record. (7) Hanaōgi: the only women who could legally be named were courtesans; her name stays on the censored second edition; she escaped the quarter in 1794 and was brought back. People, not pictures: the content note. (8) 1797: dead at 48; the firm continues under his name. *Prediction:* what did the state punish Tsutaya for, a print, a book or a party? Reveal: three comic books. *Argued:* Sharaku's identity, the readings and why it matters less than his publisher. *Closing:* six taps.

**Lecture 5 · Blue** (9 min, Place and Objects lenses)

*Thesis:* the most famous Japanese picture is a European pigment's price curve. *Stations:* (1) A chemist's accident in Berlin, 1704–06; the pigment reaches Nagasaki on Dutch and Chinese ships; the route drawn as arcs. (2) The price falls, 1824–28, from the import records; a line. (3) 1829: Eisen's all-blue fan, censor-sealed, sells so well that a bookseller's memoir says Nishimuraya issued Hokusai's Fuji series in the new blue. (4) New Year 1831: Eijudō's advertisement; the series appears; Met JP1847. (5) Inside the blue: the Met's spectroscopy, Prussian blue mixed with indigo for the dark stripes, pure Prussian blue printed twice in the hollow. (6) One design, many originals: three impressions side by side; the diagnostics the British Museum's census uses, cartouche breaks, the pink sky, the yellow boats, a splinter in the foam that no facsimile has. (7) 111 located impressions, eight states, no surviving blocks; what "original" means for a print. (8) Fuji from the road: Red Fuji and the Shower, and the ten extra views with black outlines, a later re-edition. *Prediction:* where did the blue come from, Japan, China or Prussia? *Argued:* a Japanese image, or a Dutch pigment and a Western horizon; Smith's reading that the blue "articulated" an Edo world-view against Guth's that Meiji policy and foreign buyers made it "Japanese". *Closing:* six taps, one of them "which impression is earlier?".

**Lecture 6 · The ban and the road** (9 min, Time lens)

*Thesis:* censorship made the landscapes. *Stations:* (1) The Tenpō reforms, 1841–43; Mizuno Tadakuni. (2) The edict of the sixth month of 1842, its wording on screen: single sheets of actors, courtesans and geisha concern public morals; publication and the sale of stock are forbidden. (3) The price cap of the eleventh month: 16 mon a sheet, seven or eight colour impressions, triptych at most, when an ordinary ten-colour sheet had sold for 24. (4) What the trade did: landscape, warriors, sumō; Osaka's actor prints stop almost entirely until 1847; Edo's continue as "actors without names", six or seven tenths of sales by a magistrate's 1847 count. (5) The theatres moved: the three houses forced to Saruwaka-chō, Danjūrō VII exiled; the map moves them. (6) Kuniyoshi's answer: 51 sheets of loyal retainers, 8,000 sets, 408,000 sheets in eight months by the publisher's own diary; the cats; the Taira ghosts (Met JP1115). (7) The road: Hiroshige's Tōkaidō, the stations on the map, Kanbara's snow, Mishima's mist; the Hundred Famous Views of Edo as a last portrait of the city, 1856–58. (8) November 1855: the earthquake, and catfish prints by the hundred within days. *Prediction:* what happened to sales after the ban, down, flat or up? *Argued:* did the ban create the landscape print or only speed it (Prussian blue and the travel boom were already there). *Closing:* six taps on the timeline.

**Lecture 7 · The afterlife** (8 min, Place lens)

*Thesis:* the floating world was exported by the hundred thousand, and the export changed what it meant. *Stations:* (1) Dejima's trade reversed: what went out before 1854, copper, porcelain, lacquer; Cleveland's Arita jugs in Dutch silver. (2) 1867: Japan's pavilion in Paris; Bracquemond's Manga story told as a legend, with Eidelberg's doubts. (3) Hayashi Tadamasa: 166,000 prints in 218 shipments, or over 300,000, both counts shown; Bing's journal and his 1890 exhibition. (4) October 1887: Van Gogh's three copies, the plum orchard and the bridge in the rain, the Eisen courtesan from a magazine cover; Met JP2522 beside a link to Amsterdam. (5) Monet's bridge at Giverny, 1895–99 (NGA, open); Whistler's tearoom. (6) America: Fenollosa, the Spauldings, Wright as a dealer, the Buckingham prints; the Plum Garden's provenance line. (7) Japan's own reckoning: Fenollosa's Tokyo show of 1898; shin-hanga and sōsaku-hanga; the Smithsonian's blocks from Tokunō. (8) 2024: the thousand-yen note. *Prediction:* who sold more Japanese prints to the West, Japanese dealers or French ones? *Argued:* exchange against appropriation, the 2015 Boston kimono protests on both sides; and whether manga descends from Hokusai's Manga (Exner's revisionist history against the lineage story). *Closing:* six taps.

**Lecture 8 · The other makings of Edo** (6 min, Objects lens)

*Thesis:* the same city and the same buyers sustained a dozen trades, each with its own licence and its own names. *Stations:* (1) Edo komon: stencil-dyed patterns on samurai dress, each domain its own, the dye-houses along the Kanda river; Met 2001.428.40 and the stencil 55.175.16. (2) Nishijin silk in Kyoto, and why three of its weavers went to Lyon in 1872. (3) Lacquer: the Kajikawa family making inrō for the Tokugawa across generations, and Shibata Zeshin's complete set (Cleveland 1916.848). (4) Netsuke: the 1781 book that listed the carvers; the ivory question flagged. (5) Arita porcelain: the VOC order of 64,866 pieces in 1659; Cleveland's jugs. (6) The sword and the carpenter: Kiyomaro's subscription scheme at three ryō a blade; the 1608 proportion scrolls that fixed every timber as a ratio of the column. *Prediction:* which trade had a written rulebook in 1608? *Closing:* six taps.

**What the lectures leave for the interactives.** Reading a print's margin, peeling the colours, comparing impressions, walking the city map, pricing a sheet, following the blue, seeing the ban before and after, counting an edition, mapping the export, and the Yoshiwara contract: each is a joint of the system a learner should operate, not watch, and section 6 designs them.

## 6. The interactives: ten, each designed

Each interactive opens from the lens its lecture ran on and stays live when the lecture ends. None asks for typed text. Each states what it draws from, what the learner does, what it shows, and its one-tap check. All are built on the open stack of the audit: deep zoom with OpenSeadragon on IIIF or the holders' large images, model-viewer for 3D, MapLibre with OpenHistoricalMap tiles and deck.gl arcs for the maps, CSS scroll-driven animation for the 2.5D beats, Motion Canvas for anything that plays.

**I1 · Read the margin.** *Lens:* Objects. *Data:* AIC 1925.3030 (Utamaro, *Takashima Ohisa*, with the publisher's seal of Matsumura Yahei and the kiwame censor's seal transcribed in the record), then Met JP2522 (Hiroshige 1857, with aratame and date seals), then a third print with nanushi seals of 1843–52. *What the learner does:* taps hotspots on the deep-zoomed sheet: signature, publisher's seal, censor's seal, date seal, title cartouche, series cartouche. Each tap opens the reading: what the mark is, who put it there, what year it implies. *What it shows:* that a print documents its own licensing, and that the margin dates it better than the picture. *Check:* given a fourth print with its seals visible, tap the band on the timeline it belongs to (before 1790, 1790–1842, 1843–52, 1853–75). *Fallback:* if no third seal print is open, the seal chronology is drawn as a strip the learner scrubs.

**I2 · Peel the colours.** *Lens:* Objects. *Data:* Met JP1847 (the Great Wave) and the Met's published analysis; the Smithsonian's 23-stage proof set as the linked reference. *What the learner does:* drags a slider from the key block to the finished sheet; our reconstruction separates the sheet into its printed layers, the black key block, the two blues, the mixed outline blue, the pale sky, the yellow of the boats, and labels each layer as a reconstruction, not a proof. A second slider shows the same design in three impressions. *What it shows:* that colour was added block by block, that the deep blue is two printings, and that "the" print is an edition. *Check:* which layer was printed twice? One tap. *Honesty line:* "These layers are our reconstruction from the Met's spectroscopy; the real progressive proofs are at the Smithsonian", with the link.

**I3 · One design, many originals.** *Lens:* Objects. *Data:* Met JP1847, Met JP10, AIC 1925.3245; the British Museum census diagnostics (three breaks in the cartouche border, the break behind the right-hand boat, the break near Fuji's summit, the late new blocks for the sea and the boats, the pink boats of the last four, the splinter in the foam). *What the learner does:* swipes between impressions; toggles each diagnostic, which draws a circle on all three sheets at once; the sheets are ordered by state. *What it shows:* wear, re-cutting and cost-cutting over an edition of perhaps 8,000; 111 located, no blocks survive. *Check:* drag the three into order, earliest to latest; the reveal shows the Institute's own statement that all three of its impressions are later than the first state.

**I4 · The city, 1677 to 1859.** *Lens:* Place. *Data:* the two Library of Congress maps, georeferenced; the publishers' addresses, the three theatres before and after 1841, the Yoshiwara, Ryōgoku, Nihonbashi; OpenHistoricalMap for the modern street grid. *What the learner does:* scrubs a transparency between the two maps; taps a district for who worked there and what it published; moves the year to 1841 and watches the theatres relocate to Saruwaka-chō. *What it shows:* a trade's geography, and a state's power to move it. *Check:* tap where Tsutaya's shop stood after 1783.

**I5 · What a sheet cost.** *Lens:* Time. *Data:* the sourced prices: an ōban sheet 20 mon in 1805, about 24 by the end of the period, 16 to 32 as the range; a bowl of soba 16 mon for most of the period, 24 by mid-century; a triptych 60 to 72 mon in 1847–48; the 1842 cap of 16 mon; the Samurai Archives benchmarks marked as secondary; a Kuniyoshi set of 51 sheets. *What the learner does:* picks a year on a strip, and the card shows what a print, a bowl of soba, a theatre seat and a triptych cost that year, in mon, with each figure's source and confidence; a second tap shows the 1842 cap landing on the price line. *What it shows:* a mass product priced at a meal. *Check:* in 1842 the state cut the price of a sheet by about what share, a tenth, a third or a half?

**I6 · The blue route.** *Lens:* Place. *Data:* Berlin 1704–06; the Dutch and Chinese routes to Nagasaki; the import-price series 1824–28 from Smith; the 1829 fan; the 1831 advertisement; the Fuji series. *What the learner does:* scrubs the years; arcs draw the pigment's route; the price line falls; at 1829 the fan appears, at 1831 the series. *What it shows:* a global material chain under a "Japanese" image. *Check:* one tap on the map, where was Prussian blue made in 1830, Japan, the Netherlands or Prussia and elsewhere in Europe? Reveal: never in Japan in the period.

**I7 · Before and after the ban.** *Lens:* Time. *Data:* AIC 2004.241–243 (Kunisada actors c. 1820), Met JP1115 (Kuniyoshi's Taira ghosts 1849–52), AIC 1975.477; the edict's wording; the 1847 magistrate's report. *What the learner does:* drags a slider across 1842; the actor triptych fades as the warrior triptych rises; the edict's text appears at the midpoint with its gloss; a second drag shows "actors without names" taking six or seven tenths of sales by 1847. *What it shows:* how a trade routes around a ban. *Check:* which genre was exempt as morally uplifting? One tap: warriors.

**I8 · An edition.** *Lens:* Time. *Data:* the Fujiokaya diary: 8,000 sets of 51 sheets between the 7th month of 1847 and the 3rd month of 1848; 200 impressions a day; a flop of 3,000 printed and 450 sold; the standard estimates (2,000 to break even; the Tōkaidō 12,000 to 15,000; the Great Wave up to 8,000). *What the learner does:* presses play and watches a counter run from the first day: sheets per day, sets, the total reaching 408,000; then switches to the flop. *What it shows:* the scale of the market and the risk the publisher carried. *Check:* at 200 a day, how many days does one printer need for 8,000 impressions of one sheet? Three options.

**I9 · The export map.** *Lens:* Place. *Data:* Dejima's exports; 1867 Paris; Hayashi's 218 shipments (166,000 or over 300,000 prints); Bing's shop and journal; Van Gogh's purchases (660 prints in early 1887); Boston (Bigelow's 30,000, the Spauldings' 6,000), Chicago (Buckingham), Washington (Tokunō's blocks 1889, the Noyes gift 1905); the 2024 banknote. *What the learner does:* scrubs 1854 to 1925; arcs leave Japan with their counts; taps a city for its collection and its collector; a last tap jumps to 2024. *What it shows:* the afterlife as a second trade, with its own dealers and scandals. *Check:* which collection may never be exhibited, by its donors' condition? One tap: the Spaulding gift, Boston.

**I10 · The contract.** *Lens:* Concepts. *Data:* the documented indenture terms c. 1800 (a lump sum, for instance 25 ryō for five years, signed by the father; debts for food, clothes and medicine that could extend the term; temple registration); Hanaōgi's record; Seigle's reading and Stanley's. *What the learner does:* reads the contract's clauses as cards, each with its source; taps between the two scholarly readings, shown as two columns with the same facts under each; nothing is scored. *What it shows:* that the pictures' subjects were people under contract, and that historians disagree about what that meant, without the course taking a side. *Content note first, and a skip.* *Check:* none. This one is read, not played.

**Two things these interactives are not.** They are not guessing games: every prediction is one tap and the answer appears within seconds, on the object, the map or the timeline. And they are not decorations: each exposes a joint of the system in section 1 that the lecture only described.

## 8. Protocol: content notes, holds and what stays out

- **The Yoshiwara.** A content note before lecture 1 station 8, lecture 4 station 7 and interactive I10, with a skip: the pictures' subjects were women under indenture; the course names them as people, gives the contract's documented terms, and shows both scholarly readings without ruling. No image of a named courtesan is shown without her name and house as the record gives them, and none is used as decoration.
- **Shunga.** Named once as a fact of the trade (a genre the same publishers sold); not shown, not linked.
- **Violence and the macabre.** Kuniyoshi's ghosts and the Okazaki cat are shown; the Tenpō punishments (manacling, exile) are named with their actors and verbs; the 1657 fire's death toll is given as the range the sources give.
- **Living makers.** The Adachi Institute's and Mokuhankan's printers, and the Komiya line of Edo komon, are named as the record names them; nothing is quoted beyond 25 words; their own pages are the first source.
- **Ivory.** The netsuke (Cleveland 1947.652) is shown with a line on the material and no trade link.
- **Licences held.** The Smithsonian's process set (images "usage conditions apply"): linked until asked. The Van Gogh Museum's plum orchard (non-commercial): linked. The Rijksmuseum Nagasaki print: shown only once its licence line is read. The Met's Monet bridge and komon stencil (`isPublicDomain: false`): not shown; open substitutes named in section 4.
- **Names.** Japanese order throughout, kanji on first mention, the artist's own art-names where the record uses them (Shunrō for the young Hokusai), "Toyokuni III" with Kunisada. A Japanese reader should check the romanisation and the choice of names before narration is recorded.
- **No reader hold applies** to the case as a whole: no living community's sacred or restricted material is involved. The AI reader protocol's log gets one grouped entry for the Yoshiwara notes.

## 9. What is still missing, and what this changes for every other page

**Missing in the research**, each a bounded task: a wage table for carvers and printers (only Hokusai's 1835 letter was found); the names of any printer (the Met calls them anonymous); copperplate prints as a Dejima import (not confirmed on any page opened); a kimono's cost from the Mitsui account books of the 1680s (the source exists; the figures were not read); the Rijksmuseum licence line; permission or a licence reading for the Smithsonian's proof images; an open impression of Kuniyoshi's *Suikoden*; Katsushika Ōi in the open set; the Japanese-language sources for the 1842 edict read in full rather than in transcription; Exner's book on manga opened rather than reviewed.

**Missing in the build**: the depth maps for the 52 objects (the batch job exists; it needs to run); the eight lecture scripts at full length (the storyboards above are the chapter plans; F1.13's script shows the finished form); the narration audio; the ten interactives as components on the house stack; the three reference pages written against the templates with their fact-check lists; the two band cards; a Japanese reader's pass on names.

**What this changes for every page.** The twenty pages written on 4 October were built to the templates' rules and are thin where this case is thick. The difference is not rigour; it is scope and design. From now on a civilisation or movement page is produced as a *case*, in this order:

1. **The system drawn first**: inputs, production, licence, market, afterlife, as one picture. If it cannot be drawn, the subject is not understood yet.
2. **The cast by role**, with the organiser named first (publisher, guild, patron, platform, state), then makers by trade, then subjects, censors and dealers.
3. **The spine**: eras turned by datable events, with the edicts, prices and counts that survive.
4. **The object set chosen to teach**, each object carrying one idea, with the edition shown as an edition.
5. **The lectures as station tours** on the lenses, with one prediction per chapter and one argued station each.
6. **The interactives as joints of the system**, never games.
7. **Then** the facts block, the ids, the licences, the holds and the fact-check list, which the templates already demand.

Measured against this, each of the twenty pages needs a system diagram, a cast with its organiser, an object set that includes an edition or a workshop, and a lecture plan. That is about two days of research and a day of design per page with the agents and the template, so roughly three months for the sixty Ring-1 pages, which matches the audit's estimate; the difference is that the pages will now be worth building.
