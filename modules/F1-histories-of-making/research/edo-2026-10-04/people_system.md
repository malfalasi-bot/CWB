## 1. The production system of a print

### The four roles (the "ukiyo-e quartet")

A nishiki-e was a team product, never one hand. The V&A calls the team the "ukiyo-e quartet"; Japanese workshops still use the four role-names (documented: V&A; Takahashi Kōbō; Asian Art Museum).

- **Hanmoto 版元 (publisher)** – planned the title, chose the subject, financed blocks, paper and labour, applied for censorship permission, and sold the result from his own shop, by exchange with other publishers, and through retail *ezōshiya* (documented: Hoover Institution "Nishiki-e Defined"; Sumida Hokusai Museum). The Sumida Hokusai Museum notes that a hanmoto combined today's publisher, distributor, bookshop and second-hand bookshop in one business, and that Edo publishers split into *shomotsu-don'ya* (scholarly books) and *jihon-don'ya* (popular fiction and prints) – ukiyo-e belonged to the second.
- **Eshi 絵師 (designer)** – drew the *hanshita-e* 版下絵, the brush drawing that was pasted face-down on the block and cut through, destroying the original (documented: Wikipedia "Ukiyo-e"; V&A). Designers rarely cut blocks.
- **Horishi 彫師 (block carver)** – cut the key block (*sumi-ita* 墨板) and then the colour blocks (*iro-ban* 色板). Workshops were tiered: a master carver (*kashirabori*) cut heads and hair, assistants (*dōbori*) cut the rest (documented: Wikipedia "Egawa Tomekichi").
- **Surishi 摺師 (printer)** – inked each block with water-based pigment and rice paste and took impressions by rubbing the back of the sheet with a *baren* 馬連, a disc of coiled cord in a bamboo-leaf wrapper (documented: Glossary of ukiyo-e; Takahashi Kōbō).

**Pay (what is documented).** Hard figures are scarce. One letter survives: in 1835 Hokusai, securing the carver Egawa Tomekichi for his *Fugaku hyakkei*, offered to forgo his customary share of the carver's fee – which implies that a designer normally took a cut of the carving commission for bringing the work (documented: Wikipedia "Egawa Tomekichi", citing the 1835 letter). Tsutaya Jūzaburō is recorded as paying authors an advance honorarium (documented: UTokyo BiblioPlaza, Satō Yukiko). Carvers and printers were otherwise paid by the publisher per job; no wage table was found in the pages opened (gap noted).

### Blocks, registration, materials

- **Wood**: mountain cherry (*yamazakura*), prized for fine even grain; block size was limited by trunk diameter (documented: V&A; Ronin Gallery).
- **Key block and proofs**: the carver cut the key block first; the printer pulled several key-block proofs (*kyōgō-zuri*) on which the designer marked colours; each colour then got its own block (documented: V&A, which holds key-block proofs for Harunobu). A standard nishiki-e needed about ten impressions; Harunobu's prints used up to a dozen blocks (documented: Asian Art Museum; Wikipedia "Ukiyo-e").
- **Kentō 見当 registration**: an L-shaped notch at one corner and a straight notch on the adjacent side, cut into every block so each sheet could be dropped into the same position (documented: Wikipedia "Ukiyo-e"; Asian Art Museum). The V&A notes that key-block proofs carry these marks outside the picture so the stops could be copied to colour blocks.
- **Paper**: *hōsho* 奉書, a thick, soft, dimensionally stable paper of pure *kōzo* (paper mulberry); the finest, *Echizen hōsho* (Fukui, also called *masa* 柾), came from Echizen and was used for nishiki-e and especially surimono (documented: JAANUS "hōsho", "ōbōsho"). An *ōbōsho* sheet of about 39 × 53 cm was the standard stock, cut into ōban, chūban and the other formats (documented: JAANUS "ōbōsho"). Ōban, the standard print, was roughly 38 × 25 cm (documented: Ronin Gallery). A London dealer notes paper on Hiroshige sets is visibly thinner than on Harunobu or surimono, and that 19th-century stock often had white clay added as a brightener (probable: JapanesePrints-London).
- **Pigments**: *sumi* (soot ink) for the key block; *beni* (safflower) pink, which fades fast and is water-soluble; *ai* (indigo) and dayflower (*tsuyukusa*) blues, often mixed with safflower for purple; orpiment (arsenic sulphide, *shiō*) for yellow and, mixed with indigo, for green; red lead and madder reds (documented: Library of Congress conservation page; Arnold Arboretum FORS/XRF study of MFA prints). Mica (*kira*) backgrounds and metallic powders were luxury effects later proscribed (documented: Viewing Japanese Prints "Sumptuary Edicts").
- **Prussian blue (*bero-ai* ベロ藍, from "Berlin")**: the synthetic ferric ferrocyanide first made in Germany in 1704. Its arrival date is **contested**: Artelino says it reached Japan via Dejima around 1790 and stayed costly until about 1820; the Freer study (Fitzhugh, citing H. Smith) says it was known but little used in the late 18th century and increasingly available after the 1820s; Wikipedia says imported from Holland from 1820 and in large quantities from 1829; the British Museum says it came from both China and the Netherlands. What is **documented** by analysis: Eijudō advertised Hokusai's *Fugaku sanjūrokkei* for New Year 1831 as using the new pigment, and the Met's spectroscopy shows the printers mixed Prussian blue with indigo for the wave's dark stripes and overprinted pure Prussian blue for the hollow (documented: Met "The Great Wave: Anatomy of an Icon"). It was never manufactured in Japan in the period (documented: Fitzhugh).

### Print runs and reprinting

- About 200 impressions could be pulled in a day, and 200 is often quoted as "the usual edition" of a design; blocks were stored and reprinted until the lines wore (documented: Asian Art Museum). Runs were counted in *hai* (cups) of roughly 200 sheets (probable: Deeper Japan).
- Larger numbers are documented for the 1840s: a set yielded 1,000–1,500 sheets at the start of the decade, and from the late 1840s a blockbuster set ran to 8,000 copies in about a month (documented: Hoover "Nishiki-e Defined"). The *Fujiokaya nikki* records Kuniyoshi's 51-sheet *Seichū gishi den* selling 8,000 sets – 408,000 sheets – between the 7th month of 1847 and the 3rd month of 1848, and a Sadahide triptych reprinted to 8,000 sets; a flop in the same diary printed 3,000 and sold 450 (documented: Fujiokaya nikki transcription, Kaei years).

### Prices

- A 1805 record gives 20 mon for an ōban sheet; by the end of Edo the typical retail price was about 24 mon, some a little over 30 (documented: Hoover). Wikipedia's "Nishiki-e" gives a range of 16–32 mon. The *Fujiokaya nikki* prices triptychs at 60 and 72 mon (20–24 mon a sheet) in 1847–48 (documented).
- The comparison everyone uses: a bowl of *kake-soba* cost 16 mon for most of the period – the folk etymology of "ni-hachi soba" (2 × 8) – rising to 24 mon by the mid-19th century (documented: nippon.com; ffa-edo). So a standard print cost a bowl of soba or a little more; luxury impressions cost several times that (documented: Hoover, citing *Edo meisho zue*).
- Other benchmarks from the Samurai Archives price list (secondary, after Kobata): a cheap print 2–3 momme of silver, a good one 20 momme; a theatre seat at Ryōgoku in 1820, 32 momme; the Kansei cap on actors' salaries, 500 ryō (probable).

### Censorship and the seals

- **Before 1790** books and prints answered to the *machi-bugyō* (city magistrates). The Kansei reforms of Matsudaira Sadanobu required, from the 5th month of 1790, that publishers' guilds appoint rotating censors (*gyōji*) from their own members and that *hanshita-e* be submitted; approval was marked by a small round seal reading *kiwame* 極 ("examined"), cut into the key block so it printed on every sheet (documented: JAANUS "aratame-in"; Viewing Japanese Prints "Inscriptions and Seals"). Dating is **contested at the margin**: JAANUS gives 1791–1842 for the kiwame seal; the earliest known sealed prints are late 1790 or early 1791; Forrer says the seal became obligatory from the 8th month of 1793.
- Further Kansei edicts: no named women other than courtesans on prints (1793/1796); no *ōkubi-e* bust portraits of women (1st month 1800, with the odd admission that they were merely "conspicuous"); rebus-titles (*hanji-e*) banned 8/1796 after Utamaro's puzzle series (documented: Viewing Japanese Prints "Sumptuary Edicts"; Forrer).
- **Date seals** with the zodiac year and month were used especially in 1805–10, 1814 and 1852–75 (documented: JAANUS).
- **Tenpō**: from 1842/43 guild self-censorship ended and government *nanushi* (ward headmen) sealed prints with their personal seals – one seal 1843–47, two seals 1847–52, two seals plus date 1852–53 (documented: JAANUS; Ukiyo-e Notes table). In 1853 the single *aratame* 改 seal replaced them, usually with a date seal, until formal censorship ended around 1875–76 (documented: JAANUS; MIT "Date Seals").
- The 1842 edict (6th month) banned single-sheet nishiki-e of kabuki actors, courtesans and geisha, and the sale of existing stock; a magistrate's report of 1847 says that by then six or seven tenths of what sold were actor pictures without names, and that a three-sheet set changed hands between amateurs at 2 shu 1 bu (documented: Ukiyo-e hikka-shi transcription of *Shichū torishimari ruishū*; Forrer). Secondary sources add a cap on colour blocks (six to eight) and on format (triptych maximum) (probable: TheCollector).

### Guilds, districts, shops, lending libraries

- The **jihon toiya** 地本問屋 (picture-book and print publishers' guild) was formally constituted in 1807; Tsuruya Kiemon, Izumiya Ichibei and Moriya Jihei were founder members, and all three served turns as guild censors in 1811–13 (documented: Lyon Collection entries after Marks). Members traded under a shop name (*yagō*), a studio name (*dōgō*) and a personal name (documented: printsofjapan.com citing Turk).
- **Nihonbashi** was the trade's centre: Tsutaya at Tōri Abura-chō from 1783, Tsuruya in the same street, Nishimuraya (Eijudō) and Moriya at Bakuro-chō 2-chōme, Iseya at Kodenma-chō (documented: Sumida Hokusai Museum). Izumiya Ichibei's shop stood at Shiba Shinmei-mae, illustrated in the *Tōkaidō meisho zue* of 1797, whose text calls Edo prints the city's number-one souvenir (documented: Forrer, "Print sellers in Edo").
- **How a print was sold**: displayed in piles and hung in the publisher's shop front; passed to other publishers by exchange; retailed by *ezōshiya*; bought in bulk by travellers as souvenirs; children are shown buying prints in an 1817 illustration (documented: Hoover; Forrer). Illustrations by Kitao Masayoshi (1797), Toyokuni (1801, 1817) and Eisen (1839) show the shops (documented: Forrer).
- **Lending libraries (*kashihon'ya* 貸本屋)**: a guild register of the 5th month of 1808 lists 655 (often rounded to 656) in Edo; *Edo hanjōki* (1832) gives about 800 (documented: Kornicki; Samurai Archives). Each served an estimated 150–200 households and did much of its business by home visits (probable: Samurai Archives). A Tokyo Metropolitan Library page gives 656 for 1806, with over 100,000 customers – a one-year discrepancy, noted.

## 2. The people, by role

### Publishers (hanmoto)

- **Tsutaya Jūzaburō 蔦屋重三郎 (1750–1797)** – born in the Shin-Yoshiwara; opened a bookshop on the Gojikken road at the quarter's gate in the An'ei era, moved to Tōri Abura-chō in 1783; sole publisher of the *Yoshiwara saiken* guides and of all of Sharaku; fined half his assets in 1791 for Santō Kyōden's *sharebon*; died 1797 aged 48 (documented: Sumida Hokusai Museum; Suntory; Samurai Archives). Seal: ivy leaf under a three-peaked Fuji.
- **Nishimuraya Yohachi 西村屋与八 / Eijudō 永寿堂 (firm c.1751–1860s)** – Bakuro-chō; publisher of Kiyonaga, Eishi, Toyokuni and Hokusai's *Fugaku sanjūrokkei* (c.1830–33); Yohachi I was a Fuji-kō devotee, portrayed by Toyokuni at 71 (documented: Wikipedia "Nishimura Yohachi"; Sumida Hokusai Museum).
- **Tsuruya Kiemon 鶴屋喜右衛門 / Senkakudō (Edo branch from the 1660s–1852)** – a Kyoto house; published Moronobu's albums in the 1670s, Utamaro, Hiroshige, and the serial *Nise Murasaki inaka Genji* (1829–42), whose blocks were confiscated in the Tenpō purge; business taken over 1852 (documented: Lyon Collection; Sumida Hokusai Museum).
- **Izumiya Ichibei 和泉屋市兵衛 (prints from c.1776–1886)** – Shiba Shinmei-mae; Kiyonaga, Toyokuni, Utamaro, Hokusai, Hiroshige; guild founder member (documented: Lyon Collection).
- **Moriya Jihei 森屋治兵衛 / Kinshindō (c.1797–1886)** – Bakuro-chō; leading Bakumatsu house; Utamaro, Toyokuni, Kunisada, Hiroshige, Hokusai's bird-and-flower ōban; "Moriji's bad carving" was proverbial (documented: Sumida Hokusai Museum; Samurai Archives).
- **Ezakiya Kichibei 江崎屋吉兵衛 (c.1773–1852)** – Shun'ei actor prints, Utamaro and Eisen beauties (documented: Lyon Collection).
- **Uoya Eikichi 魚屋栄吉** – publisher of Hiroshige's *Meisho Edo hyakkei* (1856–58, 120 sheets with the Gengyo title page) (documented: hiroshige.org.uk; Forrer).
- **Eirakuya Tōshirō 永楽屋東四郎 (Nagoya, from the An'ei era to 1951)** – publisher of the *Hokusai manga* (documented: Sumida Hokusai Museum).

### Designers (eshi), by school

- **Hishikawa Moronobu 菱川師宣 (1618–1694)** – Hishikawa school; signed his work from 1672; earliest known single-sheet ukiyo-e prints (documented: Wikipedia "Ukiyo-e").
- **Torii Kiyonobu I 鳥居清信 (fl. c.1700–)** and **Torii Kiyonaga 鳥居清長 (1752–1815)** – the Torii line, hereditary painters of theatre signboards and actor prints; Kiyonaga's 1780s full-length beauties on large sheets (documented: Wikipedia "Ukiyo-e").
- **Okumura Masanobu 奥村政信 (1686–1764)** – self-publisher from 1707; perspective *uki-e* and pillar prints (documented: Wikipedia).
- **Suzuki Harunobu 鈴木春信 (c.1725–1770)** – nishiki-e from 1765; the dominant designer of his five final years (documented: Wikipedia; Met).
- **Katsukawa Shunshō 勝川春章 (1726–1793)** – Katsukawa school; actors with recognisable faces; teacher of Hokusai (documented: Wikipedia).
- **Isoda Koryūsai 磯田湖龍斎 (1735–c.1790)** – perhaps the most prolific 18th-century designer; *Hinagata wakana no hatsumoyō* (from 1776, co-published Tsutaya/Nishimuraya) (documented: Wikipedia "Ukiyo-e"; "Tsutaya Jūzaburō").
- **Kitao Shigemasa 北尾重政 (1739–1820)** – founder of the Kitao school; teacher of Santō Kyōden (Kitao Masanobu) (documented: Wikipedia; UTokyo).
- **Kitagawa Utamaro 喜多川歌麿 (c.1753–1806)** – *ōkubi-e* beauties of the 1790s for Tsutaya; kyōka picture books *Ehon mushi erami* (1788), *Shiohi no tsuto* (c.1789), *Momochidori* (c.1790–91); manacled in 1804 for a Hideyoshi print (documented: Wikipedia; History of Art "Utamaro's Nature Books").
- **Tōshūsai Sharaku 東洲斎写楽 (active 10 months, 1794–95)** – all published by Tsutaya; identity unknown (documented: Wikipedia; Samurai Archives).
- **Chōbunsai Eishi 鳥文斎栄之 (1756–1829)** – left service as painter to shōgun Ieharu for ukiyo-e (documented: Wikipedia).
- **Utagawa Toyoharu 歌川豊春** (perspective prints, 1770s) and **Utagawa Toyokuni I 歌川豊国 (1769–1825)** – founder and star of the Utagawa school, which dominated late Edo output; *Yakusha nigao haya geiko* (1817) (documented: Wikipedia; Lyon Collection).
- **Katsushika Hokusai 葛飾北斎 (1760–1849)** – Katsushika school; apprenticed to a wood-carver as a boy; *Hokusai manga* (from 1814), *Fugaku sanjūrokkei* (c.1830–33), *Fugaku hyakkei* (1834–35) (documented: Wikipedia; Sumida Hokusai Museum).
- **Utagawa Hiroshige 歌川広重 (1797–1858)** – *Tōkaidō gojūsan tsugi* (c.1833–34), *Meisho Edo hyakkei* (1856–58) (documented: Wikipedia; hiroshige.org.uk).
- **Keisai Eisen 渓斎英泉 (1790–1848)** – beauties, landscape, *Kisokaidō* with Hiroshige; his Eisen courtesan on the 1886 *Paris Illustré* cover was copied by Van Gogh (documented: Wikipedia; Agorha).
- **Utagawa Kunisada 歌川国貞 / Toyokuni III (1786–1865)** – the most productive actor-and-beauty designer of the century; earliest known print 1807 for Tsuruya (documented: Wikipedia; Lyon Collection).
- **Utagawa Kuniyoshi 歌川国芳 (1797–1861)** – *Suikoden* heroes (1827–30), *Seichū gishi den* (1847–48), satire and cats (documented: Wikipedia; Fujiokaya nikki).
- **Tsukioka Yoshitoshi 月岡芳年 (1839–1892)** – Kuniyoshi's pupil from 1850; *Tsuki hyakushi* (1885–92) (documented: Wikipedia; AGNSW).
- **Kawanabe Kyōsai 河鍋暁斎 (1831–1889)** – Kuniyoshi's pupil as a child, then Kanō-trained; arrested in 1870 after a caricature at a painters' gathering; changed 狂斎 to 暁斎 on release (documented: thehistoryofart.org; Wikipedia).

**Women.** **Katsushika Ōi 葛飾応為 (c.1800–c.1866; some sources "after 1857")** – Hokusai's daughter by his second wife; production assistant and painter in her own right; praised by Eisen as a professional artist; living alone in Aoyama by 1857 selling paintings (documented: Wikipedia; Samurai Archives; Japan Objects quoting Eisen). **Yamazaki Ryūjo 山崎龍女 (act. c.1716–36)** – daughter of a shogunal foot-soldier of Shitaya; painted beauties signed "Yamazaki-shi Joryū, aged fourteen"; her manner was called *Oryū-e*; the 1734 *Honchō seji dan ki* already records her (documented: Christie's lot essay; Ukiyo-e bunken shiryōkan compilation). Earliest known female ukiyo-e painter with dated works (probable).

### Carvers and printers who are named

Carvers were anonymous until the 19th century, when seals such as *hori Take* appear. Printers remain almost wholly anonymous – the Met refers to "Eijudō's anonymous printing masters" for the Great Wave (documented: Met).

- **Egawa Tomekichi 江川留吉 (fl. c.1830–50)** – master carver of Asakusa; credited in *Hokusai manga* vol. 12 (1834) and in the colophon of *Fugaku hyakkei*; ran a workshop of six named men including Egawa Sentarō (documented: Wikipedia "Egawa Tomekichi").
- **Yokokawa Takejirō 横川竹次郎 "Hori Take 彫竹" (fl. 1847–1866)** – cut for Hiroshige, Kuniyoshi, Toyokuni III and Kunichika for at least 25 publishers; ten different seals recorded by Horst Graebner (documented: Lyon Collection).
- **Kubo Shunman 窪俊満 (1757–1820)** – from a lacquerer family; ran the Shōsadō studio devoted to surimono printing (documented: De Gruyter chapter on surimono).

### Writers tied to the prints

- **Santō Kyōden 山東京伝 (1761–1816)** – also the designer Kitao Masanobu; manacled 50 days in 1791 (documented: Samurai Archives; UTokyo).
- **Jippensha Ikku 十返舎一九 (1765–1831)** and **Kyokutei Bakin 曲亭馬琴 (1767–1848)** – both began in Tsutaya's orbit; Bakin was apprenticed in Tsutaya's shop; Hokusai illustrated Bakin (documented: Suntory; UTokyo; Wikipedia).
- **Ōta Nanpo 大田南畝 / Yomo no Akara (1749–1823)** – the kyōka leader; recorded the 1773 *takara-awase* (documented: Suntory; De Gruyter).
- **Ishikawa Masamochi / Yadoya no Meshimori** – compiler of *Ehon mushi erami* (documented: History of Art).
- **Ryūtei Tanehiko 柳亭種彦 (1783–1842)** – author of *Inaka Genji*, died during the Tenpō interrogation (documented: Lyon Collection).
- **Shikitei Sanba 式亭三馬** – *Ukiyoburo* (1809) shows children swapping nishiki-e at a bathhouse (documented: Hoover).

### Actors and the houses

- **Ichikawa Danjūrō I 市川團十郎 (1660–1704)** – debut 1673 at the Nakamura-za as Sakata Kintoki with red-and-black *kumadori*, the origin of *aragoto*; stabbed in his dressing room in 1704 (documented: NTJ Kabuki A to Z; Kabuki21; Wikipedia). Wikipedia's lineage table gives "May 1675" as the start of his name-holding, not birth – noted.
- **Ichikawa Danjūrō VII (1791–1859)** – banished from Edo in 1842 for using real samurai armour on stage; ten years in exile, mostly in Osaka (documented: Viewing Japanese Prints "Sumptuary Edicts").
- The Yamamura-za was abolished after the Ejima–Ikushima affair of 1714, leaving three licensed houses (documented: Kabuki D-13).

### The Yoshiwara: people, not pictures

- Numbers: some 1,750 women in the 18th century, with records of up to 3,000; another source says "as many as 4,000" (contested range; Wikipedia "Yoshiwara"; world4.eu).
- Indenture: by 1626 contracts were limited to ten years; a typical contract c.1800, signed by the father, exchanged a lump sum – e.g. 25 ryō for five years – for the girl's service, certified she was not Christian and gave her temple registration (documented: Samurai Archives "Yoshiwara"). Contracts ran five to ten years but debts for food, clothing and medicine could extend them indefinitely (documented: Wikipedia; Grokipedia summarising the same). A 1641 edict forbade leaving without permission (probable: Grokipedia). Ranks: *tayū* (last Yoshiwara tayū retired 1761), *oiran*, *shinzō*, *kamuro* (documented: Samurai Archives).
- Named subjects: **Hanaōgi 花扇 of the Ōgiya 扇屋** – Utamaro's half-length portrait c.1793–94 (Met JP) praises her brush and poem; the *Kinsei shogadan* records her poetry and calligraphy; in 1794 she escaped the quarter to live with a man and was brought back (documented: Met; world4.eu after Kinsei shogadan). **Takao II (1640–1659)**, tayū (documented: Wikipedia). Utamaro's *Hanaōgi* kept her name on the censored second edition precisely because, as a courtesan, she was the only woman who could legally be named (documented: Viewing Japanese Prints).
- The Meiji "Prostitution Liberation Decree" of 1872 cancelled the women's debts; an estimated 90% left in the following months (probable: Japan Forward).

### Censors and reformers

- **Matsudaira Sadanobu 松平定信 (1758/59–1829)** – chief councillor 1787–93; Kansei reforms; publishing edicts of 1790 (documented: Viewing Japanese Prints; Wikipedia "Kansei Reforms").
- **Mizuno Tadakuni 水野忠邦 (1794–1851)** – architect of the Tenpō reforms 1841–43; ousted by the 9th month of 1843 (documented: osakaprints.com; Forrer).
- Named nanushi censors whose seals appear 1843–52 include Murata Sahei (who handled Kuniyoshi's *Seichū gishi den* approval), Fukushima, Watari, Muramatsu (documented: MIT "Nanushi Censor Seals"; Fujiokaya nikki).

### Collectors, dealers, exporters

- **Hayashi Tadamasa 林忠正 (1853–1906)** – came to Paris as an interpreter for the 1878 Exposition; shop at 7 Cité d'Hauteville from 1884; commissioner-general for Japan in 1900; estimated 166,000 prints and 9,708 illustrated books exported in 218 shipments over 11 years (Segi 1980 via Emery 2022); Koyama-Richard gives "more than 300,000 works" (contested count; Agorha; Persée; NMWA).
- **Siegfried Bing (1838–1905)** – Hamburg-born Paris dealer; *Le Japon Artistique* 1888–91; first comprehensive ukiyo-e exhibition in Europe at the École des Beaux-Arts, 1890, over 700 prints (documented: Wikipedia; Freer/Sackler biography).
- **Ernest Fenollosa (1853–1908)** – first curator of Japanese art at the MFA Boston; curated Japan's first ukiyo-e exhibition in Tokyo, 1898; quarrelled with Hayashi (documented: Wikipedia "Ukiyo-e"; Agorha).
- **Frank Lloyd Wright (1867–1959) as dealer** – first Japan trip 1905; Hiroshige exhibition at the Art Institute of Chicago 1906; 1913 agreement with William and John Spaulding for $20,000 that grew to $125,000 in five months; two Met sales in 1918 totalling $20,000; 1919–20 "revamped" print scandal (recut colour blocks over faded originals, pinholes) traced to dealer Hayashi Kyūgo and collector Takamizawa Enji, costing Wright about $30,000 in restitution; Bank of Wisconsin seized Taliesin in 1928 and sold thousands of prints at a dollar each (documented: Met Bulletin article; Hammer Museum; Viewing Japanese Prints "Takamizawa").

## 3. The schools and movements

- **Torii** (actor signboards and prints from c.1700; Kiyonaga), **Katsukawa** (Shunshō's realistic actors from the 1760s), **Utagawa** (Toyoharu → Toyokuni → Kunisada, Kuniyoshi, Hiroshige; the dominant late-Edo school in output and range), **Katsushika** (Hokusai and pupils Hokkei, Gakutei) (documented: Wikipedia "Ukiyo-e"; JAANUS "surimono").
- **Nishiki-e's dated beginning, 1765.** Picture-calendars (*egoyomi*) hid the long and short months of the lunar year in the design and were exchanged at New Year parties of poetry circles; the 1765 (Meiwa 2, *kinoto-tori*) burst – potentially over 100 designs, half as many in 1766 – was funded by two literati circles led by "Kyosen" (the bannerman Ōkubo Jinshirō Tadanobu) and "Sakei"; the Met's Harunobu *Parody of Minamoto no Tametomo* carries "Meiwa 2" on the neckband (documented: Hamatake Foundation after Waterhouse 2013; Met 54181; Viewing Japanese Prints "Poetry"). Blocks were later re-cut for commercial sale with the patron's name replaced by the artist's (documented: Wikipedia). Britannica attributes the technique to the carver Kinroku – a tradition, **contested**.
- **Genres**: *bijin-ga* (beauties), *yakusha-e* (actors), *meisho-e* (famous places), *musha-e* (warriors – exempt from the 1842 ban as morally uplifting), *surimono* (privately commissioned, exempt from sumptuary rules, on thick hōsho with embossing and metallics; earliest known kyōka surimono 1785; the Edo kyōka vogue ended in the 1830s), *shunga* (named here as a fact; excluded from view) (documented: osakaprints.com; JAANUS "surimono").
- **Kyōka circles**: the *takara-awase* gatherings of 1773 (Ekōji, Ushigome) and 1783 (Yanagibashi), recorded by Ōta Nanpo, seeded the still-life surimono; Tsutaya turned the circle into a commercial programme – the 1788 advertisement in *Ehon mushi erami* invited poets to submit verses for bird, animal and fish books (documented: De Gruyter; History of Art).
- **The shift to landscape**: the Tenpō edicts of 1842 banned actors and beauties; Edo designers turned to landscape, sumō and warriors, genres with existing traditions, while Osaka's actor-print industry stopped almost entirely until 1847 (documented: osakaprints.com; Forrer). Prussian blue made large skies and water printable from 1829–31 (documented: Met). The travel boom supplied the subject: Hiroshige's *Tōkaidō* (c.1833–34) and the guidebook *Tōkaidō meisho zue* (1797) belong to the same market (documented: Wikipedia; Forrer).

### The other makings of Edo (one or two names each)

- **Kimono / Nishijin 西陣**: Kyoto's weaving quarter took its name from the Western Camp of the Ōnin War (1467–77), when weavers returned from Sakai; patterned silks for court, warriors, Noh and temples on drawlooms; three Nishijin weavers went to Lyon in 1872 and the first Kyoto jacquards (1877) had 100–200 needles (documented: Okamoto Orimono; Kobayashi, TSA paper).
- **Edo komon 江戸小紋**: stencil-dyed micro-patterns on samurai *kamishimo*, each domain with its own reserved pattern; dye-houses grew along the Kanda river because sending cloth to Kyoto was too slow; the name "Edo komon" dates from 1955 when **Komiya Kōsuke 小宮康助** was designated a Living National Treasure; stencils are *Ise-katagami* (documented: Tokyo Metropolitan Government crafts page; kimono.or.jp; Highlighting Japan).
- **Lacquer**: the **Kajikawa 梶川** family, makers of inrō to the Tokugawa from the early 18th century, signing simply "Kajikawa" across generations; **Kajikawa Tokusaburō (1827–1865)**, 11th master (documented: V&A; Britannica; Tsubashi). **Ogata Kōrin 尾形光琳 (1658–1716)**: *Writing Box with Eight Bridges* in lead, silver, gold maki-e and shell (documented: Wikipedia; nippon.com).
- **Netsuke and inrō**: the 1781 *Sōken kishō* by Inaba Tsūryū (1736–1786) is the earliest known list of carvers; Kyoto founders **Tomotada**, **Masanao**, **Okatomo**; Osaka's **Yoshimura Shūzan (1700–1773)** (documented: Hermitage exhibition text).
- **Ceramics**: Arita – **Sakaida Kakiemon I 酒井田柿右衛門 (1596–1666)**, overglaze enamel; the house tradition claims a first overseas sale in 1646 (probable, family record); the VOC ordered 4,149 pieces in 1656 and 64,866 in 1659 (documented: Wikipedia "Japanese export porcelain"; turuta.jp). Kyō-yaki – **Nonomura Ninsei 野々村仁清** (Omuro kiln at Ninna-ji from the 1640s) and his pupil **Ogata Kenzan 尾形乾山 (1663–1743)** (documented: Wikipedia).
- **The sword**: **Yamaura Kiyomaro 山浦清麿 (1813–1854)**, "Yotsuya Masamune"; his patron Kubota Sugane set up a subscription scheme (*bukikō*) selling swords at 3 ryō each; died by his own hand in Kaei 7 (documented: Tōken Hakubutsukan caption; NihontoWatch).
- **Carpentry (daiku 大工) and kiwari 木割**: the *Shōmei* 匠明 (1608), five scrolls written by **Heinouchi Masanobu 平内政信 (d. 1645)** from his father Yoshimasa's instruction, fixing every timber as a ratio of the column diameter; the Heinouchi were the bakufu's hereditary master carpenters; oldest extant copy 1697–1727, University of Tokyo (documented: JAANUS "Shōmei"). About 500 *kiwari-sho* survive (documented: MDPI study).

## 4. The institutions and places

- **Edo's population**: over one million by the early 18th century (1721 is the usual benchmark), probably the largest city in the world; Osaka and Kyoto each about 400,000; Japan about 30 million at the 1721 survey and roughly 32 million at the Restoration (documented: Wikipedia "Edo period"; Brill "Population Trends"). Roughly half were samurai and half *chōnin*, yet commoner wards held only about 20% of the ground against 35% daimyō mansions and 35% other samurai districts (documented: Samurai Archives "Edo"). Enumerations counted only commoners; samurai numbers are estimates (Sekiyama 1957) (documented: Hitotsubashi RCESR paper). Edo lost half its million in under seven years at the Restoration, 300,000 leaving in 1868 alone (documented: H. D. Smith, Columbia).
- **Sankin-kōtai 参勤交代**: compulsory for all daimyō from the 1630s under Iemitsu; families held hostage in Edo, lords alternating yearly; it pushed the samurai population past 100,000 early and, Smith argues, is what lifted Edo from a half-million castle town to a million; its retinues created an itinerant market for souvenirs and prints, and the daimyō's need to distinguish their kamishimo is the origin story of Edo komon (documented: Samurai Archives; Smith; Christie's; Highlighting Japan).
- **Nihonbashi 日本橋**: the publishers' streets (Tōri Abura-chō, Bakuro-chō, Kodenma-chō) and the fish market (documented: Sumida Hokusai Museum).
- **Yoshiwara 吉原**: licensed in 1617, moved to Asakusa after the 1657 fire as Shin-Yoshiwara; by 1626 the last Sumi-chō brothels had moved in (documented: Samurai Archives). See section 2 for the human facts.
- **The three theatres and Saruwaka-chō**: in 1670 the shogunate licensed four houses – Nakamura-za (founded 1624 by Saruwaka Kanzaburō), Ichimura-za (1634), Morita-za (1660) and Yamamura-za (abolished 1714). After the 1841 fire the Tenpō government forbade rebuilding in Sakai-chō, Fukiya-chō and Kobiki-chō and ordered all three to Shōten-chō in Asakusa, renamed Saruwaka-chō, in 1841–43; the theatre town lasted to early Meiji, the Morita-za leaving for Shintomi-chō in 1872 (documented: NDL "Saruwaka-cho"; Kabuki D-13; Ichimura-za and Morita-za articles).
- **Dejima 出島 and the Dutch trade**: the VOC post moved from Hirado to Dejima in 1641; 606 Dutch ships arrived 1641–1847 – about 7 a year before 1671, 5 to 1715, then 2 a year, cut to 1 in 1790, back to 2 in 1799; peak year 43 ships in 1658 (documented: Wikipedia "Dejima"; Nagasaki City PDF). **In**: Chinese silk, cotton, sugar, deer hides, shark skin, books, scientific instruments – the seed of *rangaku* – and pigments including Prussian blue; **out**: copper above all, silver (peak 1661), camphor, porcelain, lacquerware, rice (documented: Wikipedia; Wereldmuseum Leiden; Nichibunken paper). An Imari shop opened on Dejima in 1662 (documented). Copperplate prints are not named on the pages opened (gap noted).
- **Terakoya 寺子屋 and literacy**: private schools teaching reading, writing and abacus to commoners' children. Counts are **contested**: Dore/Passin's figure of some 14,000 terakoya, 300 domain schools and 1,500 private academies by late Edo; the Tokyo Metropolitan Library says 30–40,000 by the end of the shogunate; nippon.com says over 60,000 villages had one. Literacy estimates of 40–50% of adult men (JICA; Wikipedia "History of education") rest on attendance rates and are challenged by Rubinger's *Popular Literacy* and the *Social History of Literacy in Japan* volume, which stress regional and class disparity (documented: Hmeljak Sangawa; JSTOR TOC).
- **The status order**: *shi-nō-kō-shō* (warrior, farmer, artisan, merchant), with actors ranked below merchants; samurai about 6% of the population, peasants 80%; the New Cambridge History reads it as a system of locally specific status groups governing through household registration, dismantled around 1868 (documented: Britannica; Wikipedia "Edo period"; Cambridge). The *chōnin* 町人 – merchants and artisans at the bottom – were the class that benefited most from Edo's growth and bought the prints (documented: Wikipedia "Ukiyo-e").
