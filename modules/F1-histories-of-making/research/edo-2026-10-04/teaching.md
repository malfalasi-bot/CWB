# How the best exhibitions, books, courses and interactives teach Edo print culture

Research note for the F1 "Histories of making" Edo case · 4 October 2026 · all facts below come from pages opened today (list at the end). Confidence words: *documented* (stated on the institution's own page or in a cited scholarly text), *probable* (stated by a secondary source or inferable), *contested* (both readings given).

## 1. Exhibitions and permanent galleries, read as teaching structures

### British Museum, London

**"Hokusai: beyond the Great Wave" (25 May–13 Aug 2017; Osaka, Abeno Harukas, Oct–Nov 2017).** Documented. Built on the AHRC project *Late Hokusai: Thought, Technique, Society* (2016–19) and deliberately **not** chronological: the catalogue (ed. Timothy Clark, Thames & Hudson/BM, 352 pp.) says earlier shows "presented his long career as a chronological sequence", and this one groups late works thematically. Catalogue order, mirroring the rooms: six essays (Roger S. Keyes on the final years; Clark "Late Hokusai, backwards"; Angus Lockyer on thought; Matsuba Ryōko on line; Asano Shūgō on old age; Alfred Haft on late Tokugawa society), then **Hokusai from twenty to sixty → Mt Fuji and the Great Wave → Worlds seen → Worlds imagined → Hokusai's world → Immortality**. Clark's blog: the show culminates in a room of paintings made at 88, 89 and 90, each sealed with the character "hundred" (百), framed by the 1834 postscript to *One Hundred Views of Mt Fuji* (trans. Henry D. Smith II) hoping for a "divine state" at 100. Anchors: *Under the Wave off Kanagawa* (BM 2008,3008.1), *Red Fuji*, *Tiger in the Snow* (1849, private collection, USA), *Head of an Old Man* (Leiden). The daughter 葛飾応為 Katsushika Ōi (Eijo, c.1800–after 1857) has her own strand. Haft's essay places Hokusai in a "commercialised art market"; the symposium volume *Late Hokusai: Society, Thought, Technique, Legacy* uses those four sections, with fakes "contaminating and supporting" the story. Process was handled in a parallel free Room 3 display, **"Japanese woodblock printing: a craft of precision"** (to 16 July 2017): Kunisada's triptych *Shokunin* (Artisans, 1857) showing cutter and printer workshops; a rare Hokusai hanshita-e; a modern carved block for *Fuji-view Moor in Owari*; Hiroshige's *Sudden Shower at Shōno* for bokashi. Haft's numbers: a standard colour print needed "four or five woodblocks cut on both sides"; a surimono ten or more; mountain cherry (yamazakura) is the wood. Film: "British Museum presents: Hokusai", the first UK film biography; its carving footage is reused in later blogs, with David Bull on camera (probable).

**"Hokusai: The Great Picture Book of Everything" (30 Sept 2021–30 Jan 2022).** Documented. 103 block-ready brush drawings (hanshita-e) for an unpublished encyclopaedia, last recorded at a Paris auction in 1948, bought 2020 with the Theresia Gerda Buch Bequest and Art Fund. The narrative hinge: the drawings survive **because the book was never cut** — a block cutter would have pasted each face down on cherry and cut through it. Sections: India/China/Korea, Buddhist origins, ancient Chinese inventions, real and imagined animals, "Southern Barbarians". Anchors: the Great Wave, the paulownia storage box, the mallard duck sketch that reappears in the 1847 scroll; a link to 178 Hokusai drawings in Boston pasted into publishers' blank volumes, the intended format. Teaching kit: large-print guide, a KS3–5 teachers' guide (PDF) and classroom image bank (PPTX); on-demand films including a curators' tour, Alfred Haft's picks, and Korenberg's "**The Great Wave: spot the difference**". ResearchSpace hosts all 103 drawings as open data.

**The BM's "spot the difference" research is itself a teaching structure.** Korenberg (Dept of Scientific Research) gathered photographs of **111** surviving Great Wave impressions (77 in public institutions) and re-tested Roger Keyes's **21 "states"** of keyblock wear; her video concludes there are **eight**. Diagnostics a learner can apply: three breaks in the title cartouche's double border; a break in the wave line behind the right-hand boat; a break near Fuji's summit; **two new blocks** in late impressions (light-blue sea, yellow boats); the last four known impressions with pink boats; and a tiny dark sliver in the foam present on every original and no facsimile, which David Bull identified as a splinter the cutter never removed. Numbers, all flagged as estimates: a Great Wave cost about two helpings of noodles; a publisher needed ~2,000 sales to profit; Kunisada designs ran 3,000–4,000; Hiroshige's Tōkaidō 12,000–15,000; up to ~8,000 Great Waves; 3,000 keyblock impressions in a day is recorded. Her blog on Hiroshige's *Plum Garden at Kameido* repeats the method (82 impressions, four states, cost-cutting between states) and shows Van Gogh owned a fourth-state impression but painted first-state colours.

### The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York

The Met teaches Edo prints mainly through the **Heilbrunn Timeline essays** (Department of Asian Art, 2003–04) and one scientific essay. *"Woodblock Prints in the Ukiyo-e Style"* (2003) runs: 8th-century Buddhist printing → 1765 full-colour calendar prints (nishiki-e) for Edo patrons → subjects (courtesans, actors, then landscapes, then late-19th-century history) → **"four experts: the designer, the engraver, the printer, and the publisher"**, the publisher "often also a bookseller" choosing theme and quality → process (cherry block, baren, mulberry paper, two alignment cuts, "up to twenty" colour blocks, runs "in the thousands"). Its slider runs Masanobu → Harunobu → Utamaro → Sharaku → Hokusai → Hiroshige → Sadahide (Yokohama foreigners, 1861) → Chikanobu (1887). *"Art of the Pleasure Quarters and the Ukiyo-e Style"* (2004) adds the social frame: four Confucian classes, merchants at the bottom; walled quarters set aside by the shogunate; the pun swapping "sorrowful" for "floating" (憂き世→浮世); kabuki's 1629 ban on women and 1652 on boys; the "golden age" in the last quarter of the 18th century; ukiyo-e "swept away" after 1868. *"Japonisme"* (Colta Ives, 2004) gives the Western reception as exchange: Whistler's tearoom, Monet's wrapping paper ("it is said"), Degas and Tissot as collectors, Cassatt's ten etchings after the 1890 École des Beaux-Arts show, Lautrec, Gauguin. **"The Great Wave: Anatomy of an Icon"** (Marco Leona, 2020) is the Met's process lesson: the publisher 永寿堂 Eijudō advertised *Thirty-six Views* for New Year 1831 "featuring an exotic pigment newly available", and spectroscopy shows the printers **mixed Prussian blue with indigo for outlines and double-printed pure Prussian blue over dark stripes** in the wave's hollow, so three colours sit at three heights in the paper; Leona's closing line makes Hokusai "only one of the artists involved". **"Kimono Style: The John C. Weber Collection"** (2022) ran across ten galleries: Noh/Kyōgen costume → Edo sumptuary control (merchant women breaking the rules "to be fashionable", shown via prints and fashion books) → firemen's coats → cotton via the kitamaesen route → meisen → Poiret's 1919 "Paris" coat cut from one 15-foot length. Objects a course can call by id: JP1847 (Great Wave), JP29a–e (Hokusai's Ōgiya New Year pentaptych), JP1527 (Utamaro *Hanaōgi*), JP1524 (Eishi *Takigawa*), JP1704 (Koryūsai), JP1746 (Toyokuni *Takigawa* with Komachi memento mori).

### The Art Institute of Chicago

Documented. The Clarence Buckingham Collection (the Great Wave bought from Yamanaka & Co., New York, Dec 1905; given 1925) anchors a collection Janice Katz puts at "about 16,000" prints, rotated **quarterly** in Gallery 107, which she calls the only US museum able to sustain that. Three Great Wave impressions, all later than the first state, one retaining the pink sky. Teaching shows: **"Connoisseurship of Japanese Prints"** (6 Apr–25 Jun 2019) hung all three Great Waves side by side with other multiples and asked visitors to find cartouche breaks and fading; **"Fantastic Landscapes: Hokusai and Hiroshige"** (2021), built around Berlin blue, with Katz's lecture explaining aizuri-e (first all-blue issue, colour added in later editions) and citing Henry Smith; **"Painting the Floating World: Ukiyo-e Masterpieces from the Weston Collection"** (4 Nov 2018–27 Jan 2019): ~160 paintings in **eight chronological rooms**, cases only eight inches deep, "dynamic maps of the city, educational videos, and digital tablets", a 350-page catalogue with essays on shunga and mitate-e, paired with "Two Floating Worlds: Japanese Prints and Paintings"; **"The Floating World Emerges"** (24 Jun–6 Sep 2026, ~40 early prints, Kiyotada's *Main Gate, New Yoshiwara* c.1745). Buckingham bought the Plum Garden from Frank Lloyd Wright. "10+ things to know about the Great Wave" is a listicle (title is a nickname; 46 prints not 36; indigo and Berlin blue; Shōhaku's screens as antecedent).

### Museum of Fine Arts, Boston

Documented. The MFA held the earliest known museum Hokusai show anywhere ("Hokusai and His School", 1892–93); Bigelow gave "more than 30,000 ukiyo-e prints". **"Hokusai" (5 Apr–9 Aug 2015)**, 230+ works, all MFA, in seven themes — *Urban Pleasures; Views of Mount Fuji; Waterfalls and Bridges; Ingenious Designs; Private Commissions; Nature Studies; Legend and Literature* — plus a **blockcarving section with a set of woodblocks and reproduction prints laying out the Great Wave step by step, two videos of carving and printing**, and a Saturday toolbox cart where visitors handled tools. **"Hokusai: Inspiration and Influence" (26 Mar–16 Jul 2023; Seattle after)**: 100+ Hokusai works with ~200 by teachers, students, rivals and admirers; sections on teachers/students (Katsukawa Shunshō, Ōi), surimono, origins of Japonisme, landscapes, nature studies, heroes and monsters, with a central **"Wave room"** (Cederquist, Lichtenstein's *Drowning Girl*, Warhol, a 50,000-piece Lego Wave). Curator Sarah E. Thompson to WBUR: "He was a commercial artist".

### Tokyo: the four museums

**すみだ北斎美術館 Sumida Hokusai Museum (opened 2016, architect 妹島和世 Sejima Kazuyo).** Documented. Because originals are shown no more than 30 days a year, the 4th-floor permanent room (formerly AURORA, now **"Education Room—Discover Hokusai—"**) uses full-size high-definition replicas and touch panels in **seven areas**: (1) *Sumida and Hokusai*, then six areas named by his art-names, from *The period of practice* to *The period of original paintings*, one major replica and its episode per period. Fixtures: a reconstruction of the lost 1845 votive painting *Susanoo no Mikoto Yakujin Taiji no Zu* (destroyed 1923, rebuilt from a photograph); a touch-screen explainer of the *Hokusai Manga*; nishiki-e on 4K monitors; **a video workshop of nishiki-e making**; and a life-size model of the studio Hokusai shared with Ōi at 84, built from a pupil's sketch. The Peter Morse and 楢崎宗重 Narazaki Muneshige collections underlie the holdings.

**太田記念美術館 Ōta Memorial Museum of Art, Harajuku (opened 13 Jan 1980).** Documented. ~14,000 works, ~12,000 collected by 五代太田清蔵 Ōta Seizō V (1893–1977), who began after seeing Hokusai bird-and-flower prints at the Art Institute of Chicago. **No permanent display; the whole hang changes monthly** by theme (recent: *Beauty of Mounting*, 40 of its 600+ paintings; a 210-work Kunichika retrospective; the Ōi *Yoshiwara kōshi-saki no zu* night-scene show, 6 Oct–6 Dec 2026). Its anniversary text gives the contrast in one line: prints are "the combined labor of painters, carvers and printers", paintings show the brush.

**東京国立博物館 Tokyo National Museum, Honkan Room 10 "The Art of Ukiyo-e".** Documented. Rotated roughly monthly; the wall text is a four-sentence narrative: economic growth → urban culture "passionate about trends, fashion, and entertainment" → subjects from celebrities to festivals and landscapes → technique from hand-painting to black-and-white blocks to full colour. Room 20 shows kosode with prints showing how they were worn.

**江戸東京博物館 Edo-Tokyo Museum, Ryōgoku (opened Mar 1993; closed Apr 2022; reopened 31 Mar 2026).** Documented, the museum's own permanent-exhibition page. The approach is **reconstruction at scale**. Entry on the 6th floor across the **northern half of 日本橋 Nihonbashi at full size** (original ~51 m × 8 m; 14 of 28 ken rebuilt from 1806 and 1829 records; zelkova piles, cypress deck; two bronze finials copied from survivors inscribed 1658). New since 2026: sky screens that cycle weather, season and time of day "in styles ranging from realistic imagery to ukiyo-e". Edo Zone E1–E10: *Edo Castle and the District Zones* (1/200 palace model; a daimyo mansion vs a chōnin block of equal footprint); *Life of the Townspeople* (life-size nagaya units of 13.2 m²); **E3 *Publication and Information*** — "the process of production and marketing of books and woodblock prints", a Front Street, and the façade of the jihon shop Kansendō of publisher 和泉屋市兵衛 Izumiya Ichibei from *Tōkaidō meisho zue* (1797), who published kibyōshi from the Tenmei era and Toyokuni prints in Kansei; *Commerce* (Mitsui Echigoya storefront, a 1,500-koku cargo ship); *Villages and Islands*; *Four Seasons and Entertainment Districts* (Ryōgoku Nishizume diorama, ~1,500 figures from Tenpō-reform police records); *Cultural City Edo*, whose panel says Japan "is often viewed as being 'closed'" yet exchange with Holland, China and Korea "continued to flourish"; **E8 *Aesthetics of Edo*** with "The World of Ukiyo-e" (birth, development, artists, works that spread abroad); **E9 *Theaters and Pleasure Quarters*** — the **中村座 Nakamura-za façade at full scale, 11 ken (~20 m) wide, 3 ken deep**, dressed for the kaomise of the 11th month of 1805 with billboards by Torii Kiyomitsu IX, a *Sukeroku* tableau, and the *Yotsuya kaidan* stage-trick model with mirrors behind so visitors see the mechanism. The 2026 renewal opened the theatre interior to a walk-through, added a tactile model for blind visitors, and a reconstructed ukiyo-e print shop (Wikipedia caption; probable). "The Two Faces of Yoshiwara" and "the lives of the courtesans" is the museum's own wording for the exploitation reading. ~350,000 items; ~2,500 on display; QR guidance in 13 languages.

### Honolulu Museum of Art

Documented. "Third largest collection of Japanese woodblock prints in the United States", 12,400+ works; the James A. Michener Collection (given 1950–91; the parking-ticket-at-the-Met story in the 1999 Star-Bulletin is anecdote). Great Wave accession 13695 (Gift of Michener, 1955). Teaching format: **"Hokusai's Mount Fuji" (Oct 2020–3 Oct 2021)** showed **seventeen prints one at a time, each for two weeks**, with panels on Hokusai's career, Fuji's mythology and landscape print history — a "print of the fortnight" structure a course can imitate.

### V&A, London

Documented. 25,000+ prints and related material; the article names the **"ukiyo-e quartet"** (publisher "who usually had overall control", designer, block cutter, printer) and teaches the **key-block proof**: black proofs with registration marks from which colour blocks are cut; the V&A holds copyists' drawings and proofs for prints never produced (Harunobu, E.1175-1912). Great Wave E.4823-1916; caption: *Thirty-six Views* was "the first to exist exclusively of large-format prints of landscapes". Toshiba Gallery rethought 2015.

### Also useful as structures
- **Asian Art Museum, San Francisco, "Seduction: Japan's Floating World" (2015, Weber Collection)**: Part I built around Moronobu's 58-foot *Visit to the Yoshiwara* handscroll (fifteen episodes from the gate to an ageya party), paired with porcelain, yogi bedcovers, costume and printed Yoshiwara guides; Part II on tayū/oiran celebrity; shown with "The Printer's Eye" (88 Grabhorn prints chosen for printing quality). Catalogue intro: 987 women in 1642; ~3,000 by mid-1700s.
- **Cleveland Museum of Art, "Japan's Floating World 日本の浮世" (2022)**: its text says ukiyo-e "eschewed the grim realities of sex work, instead marketing beauty, celebrity, pleasure, and fashion"; closed with a feminist work by Oda Mayumi (b. 1941).

## 2. Books, syllabi and databases as structures

**Timothy Clark (ed.), *Hokusai: beyond the Great Wave* (2017)** — structure above: thematic late-career grouping, six essays, catalogue in six parts ending in *Immortality*. Companion: Clark (ed.), *Late Hokusai: Society, Thought, Technique, Legacy* (BM research publication), sections *society / thought / technique / legacy*.

**Andreas Marks, *Japanese Woodblock Prints: Artists, Publishers and Masterworks 1680–1900* (Tuttle, 2010, 336 pp., 500+ illustrations).** Documented via reviews/catalogues: a long introduction on history and production, then **fifty artist entries (Kiyonobu to Kokunimasa) and forty-nine publisher entries**, each publisher with a list of prints and artists; the review's summary of the thesis: publishers "owned the woodblocks and copyrights" and at one point served as censors. This is the only general survey organised so that a learner can look up a publisher as an entity.

**Julie Nelson Davis, *Partners in Print: Artistic Collaboration and the Ukiyo-e Market* (UH Press, 2015).** Documented. Four case studies dated 1776–1790, each a dyad and an object: teacher–students (鳥山石燕 Toriyama Sekien with Utamaro and Sekichūjo, a surimono c.1786); two painter-designers and their publisher (*Mirror of Yoshiwara Beauties*, 蔦屋重三郎 Tsutaya Jūzaburō); designer–publisher (the erotic *Scroll of the Sleeve*, attributed to 鳥居清長 Torii Kiyonaga); writer–illustrator (山東京伝 Santō Kyōden and 北尾政美 Kitao Masayoshi, 1790). The introduction widens Volker's 1949 "quartet" to copyists, paper preparers, wood and pigment suppliers, lenders and shopkeepers, and notes the Tokugawa edict requiring designers', writers' and publishers' names on printed matter — carvers and printers rarely named. **Davis, *Picturing the Floating World* (UH Press, 2021)**: four chronological chapters (*Inventing the Pictures of the Floating World; Creating the Brocade Pictures of the East; Making Famous Names; Expanding Horizons*) plus a Meiji epilogue; its target is the myth that ukiyo-e was unvalued "and even used as packing material", countered with period documents of appraisal and collecting. A reviewer notes it still follows the old primitive/polychrome/golden-age/decadent periodisation.

**Christine Guth, *Hokusai's Great Wave: Biography of a Global Icon* (UH Press, 2015).** Chapters: *"Under the Wave off Kanagawa" → International Nationalism → America's Japan → Lifestyle Branding → Placemaking → Epilogue: After the Tsunami.* One image followed from Edo reception through Meiji export nationalism, US perceptions, branding and the 2011 tsunami.

**Timon Screech, *Tokyo Before Tokyo* (Reaktion, 2020; Yamagata Bantō Prize 2022).** Six chapters as "vignettes" rather than chronology: geomancy and the ideal city (ki flowing NE→SW; fudai daimyō NE, tozama SW), Edo Castle interior, sacred spaces, the great bridge, pleasure districts, the city in literature, an Edo–Meiji epilogue.

**Henry D. Smith II, "Hokusai and the Blue Revolution in Edo Prints" (in Carpenter ed., *Hokusai and His Age*, Hotei 2005, pp. 234–69; PDF on his Columbia site).** The article for colour-as-economy: the single source *Masaki no kazura* by the bookseller Seisōdō Tōho says Berlin blue (bero) was first tried on surimono in 1829, then a fan-maker put out a 渓斎英泉 Keisai Eisen all-blue fan, then 西村屋与八 Nishimuraya Yohachi of Bakuro-chō published Hokusai's *Thirty-six Views* in bero, "a great fad, double that of the fans". Smith corroborates with the Brooklyn Museum Eisen fan (censor seal 1829) and Nagasaki import records showing volume rising and price falling 1824–28; ten Fuji prints were issued as aizuri first.

**Ellis Tinios, *Japanese Prints: Ukiyo-e in Edo 1700–1900* (BM Press, 2010).** Chapters: *Reception in the West 1860s–1910s → The production of woodblock prints → Censorship → Actor prints → Beauties → Landscape → Warrior prints → Meiji → outline history.* Premise: prints were "products of a highly commercialised and competitive publishing industry", explained as first buyers "would have read them".

**Rebecca Salter, *Japanese Woodblock Printing* (A&C Black 2001 / UH Press 2002).** A maker's manual: history, wood (yamazakura and substitutes, cut along the grain), kōzo paper, pigments, the three quality determinants (dampened washi, kentō registration, baren), making a baren, transferring, carving, printing; water-based ink throughout.

**Smarthistory / Khan Academy.** Leila Anne Harris, "Katsushika Hokusai, *Under the Wave off Kanagawa*" (2015; also an A-level variant; reused by Lumen and LibreTexts). A short essay (c. 800 words, probable) in five moves: the image's "visual play" (spray as snow, diagonal to the peak); series facts (1830–33, ~10 × 14 in., Met JP1847); Hokusai, Dutch prints and "a Japanese variant of linear perspective", low horizon, Prussian blue; Mount Fuji, the "Fuji cult", travel and souvenirs; the subject shift from courtesans to fishermen. Questions implied: where is Fuji; who bought this; what is Western in it.

**University structures.** MITx/HarvardX **"Visualizing Japan (1850s–1930s)"** (Dower, Gordon, Miyagawa, Weisenfeld): three modules — *Black Ships & Samurai; Hibiya Riot 1905; Shiseido* — 3–5 hours a week; the Visualizing Cultures curriculum teaches "reading" prints as documents (American oil vs Japanese woodblock of Perry's ship side by side, the 30-foot Black Ship Scroll, a daimyō role-play). Its sequel **VTx** opens with "Nihonbashi, a bridge through time" and runs Hiroshige's *100 Views* → Kiyochika → Koizumi → *100 Views of New Tokyo*. **Rikkyo "Japanese Arts A" (2026)**: 14 weeks — beginnings of ukiyo-e; ukiyo-e as popular culture; kabuki; Yoshiwara; women artists; yōkai; yūrei; art and disasters; Japan and the changing world. **Dokkyo "Japanese Art History" (2026)**: schools-first (Tosa, Kanō, Rinpa, Literati, Maruyama-Shijō), ukiyo-e in weeks 12–13, a museum visit. Penn: Davis teaches *Early Modern Japanese Arts and the City of Edo* and a seminar *Ukiyo-e: Beyond the Great Wave*. No Open University Edo module was found.

**Databases.** **Ukiyo-e.org (John Resig, Dec 2012)**: 220,000+ prints from 24+ institutions, text search across inconsistent metadata, **image-upload search**, and automatic clustering of similar impressions so a learner sees every museum's copy of one design side by side — the tool Korenberg used (56 hits, 25 original). **Smithsonian Libraries**: Freer's *Hokusai Manga* (bought 23 Feb 1901 from James Tregaskis for twelve guineas, 14 vols, missing vol. 15) digitised as two-page spreads; the Pulverer set (acquired 2003) is complete. **The Met Libraries** host *Denshin kaishu Hokusai manga*, vols 1–15, publisher 永楽屋東四郎 Eirakuya Tōshirō, Nagoya. The Freer page: the first volume (1814) sorts sketches by category; fourteen more followed, some posthumous; the Hokusai catalogue lists 260+ book titles.

## 4. What is argued: the readings a course must present both ways

**Hokusai the genius vs the system.** *Genius:* the BM touring text calls his work "intensely individual, subjective, energised and sublime"; the 2017 show is framed by his "divine state at 100" postscript. *System:* Leona shows Eijudō's unnamed printers invented the double-printed blue; Thompson: "He was a commercial artist"; Davis reframes authorship as publisher-led networks and shows the law named designer, writer and publisher but not carver or printer; Marks makes publishers look-up entries; Smith shows the Fuji series rode a fan-maker's blue fad. Contested; both documented.

**The Great Wave as "Japanese" vs a product of Prussian blue and Western perspective.** Documented: the pigment is a European synthetic (1706) imported through Nagasaki by Dutch and Chinese ships, cheaper after 1824–28 (Smith); Smarthistory frames the low horizon and recession as learned from Dutch prints; the MFA 2015 "perspective prints" section shows the uki-e peep-box tradition. Counter-reading: Smith argues the blue "articulated the changing worldview" of Edo popular culture, a local choice; Guth shows the image was later made "Japanese" by Meiji export policy and by foreign reception. Contested.

**Ukiyo-e as art vs commercial product.** Davis (2021) rejects the "packing material" myth with period appraisal documents; Cleveland's text and the BM's noodle price keep the commodity reading; the AIC Weston show argues paintings were commissioned and unique while prints "were widely circulated" — both at once, by medium. Monet's wrapping-paper discovery (Met: "it is said") should be labelled legend.

**The Yoshiwara as culture vs exploitation.** *Culture:* Cecilia Segawa Seigle, *Yoshiwara: The Glittering World of the Japanese Courtesan* (UH Press 1993), treats early Yoshiwara as "primarily a place of entertainment and socializing" (as quoted by SamuraiWiki); the Asian Art Museum's "Seduction" and the Met's Utamaro/Eishi labels teach fashion, poetry and celebrity. *Exploitation:* Amy Stanley, *Selling Women* (UC Press 2012), reads the indenture contract (c.1800: a lump sum such as 25 ryō for five years, signed by the father; the brothel may re-sell, marry off or bury the woman; the family liable for her debts), the "filial daughter" ideology that legitimised it, and brothel owners who "offered to contain" male disorder "in exchange for a monopoly"; the Edo-Tokyo Museum's own title is "The Two Faces of Yoshiwara"; Cleveland says ukiyo-e "eschewed the grim realities of sex work". Content note required; shunga named as a fact (AIC catalogue essay; V&A scope), not shown.

**Japonisme as exchange vs appropriation.** *Exchange:* the Met essay (Ives), the Van Gogh Museum, the MFA 2023 "origins of Japonisme" section. *Appropriation:* the 2015 MFA "Kimono Wednesdays" protests (Decolonize Our Museums; signs "THIS IS RACIST. THIS IS APPROPRIATION. THIS IS ORIENTALISM"; the museum stopped the try-on, kept the kimono for touch); counter-protesters, many Japanese and Japanese-American, and the deputy consul general defended it; Kei Hiruta (Oxford) argued Orientalism's wrongness was assumed, not shown; Carriger (*Theatre Research International* 2018) proposes "inappropriation" beyond the binary and notes kimono export was also Japanese soft power from the world fairs. Present all three positions.

**"Sakoku" as closure vs managed trade.** Documented: 鎖国 was coined in 1801 by Nagasaki interpreter 志筑忠雄 Shizuki Tadao translating Kaempfer; no 1630s edict uses it; it spread in Meiji and entered textbooks in the early 20th century. Since 荒野泰典 Arano Yasunori's 1978 paper and Ronald Toby's *State and Diplomacy in Early Modern Japan* (1984), historians speak of **kaikin (海禁)** and the **"four mouths" (四つの口)**: Nagasaki (Dutch, Chinese), Tsushima (Korea; twelve embassies 1607–1811), Satsuma (Ryūkyū), Matsumae (Ainu). Counter-current: Fujita Satoru argues the late 18th century did harden into "seclusion as ancestral law"; and the BM's own 2021 page still says "virtual lockdown for 220 years" — a museum text a course can critique. The Edo-Tokyo Museum's E7 panel already states the managed-exchange reading.

**Does manga descend from Hokusai's *Manga*?** *Yes-lineage:* the word 漫画 is Hokusai's (1814), Japanese schooling teaches manga as an ancient tradition, the AIC Weston press release traces "a thread of influence" to anime and manga, and the BM Manga catalogue (Rousmaniere 2019) rehearses Chōjū-giga and Edo roots. *No-lineage:* Eike Exner, *Comics and the Origins of Manga: A Revisionist History* (Rutgers 2021, Eisner Award) documents that "manga" meant sketches à la Hokusai until 1891, picture-stories after, and became "audiovisual" (speech balloons, speed lines) only with the *Asahi Graph*'s serialisation of McManus's *Bringing Up Father* from 1923; his epilogue is "The Myth of Manga as a 'Traditional Mode of Expression'"; reviewers note he records reverse flow too (ukiyo-e influencing McManus). The course should say the *name* descends from Hokusai; the *form* is disputed.

## 5. Design implications in one paragraph

The strongest teaching units share four moves: (1) **a reconstruction at scale** (Nihonbashi, Nakamura-za, Hokusai's studio, Kansendō's shopfront) that puts the learner in the market before the picture; (2) **the publisher named first** (Nishimuraya Yohachi/Eijudō, Tsutaya Jūzaburō, Izumiya Ichibei), as Marks and Davis insist; (3) **the same design shown in multiple impressions** (AIC's three Great Waves, Korenberg's 111, Ukiyo-e.org's clusters), so "the" print is seen as an edition with states, fading and cost-cutting; (4) **process as a block set with progressive proofs** (MFA 2015, Adachi, Bull), with numbers given as ranges and disagreements shown. Content notes belong at the Yoshiwara and shunga thresholds, as Cleveland and the Edo-Tokyo Museum model.
