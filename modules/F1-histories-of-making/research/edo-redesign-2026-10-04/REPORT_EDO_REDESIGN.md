# Rebuild the Edo unit around one bet

The Edo prototype fails not because it lacks research but because it never turns that research into a story with a stage: nineteen of its thirty-three beats sit on the same static 1859 raster, the map earns its half of the screen in roughly a third of the piece, the timeline is a ruler with 3 px knobs, eight bolted-on widgets fight the scroll driver and overwrite the map, five learner-facing captions apologise for stand-ins, and 97 sentences break the programme's own 25-word rule ([critique](/home/claude/research_notes/Edo%20unit%20design%20and%20content%20redesign/critique_current_prototype.md)). The rebuild should do four things in order. First, rewrite the narrative as five acts on the publisher's gamble (Tsutaya, Eijudō, Fujiokaya/Uoya, Hayashi–Bing–Wright, the Bank of Japan), each act ending on a question the next answers, with the primary sources quoted and the carvers and printers named. Second, replace the dot map and raster swap with a three-scale vector atlas (Edo from georeferenced 1677/1859 maps and CODH's CC BY kiriezu polygons; Japan from the open highway datasets; a Pacific-centred world in d3) and a timeline that charts the trade in lanes, lifespans, censor bands and sourced prices. Third, put one state object in charge of both instruments, reduce eight tools to five rebuilt to the case specification plus the three the case designed and the prototype dropped, and give every print deep zoom. Fourth, industrialise: beats as Zod-validated YAML, assets fetched and credited by CI, narration keyed to text hashes, Playwright/axe/Lighthouse gates, all on a BSD/MIT/Apache stack costing nothing. The award evidence says this is what winners do (one pinned stage, short steps, visible research, a small named cast), and the learning evidence says why it works (segmenting d≈0.70, contiguity d≈0.79–1.30, prediction before reveal, one-tap retrieval after). The honest effort estimate is **250–400 person-hours over 10–12 weeks for this first, template-making unit**, falling to **40–65 hours per unit** once the schema, scene registry and pipeline exist; both figures are inferences from comparable teams, not measured data.

## The prototype is a sourced essay beside a slideshow of dots

The critique's one-paragraph verdict is exact: the research underneath is award-grade and the voice is mostly right, but the piece is "a well-sourced essay with a slideshow of dots beside it" ([critique](/home/claude/research_notes/Edo%20unit%20design%20and%20content%20redesign/critique_current_prototype.md)). The numbers behind that verdict matter because they are the acceptance criteria for the rebuild. The narrative runs **5,627 words across 33 beats**, about 25 minutes' reading against a case document that plans eight lectures of six to ten stations each, roughly 62 minutes ([case L181](/home/claude/creative-world/modules/F1-histories-of-making/research/edo-2026-10-04/EDO_FULL_CASE.md)). Every one of the 33 beats contains at least one sentence over 25 words; **97 sentences exceed 25 words**, the longest 69; 29 of 33 beats average above 20 words per sentence against a voice guide that asks for 15–20 ([voice guide L100](/home/claude/creative-world/docs/02-program/voice-guide/index.md)). Only one phrase from a primary source is quoted verbatim in the whole piece, Eijudō's advertisement "in the new blue" ([data.js L227](/home/claude/creative-world/modules/F1-histories-of-making/build/prototypes/edo-story/data.js)); the 1842 edict, the Fujiokaya diary and the 1847 magistrate's report are all paraphrased away. The self-referential "the course" appears eleven times ("the course does not guess", "the course does not rule"), where the voice guide's house pronoun is "we" ([voice guide L101](/home/claude/creative-world/docs/02-program/voice-guide/index.md)).

The thread is an index, not a plot. The case poses the unit's essential question, "How does a city turn pictures into a mass market, and who gets named when it does?" ([case L11](/home/claude/creative-world/modules/F1-histories-of-making/research/edo-2026-10-04/EDO_FULL_CASE.md)); the prototype never asks it and instead lists its contents in the prologue ([data.js L129](/home/claude/creative-world/modules/F1-histories-of-making/build/prototypes/edo-story/data.js)). Protagonists are dropped after one chapter: Tsutaya dies at beat 3-4 and never returns; Hokusai's 1849 death is neither a beat nor a timeline event. The cast of eighteen has **no carver, no printer, one publisher and no woman**, although the case names Egawa Tomekichi, Yokokawa Takejirō and Kubo Shunman and puts Katsushika Ōi in the cast ([case L55–71](/home/claude/creative-world/modules/F1-histories-of-making/research/edo-2026-10-04/EDO_FULL_CASE.md)); an image of Ōi's *Night Scene in the Yoshiwara* is shipped and never displayed. The timeline year runs backwards inside chapters three times and the `EVENTS` array itself is out of order ([data.js L102–103](/home/claude/creative-world/modules/F1-histories-of-making/build/prototypes/edo-story/data.js)). The one narrative loop that works, learning to read a censor's seal at beat 3-5 and using it to date a sheet at beat 5-2, shows what the rest should do.

The instruments do almost no work. Beat views tabulate as **city 19, japan 3, world 3, kanto 3, export 2** and one each of blue, tokaido, pacific and europe; the city view is a single 1400×1252 JPEG with ten pins set "by eye" ([data.js L41–53](/home/claude/creative-world/modules/F1-histories-of-making/build/prototypes/edo-story/data.js)). The 1859 map is used for the fire of 1657. The Kanto view renders as a featureless blob on a 50 m coastline, the Tōkaidō is a straight polyline that detours through Mount Fuji, which is not a post station, and Perry's squadron crosses a Pacific it never crossed. The critique's beat-by-beat coupling grade finds the map genuinely working in about six beats, partly in five, and decorative in **22 of 33**. Consecutive beats repeat identical scenes four times, which the scrollytelling design literature names as the cardinal error: "If three consecutive steps leave the figure unchanged, the reader is scrolling for nothing" ([scrollytelling.ai](https://scrollytelling.ai/scrollytelling-design-patterns/)). The timeline carries 33 events of which at most three light at any moment, era labels that overlap at every width ("Golden a[Blue]e" on mobile), no lanes although every event has a `kind`, no lifespans although every person has dates, and none of the four censor regimes that its own margin check asks about ([app.js L323](/home/claude/creative-world/modules/F1-histories-of-making/build/prototypes/edo-story/app.js)).

The tools are where the piece is "complicated in the wrong place". The case designed ten interactives; the prototype built eight, dropped the three that carry most meaning (I2 Peel the colours, I4 the city 1677↔1859, I10 the contract) and added one that is in no specification (the five-box "chain" flowchart with counters) ([case L221–239](/home/claude/creative-world/modules/F1-histories-of-making/research/edo-2026-10-04/EDO_FULL_CASE.md)). Every tool is reduced: the margin tool has one sheet where the case asks for three, with hotspots positioned as percentages of a grid cell taller than the image so two marks float below the print; the price tool draws seven bars of equal visual authority of which one has "no sourced figure", one is "interpolated" and two are "probable" ([app.js L381–391](/home/claude/creative-world/modules/F1-histories-of-making/build/prototypes/edo-story/app.js)); the ban tool stamps "FORBIDDEN · 1842" in English on a 1783 Kiyonaga that is a stand-in for the actor genre; the edition tool is a number counter with no image. Four tools autoplay for 4–5 seconds with no pause control. Worse, there is a structural bug: `activate()` schedules a tool's `_enter` 900 ms later with no stored handle, so a reader who passes the beat in under 0.9 s leaves an orphaned interval that keeps calling `Map.arcsOnly` and overwrites whatever scene is current. The audit reproduced it: at beat 4-5 the Kanto view held the six blue-route cities and three arcs instead of Edo, Kanagawa and Fuji ([app.js L460–463](/home/claude/creative-world/modules/F1-histories-of-making/build/prototypes/edo-story/app.js)).

The visual design is competent and placeless. A serif-display editorial template in a cream column sits beside a navy dashboard pane; the cream raster fades into the dark pane nineteen times per read; the seal red is spent on every UI accent (points, routes, pins, sliders, the check icon, the stamp) so it can mean nothing when the censor's seal arrives; every portrait is cropped to the same 96×120 box, which decapitates the seated memorial portraits of Hiroshige and Kuniyoshi, and the same portrait appears three times in a beat and its end grid; the `wide` flag on the Great Wave has no CSS rule, so the most famous print in the world renders at column width like everything else ([index.html L103, L110](/home/claude/creative-world/modules/F1-histories-of-making/build/prototypes/edo-story/index.html)). Document height is **42,638 px at 1440×900, about 47 viewports**, with identical beat anatomy throughout. On a 390 px phone the chapter chips and the Explore button overflow off the right edge and the timeline labels clip to "00" and "200". Zero occurrences of `prefers-reduced-motion`, no `<h1>`, a map SVG whose twenty places are invisible to assistive technology, timeline events as unfocusable `<g>` elements, verdicts with no live region, and right/wrong conveyed only by colour and strikethrough round out the accessibility picture. Payload after a full scroll is **10.44 MB, 9.27 MB of it images**, with no `srcset`, no AVIF/WebP and a 280 KB full d3 bundle plus a 756 KB world TopoJSON. What reads as lazy, in the critique's order, is the apology captions, the repeated portraits, the English rubber stamp, dots on a 50 m coastline, the raster flashing into navy, 47 identical screens, the `wide` figure that is not wide, and the kanji-in-a-box flowchart.

One gap in the diagnosis should be stated plainly: there is no reader data. No analytics, no session recordings, no test with learners exist for this build, so the 42,638 px height and the 22 decorative beats are proxies for drop-off, not measurements of it. The rebuild plan below therefore ends with a small read-aloud test, because without it the second prototype will be judged by the same instincts as the first.

## Award-level work pins one stage, cuts hard and shows its research

The award set from 2015–2026 divides into four families a small team can learn from: newsroom spatial reconstructions (NYT Tulsa and Maui, Washington Post/Pudding Minneapolis, Reuters Hong Kong, SCMP's Forbidden City), museum narrative microsites (Getty's Persepolis Reimagined, the Museum of the American Revolution's Finding Freedom, the British Museum's Museum of the World, SBS's The Boat), open scholarly atlases (American Panorama, Histography, Allmaps, Knight Lab), and Japan-specific map and print projects that are directly reusable (Rekichizu, CODH Edo Maps, the Ukiyo-e Map) ([benchmarks](/home/claude/research_notes/Edo%20unit%20design%20and%20content%20redesign/benchmarks_award_level.md)). What judges praised is remarkably consistent. The Online Journalism Awards jury on the Tulsa piece: "Borrowing elements from video games, this immersive feature encourages you not merely to consume the story but to interact with it", and its structure, a street-level tour of 70-plus businesses introducing "the movers and shakers on the block" by name and trade, then an aerial of 35 blocks, is a scale break announced as its own step ([OJA entry](https://awards.journalists.org/entries/what-the-1921-tulsa-race-massacre-destroyed/)). The Peabody citation for Maui praised "more than 400 videos ... placed with pixel-perfect precision on a photorealistic 3-D map of the town, underpinning a gripping narrative", an hour-by-hour time spine over a single map ([Peabody](https://peabodyawards.com/award-profile/inside-the-deadly-maui-inferno-hour-by-hour/)). The OJA jury on the Bronx fire piece rewarded "deeply reported yet concise investigative storytelling", concision alongside spectacle ([OJA](https://awards.journalists.org/entries/the-chain-of-failures-that-left-17-dead-in-a-bronx-apartment-fire/)).

Three exemplars map almost directly onto the Edo unit's needs. Finding Freedom (Webby People's Voice 2026, MUSE bronze 2021, Anthem silver 2022, about 1.5 million pageviews) tells "the lives and decisions of five real people" through "research-based first-person narratives" built from pension records, newspaper advertisements and period maps, in one watercolour idiom, with audio voiced by actors and Section 508 compliance added in 2025 ([MoAR](https://www.amrevmuseum.org/press-releases/museum-of-the-american-revolution-wins-webby-award-for-finding-freedom-online-experience)). That is the cast-of-five model the Edo unit should adopt for its publishers. Persepolis Reimagined (FWA of the Year, Webby winner, Awwwards Developer Site of the Year 2022) chose "a fixed camera path for this project to optimize the experience", applied the same vignette, bloom and grain to every scene "to blend them together", agreed a material style sheet with Getty curators and a named professor, and briefed its sound to "enhance, not to overrule" ([Awwwards case study](https://www.awwwards.com/case-study-getty-persepolis-reimagined.html)). That is the single-grade, deterministic-choreography discipline the prototype's two-product look lacks. The British Museum's Museum of the World maps objects "to a timeline" split "into 5 geographic tracks, further identified by color" ([Internet Archaeology](https://intarch.ac.uk/journal/issue44/13/index.html)), the device the Edo timeline needs for "Japan versus the rest of the world at the same moment", with the caution that its desktop-only WebGL is now a liability.

The Japan-specific projects are the most reusable assets found. Rekichizu is a "modern-style historical web map" of Japan c. 1800–1840 drawn in the Google Maps idiom, built essentially by one graphic designer in QGIS and rendered in MapLibre, which won the AMD Award '23 newcomer prize and a 2024 Digital Archive Industry encouragement award ([chizutodesign](https://chizutodesign.com/rekichizu)); its sibling Edo-period route search puts Edo→Kyoto at 14 days by the Tōkaidō, and its hosting on Cloudflare Workers cost **US$5.50** after 200,000 requests in a day and a half ([chizutodesign note](https://note.com/chizutodesign/n/neee5116b95b4)). Its designer tried a washi paper texture and rejected it because it looked like a degraded LCD, a caution the "ink on paper" visual direction below should respect. CODH's Edo Maps extracted **8,788 place names from the 29 NDL Owariya kiriezu sheets** via the IIIF Curation Platform ([CODH](https://codh.rois.ac.jp/edo-maps)), and the Ukiyo-e Map plots over 200 Hiroshige prints on OpenStreetMap, with every print of the *Hundred Famous Views of Edo* geocoded on Wikipedia with KML/GPX export ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Hundred_Famous_Views_of_Edo)). Google's own ukiyo-e work, the Brooklyn Museum's *Hundred Views* story, "Zoom Into 'The Great Wave'" and the British Museum's "spot the difference" between impressions ([Google Arts & Culture](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/zoom-into-the-great-wave/BwWxIlGd8SIdLg?hl=en); [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/exhibitions/hokusai-great-picture-book-everything)) supply the deep-zoom and compare-impressions devices.

On the question that matters most for the prototype, how to couple a map and a timeline to narrative without losing the thread, every exemplar holds exactly **one instrument fixed on screen** while short text steps change its state; none found keeps two full instruments live at once ([benchmarks](/home/claude/research_notes/Edo%20unit%20design%20and%20content%20redesign/benchmarks_award_level.md)). Time is either a single ordered spine or a scrubber; space is traversed on a pre-authored camera path; scale breaks are their own beat; and "A story that cannot be read backwards has a bug, not a style" ([vocab.design](https://vocab.design/scrollytelling)). Makers rarely publish type scales or millisecond timings, but they publish operating rules: trigger at mid-viewport, compute heights in pixels not `vh` because mobile browser chrome resizes the viewport, remove hover on touch, be "highly explicit about the state of your visuals at each step", and "err on the side of short and sweet" ([Pudding responsive](https://pudding.cool/process/responsive-scrollytelling/); [Pudding part 3](https://pudding.cool/process/how-to-make-dope-shit-part-3/)). Reuters: "the hardest decisions involve what to leave out" and "We try not to make our pieces online encyclopaedias" ([Nightingale](https://medium.com/nightingale/visualizing-coronavirus-impact-an-interview-with-the-reuters-graphics-team-373296bc6118)).

Team size and time are the sobering part. SCMP's Forbidden City series took "almost two years" with a team of eleven, and a typical data visualisation "about a week"; "Everything we do starts with research and a pencil and paper" ([IJNet](https://ijnet.org/en/story/design-meets-data-qa-scmps-creative-director-darren-long)). Reuters' Marco Hernandez: "until we get to version 20 we are not close to publishing" ([Online Journalism Blog](https://onlinejournalismblog.com/2020/12/16/striking-the-balance-between-graphic-design-and-data-journalism-design-is-a-conversation/)). The Tulsa piece had about eight named contributors and months with archivists; the Minneapolis reconstruction six credited makers; The Boat five roles; Ciechanowski publishes roughly one explorable a year alone ([NYT Open](https://medium.com/timesopen/how-we-reconstructed-the-neighborhood-destroyed-by-the-tulsa-race-massacre-33fcf32dd086); [World Press Photo](https://www.worldpressphoto.org/collection/digital-storytelling-contest/2021/reconstructing-seven-days-of-protests-in-minne-(1)); [Cool People Stories](https://www.coolpeoplestories.com/bartosz-ciechanowski-interactive)). The benchmark note's inference, which this report adopts, is that a 3–5 person team should budget 2–4 months for a 6–8 chapter unit if it reuses open basemaps and limits itself to one custom instrument per chapter, and that bespoke 3-D reconstruction is out of reach without a specialist. Nothing in the award set is a pure map+timeline+narrative course; the Edo unit would be novel in combining American Panorama's map-essay, Museum of the World's tracked timeline and Tulsa's placed cast on Japan-specific open data. One gap: no jury published numeric craft criteria, so the measurable rules in the specification below are inferred constraints, not cited standards.

## Adults learn from segments, predictions and contiguity, not from scroll spectacle

The learning evidence does not say "make it interactive"; it says segment it, keep the referent next to the sentence, ask before and after, and keep essential content on the linear path. Mayer's review puts segmenting at **d = 0.70**, coherence at 0.70, spatial contiguity at 0.79, temporal contiguity at 1.30, signalling at 0.46, personalisation at 0.79 and redundancy at 0.87 ([Mayer 2017](https://consensus.app/papers/details/5ca62b3d23f75783a489de775688f399/?utm_source=claude_desktop)); the meta-analysis of 56 segmenting investigations confirms small-to-medium effects on retention and transfer, with learners of high prior knowledge gaining more ([Rey et al. 2019](https://consensus.app/papers/details/af8dc817f0875ce4b8d2d9da27b24741/?utm_source=claude_desktop)). Across 6.9 million edX sessions, "median engagement time is at most 6 minutes, regardless of total video length", and the share of sessions followed by a problem attempt fell from 56% to 31% as video length rose ([Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014](https://kixlab.github.io/website-files/2014/LAS2014-Engagement.pdf)), though a later 3.1-million-view study warns that single-session metrics understate engagement and that assessments tied to a video predicted engagement better than length ([Parker et al. 2024](https://consensus.app/papers/details/fabad2eb470e59ec97e24a177eb96556/?utm_source=claude_desktop)). The reconciliation the evidence note draws, and this report adopts, is **8–14 learner-paced segments of 4–8 minutes** for a 60–90 minute unit, with boundaries at meaningful sub-stories rather than time cuts.

Reading on a scrolling phone carries a measurable cost. The network meta-analysis of 56 studies ranks paper above tablets above e-readers above computers above smartphones, and finds that when scrolling was required paper was notably better (**g 0.35–0.48**) while with no scrolling the difference was small (g 0.03–0.12) ([Clinton-Lisell et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/0f3221d886fe5edc8ee58c2bcb6dcd69/?utm_source=claude_desktop)); the penalty is for expository text and vanishes for narrative ([Clinton 2019](https://consensus.app/papers/details/52817a59c9f152cbb0d9195b5414cbaf/?utm_source=claude_desktop)). There is no experimental literature on words per screen, so the safest proxy is a text block that fits a phone without scrolling, which converges with Google Arts & Culture's guidance of 280 characters per slide and 10–15 slides per story ([GAC help](https://support.google.com/culturalinstitute/partners/answer/10460872?hl=en)) and the academic scrolly tool's "under 100 words per step" with a text panel at 33% of width ([IRIS SIUE](https://iris.siue.edu/scrolly-story-generator-tutorial/)). The prototype's beats average 170 words; the longest is 286.

On narration, the modality advantage is a system-paced phenomenon: narration beats on-screen text by **d = 0.24** overall but 0.43–0.59 under system pacing, dynamic pictures and short materials ([Wang et al. 2016](https://consensus.app/papers/details/0dda3eb1b36b51aa8d1da62aa4e65b67/?utm_source=claude_desktop)), it is "not supported" in the 113-paper video review ([Fyfield et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/babf68b9f957596086c10c159a30a8fb/?utm_source=claude_desktop)), and for second-language readers text or text-plus-narration wins ([Lee & Mayer 2018](https://consensus.app/papers/details/5cf5ab3a24035836a4baf6151372d2b7/?utm_source=claude_desktop)), while captioning second-language video is among the largest benefits in the meta-meta-analysis ([Noetel et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/1f7d04c3f664533da5621eee37530353/?utm_source=claude_desktop)). Identical narration and on-screen text beside competing pictures is harmful, but key-term labels or slightly reworded captions help ([Adesope & Nesbit 2012](https://consensus.app/papers/details/c49f18068cfe535fbe566b5624dcb5ff/?utm_source=claude_desktop); [Yue, Bjork & Bjork 2013](https://consensus.app/papers/details/9c8518d61c0c590fa9e73d5a1d3d76a5/?utm_source=claude_desktop)). The design consequence is that a self-paced, scroll-driven unit for a worldwide English audience should be **text-first with narration as an optional, captioned layer**, reserved for the short system-paced moments (an animated map transition, a 60–90 second "watch this happen") where it actually pays. This also fits a zero-cost product: cheap TTS will cut into the voice effect anyway ([Chan et al. 2020](https://consensus.app/papers/details/315a14e7e7e85adea1c7c3c464c2f264/?utm_source=claude_desktop)).

On the one-tap checks the case already specifies, the evidence is favourable but specific. Prequestions give a moderate benefit for the content asked about (**g = 0.54**) and almost none for untested content (g = 0.04) ([St. Hilaire et al. 2023](https://consensus.app/papers/details/ae47f3256d405351ba11ef75d04f388d/?utm_source=claude_desktop)), so predictions should target the fact you most want remembered; predictions made before a reveal boost memory specifically for surprising outcomes ([Brod, Hasselhorn & Bunge 2018](https://consensus.app/papers/details/dbb6eb136ec658568e53fdb7bb2d5544/?utm_source=claude_desktop)), so the prediction belongs on the expectancy-violating fact (that a print cost a bowl of noodles; that 408,000 sheets of one series sold in a year); covert prequestions work as well as typed ones, which licenses the one-tap format ([Rivers et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/7cefb4970f7555e4bb4562426db79f3f/?utm_source=claude_desktop)). Interpolated tests between segments reduce mind-wandering and improve integration across segments ([Szpunar, Khan & Schacter 2013](https://consensus.app/papers/details/4f799cd5603f52bcaf9c87199bf36fe2/?utm_source=claude_desktop); [Jing, Szpunar & Schacter 2016](https://consensus.app/papers/details/6db4882c16985de7af0b905a8637cf07/?utm_source=claude_desktop)), though a replication found only a small effect ([Welhaf et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/8e95d77e88af500da8c3840e42c5f479/?utm_source=claude_desktop)); classroom quizzing lands at **g = 0.499** ([Yang et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/d5865595581856df817e7452b18cbdc4/?utm_source=claude_desktop)); recall tests beat recognition tests ([Rowland 2014](https://consensus.app/papers/details/04c7eb0ad2bf5eb6b122f94da91c5edb/?utm_source=claude_desktop)), so the checks should demand a decision (place on the map, order on the timeline, choose the cause) rather than yes/no recognition. Unassisted discovery loses to explicit instruction (d = −0.38) and assisted discovery wins (+0.30) ([Alfieri et al. 2011](https://consensus.app/papers/details/277f0240046f5523b67f3cec440855bf/?utm_source=claude_desktop)), so every embedded tool should be preceded by a worked example, one complete reading of the map or margin before the learner is asked to make one. The "argued" two-readings station is backed by the strongest history-education evidence (students reading multiple conflicting texts score higher and source and corroborate more, [Nokes, Dole & Hacker 2007](https://consensus.app/papers/details/34544af030fc5324a018263454f9bba4/?utm_source=claude_desktop)), with the warning that without intertextual scaffolding learners collapse into binary reliable/unreliable judgments ([Jay 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/b4addb8bfa765571919b13eb3e5195f3/?utm_source=claude_desktop)), so the station must show the two claims side by side and ask which reading a given piece of evidence supports.

On scrollytelling itself the honest reading is: engagement up, measured recall neutral-to-positive, and layout decides. In a 180-participant study comprehension was significantly better with a slideshow (stepped) layout than continuous vertical scrolling, and bidirectional text–visual linking raised engagement and recall ([Zhi, Ottley & Metoyer 2019](https://consensus.app/papers/details/a5fa9a98d4d75aa290901f45dc7c8334/?utm_source=claude_desktop)); animated transitions raise engagement while continuous versus discrete control does not ([McKenna et al. 2017](https://consensus.app/papers/details/2f4e146279a15c1fafdddee07467cd5b/?utm_source=claude_desktop)); and interactivity hides content, with readers of an interactive map seeing **4.9 of 9 sub-diagrams against 8.4** for the static version ([Greussing, Kessler & Boomgaarden 2020](https://exa.ai/library/publication/g5twrs95yhv)). Introductory stories did not make readers explore visualisations more ([Boy, Detienne & Fekete 2015](https://consensus.app/papers/details/56db60f9eba056c7a00d00859abf21f7/?utm_source=claude_desktop)). The justification for the sticky instrument is therefore contiguity, keeping the referent beside the sentence, and it turns into split attention the moment the instrument moves while the reader is on unrelated text. Pacing rule: one visual state per text step, changed only at a boundary the text names, animated briefly with the changed element signalled, and free exploration offered only at segment ends with everything essential on the linear path. A progress bar and a visible table of contents are the two cues shown to make learner control pay off ([Yu et al. 2023](https://consensus.app/papers/details/a51bd9f7aad455d0ae5531b42036c34b/?utm_source=claude_desktop)); the prototype has neither.

Two further points bound the plan. Completion data for open courses is poor, **3.13% of all HarvardX/MITx participants in 2017–18 and 46% of verified learners** ([Inside Higher Ed on Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente](https://www.insidehighered.com/digital-learning/article/2019/01/16/study-offers-data-show-moocs-didnt-achieve-their-goals)), and nobody publishes completion for free image-led stories, so the unit must instrument its own per-segment completion and should be finishable in one sitting. And accessibility is a learning feature: WCAG 2.2's SC 2.3.3 requires that motion triggered by interaction can be disabled, SC 2.2.2 that auto-moving content can be paused ([W3C WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/); [Understanding 2.2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html)), accessible web maps need controls outside the canvas, discrete keyboard steps and both a brief and a detailed description ([Cartographic Perspectives](https://cartographicperspectives.org/index.php/journal/article/download/cp88-pr/1574/7625)), and timelines have a tested WAI-ARIA pattern (events as tabs, cards as tabpanels) ([Telerik](https://www.telerik.com/kendo-angular-ui/components/layout/timeline/accessibility/aria-support_timeline)). Gaps to carry: all the segmenting and pretesting evidence is STEM or generic, none is adult history; no study tests a persistent dual panel; and no source gives transition durations.

## A world-class unit tells the publisher's story and names the carvers

The reference works the earlier research lacked are organised around exactly the ideas the unit needs. The two-volume *Hotei Encyclopedia* (2005) runs history → genres → "Commerce and constraint: the publishing trade (Kornicki); censorship (Thompson)" → materials and connoisseurship, including Uhlenbeck's "The original versus the genuine" → collecting, and its second volume is the seal-and-signature apparatus a "read the margin" interactive is built from ([WorldCat contents](https://search.worldcat.org/title/61666175)). Andreas Marks's *Publishers of Japanese Woodblock Prints* makes the publisher the unit of lookup: "over 1,100 publishers", lists for 572 artists, "facsimiles of over 2300 publisher seals" ([Brill](https://brill.com/display/title/18701)), and his earlier survey restates the thesis that publishers "owned the woodblocks and copyrights" ([Foreword Reviews](https://www.forewordreviews.com/reviews/japanese-woodblock-prints/)). Across the Hotei, Marks, the Met's 2021 "Japan: A History of Style", the British Museum's 2005 "Kabuki Heroes" and the Columbia, Harvard, Rikkyo and Rare Book School syllabi, the recurring spine is **market and city → process and trade with the publisher first → genres as market segments → censorship as a shaping force → the edition and state problem → export and collecting** ([content and sources](/home/claude/research_notes/Edo%20unit%20design%20and%20content%20redesign/content_and_sources.md)). That spine and the critique's five publisher acts are the same structure seen from two sides, which is the strongest reason to adopt it.

Three stories the prototype does not tell would make it an expert unit rather than a good one. The first is Osaka as the control case: in the 8th month of 1793 the publisher Shioya Chōbei was granted the exclusive right to publish actor likenesses, bought a rival's blocks in 1794, and the artist Ryūkōsai "never put his signature" on prints Shioya published and withdrew ([Yano, PDF](https://www.colorado.edu/cas/sites/default/files/attached-files/akiko_yano_hr.pdf)); Gerstle and Clark's exhibition argued that Kamigata prints were financed by fan clubs and poetry circles rather than publishers, a different production model in the same medium ([REF case study](https://impact.ref.ac.uk/casestudies/CaseStudy.aspx?Id=42788)). The second is Ōi as the authorship case: the Ōta Memorial Museum confirms "only about 10 of her works" survive ([Ōta 2023](https://www.ukiyoe-ota-muse.jp/yoshiwarakoushi-eng/)), Hokusai "is reported to have said" her pictures of women were better than his ([Davis abstract](https://www.deafspotlight.org/events/saturday-university-katsushika-oi-and-the-hokusai-legacy)), and Davis's thesis is that her contribution was made to vanish for the modern market. The third closes the "who gets named" question: the Smithsonian's 1889 Tokunō gift (about 200 objects: 44 tools, 17 pigments, 21 blocks, 71 progressive proofs for a triptych and 35 for *Nandina domestica*) names the carver **Kido Kotarō** and the printers **Yamamoto Iwakichi** and **Nakamura Tsurusaburō** ([Smithsonian Tokuno gift](https://www.si.edu/spotlight/tokuno-gift/about); [NMAH GA.03217.24](https://www.americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_788927)), the one place a state bureau documented the trade's nameless hands because it was sending the trade abroad. Pair that with the case's Egawa Tomekichi and Hokusai's 1835 letter about a carver's fee, and the unit has its payoff.

The legends should be taught as legends with their first printed source, which turns them into a lesson in source criticism. The Ōta's chief curator traced the "ukiyo-e as packing paper" story to Léonce Bénédite's 1905 memoir, in which Bracquemond found at Delâtre's "un petit livre bizarrement broché" used to wedge porcelain, a bound volume used as a divider, with no evidence that single sheets wrapped export ceramics, and the date itself disputed ([Ōta note, Hinohara 2021](https://otakinen-museum.note.jp/n/n01248684801c)). The Great Wave is not a tsunami: Cartwright and Nakamura conclude Hokusai "intended to depict an exceptionally large storm wave", and Dudley, Sarano and Dias estimate a plunging breaker "around 10 m" about 3 km offshore ([Cartwright & Nakamura 2009](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rsnr.2007.0039); [Dudley et al. 2013](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/full/10.1098/rsnr.2012.0066)). Hokusai did train as a block-cutter between 16 and 19 ([BM blog](https://www.britishmuseum.org/blog/hokusai-father-manga)), and "father of manga" is a question even in Clark's title. The content note assembles nine recurring misunderstandings with an authoritative correction for each (one artist made it; it is a tsunami; prints were worthless packing; Hokusai invented manga; ukiyo-e means all Japanese prints; Osaka had none; an original is one object; the Yoshiwara was a free zone; the artist chose and printed the colours), and the unit's predictions should sit on exactly these, since that is where the surprise is.

## The specification: five acts, one stage, three scales, a timeline that charts the trade

**Narrative architecture.** Pose the case's question in the first screen and build five acts on four publishers' gambles and one last publisher, each act ending on a question the next answers: Act I Tsutaya (1783–1806: the quarter's publisher becomes Edo's, is fined half his assets, answers with mica portraits, ends in handcuffs); Act II Eijudō (1829–33: a Fuji series bet on a pigment fad, the Wave, the price of blue falls); Act III Fujiokaya and Uoya Eikichi (1842–58: the ban, 408,000 sheets, the *Hundred Views*); Act IV Hayashi, Bing and Wright (1867–1928: the second trade and its scandal); Act V the Bank of Japan (2024: the banknote). A second spine follows one object, a sheet of the Wave from Echizen paper to the Met's JP1847 by the Havemeyer bequest, so "the price, the blue, the seal, the export, the icon" are stations on one sheet's life. Osaka's 1793 monopoly, Ōi's hand and the Tokunō names are the three stations that make it expert. Within the five acts, reconcile the case's eight lectures with the learning evidence as **ten segments of 4–8 minutes** (two per act), each of five to seven beats; every beat has at most one event and one object, **40–100 words** of text (the prototype averages 170), one explicit instrument state, and no two consecutive beats with the same state. That yields roughly 55–70 beats and a similar word total to today's 5,627, redistributed from summary into quotation and comparison: the edict's own clause, the Fujiokaya diary's line for the hit and the flop, the 1847 magistrate's six or seven tenths, the 1835 letter, the Eijudō advertisement, each inside the 25-word limit. Every segment opens with one one-tap prediction on its most surprising fact and closes with one or two one-tap decisions resolved on the instruments (place it on the map, order it on the timeline, which band does this seal belong to), with the answer and a one-line explanation shown at once. Four "argued" stations (Hokusai genius versus system; Smith versus Guth on the blue; the ban created versus sped the landscape; exchange versus appropriation) are two-column cards that ask which reading a given piece of evidence supports, and six closing questions re-ask earlier items. Every "the course does not rule" sentence becomes either an argued station or a one-clause "argued:" marker. Names in the text link to cast cards; cast cards list the beats where the person appears; the cast page groups by the case's roles with publishers first and includes the eight publishers, the three named carvers and printers, and Ōi. Content notes begin "Content note:", sit before the first mention of the quarter, and skip to the next beat, not two beats on. Chapter openers are full-bleed objects at deep zoom (the Wave's hollow, the Shin-Ōhashi margin, the kiwame seal filling the screen) with the text column entering afterwards, one change of pace per act.

**The map.** One pinned instrument stage per chapter, with the other instrument shrunk to an indicator no larger than about 15% of the viewport; no exemplar runs two full instruments and the evidence says a moving second panel is split attention. Three scales, each a vector drawing in one visual language. The world is d3-geo on an Equal Earth or Natural Earth projection rotated about −150° to −160° to centre the Pacific; d3 cuts the antimeridian at projection time so the rotation renders cleanly ([Observable](https://observablehq.com/notebook-kit/ex/d3/antimeridian-cutting)), the 110 m world-atlas file is 210 KB against 782 KB for 50 m ([Natural Earth 110m](https://www.naturalearthdata.com/downloads/110m-cultural-vectors/110m-admin-0-countries)), labels get collision avoidance, and arcs follow sea lanes with their counts on them, not great circles across continents. Japan is MapLibre on OpenHistoricalMap's "Woodblock" or "Japanese Scroll" style with `maplibre-gl-dates` filtering to the beat's year ([OHM Reuse](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/OpenHistoricalMap/Reuse); [maplibre-gl-dates](https://github.com/OpenHistoricalMap/maplibre-gl-dates/)), the Gokaidō and the 53 Tōkaidō stations from the CODH highway and post-station datasets ([CODH historical GIS](https://codh.rois.ac.jp/historical-gis/)), Hiroshige's Kanbara and Mishima sheets pinned to their stations, and Fuji removed from the route; the Fuji/Hakone relief, if wanted, is MapLibre terrain on the keyless AWS Terrarium tiles at pitch 30–70° ([Made with MapLibre](https://madewithmaplibre.com/basemaps/styles/aws-terrarium/)), in a separate map instance because the Allmaps layer needs `maxPitch: 0`. Edo is the 1677 and 1859 maps georeferenced in Allmaps Editor with six or more ground-control points on bridges, castle gates and river mouths and a per-sheet mask (the kiriezu are schematic), rendered live by `@allmaps/maplibre` or, for longevity, baked to XYZ tiles with the Allmaps CLI and GDAL in CI ([Allmaps](https://allmaps.org/); [@allmaps/maplibre](https://allmaps.org/docs/packages/maplibre)), with a range input bound to `setOpacity()` for the 1677↔1859 scrub, CODH's townhouse polygons for the chōnin wards, and pins from CODH's place names prioritised by `symbol-sort-key`. The Owariya legend becomes the city palette: red temples and shrines, yellow roads and bridges, grey townspeople's quarters, blue water, green embankments ([Sainsbury Institute](https://www.sainsbury-institute.org/library-treasures/edo-kirie-zu-area-maps-by-owariya/)). Pins are objects: a tap on Nihonbashi opens the publishers' addresses (Tōri Abura-chō, Bakuro-chō, Kodenma-chō, Shiba Shinmei-mae) with shop and seal. Events are animated, not pinned: the 1657 burn grows as a polygon over the 1677 map, Tsutaya's pin travels in 1783, the three theatres travel in 1842, Perry follows his actual route once it is sourced from the expedition narrative, and Hayashi's 218 shipments pulse with their counts. Camera moves use `flyTo` at `speed 0.6–0.8`, `curve 1.42`, `maxDuration` about 6,000 ms, `easeTo` within a scale and `jumpTo` across scales, and both engines already skip the flight under OS reduced motion unless `essential: true` ([MapLibre FlyToOptions](https://maplibre.org/maplibre-gl-js/docs/API/type-aliases/FlyToOptions/); [MapLibre Map](https://www.maplibre.org/maplibre-gl-js/docs/API/classes/Map)). Scale breaks (city → Japan → world) are their own beat with their own sentence. Explore becomes a layer, not a mode: every place, event and lifespan is clickable and opens a card with its beats, objects and sources, and the story column is never dimmed to 35%.

**The timeline.** A chart of the trade, not a ruler. Two rows (1600–1870 and 1870–2024) replace the unexplained chevron break, following Histography's lesson that the scale change must be explicit ([Smashing Magazine](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2016/09/interview-with-matan-stauber/)). Five lanes by `kind` (state, trade, print, world, after) with the Museum of the World's colour-coded tracks as the model; cast lifespans as bars from `PEOPLE.dates` that light while the person is on stage (Hokusai's 1760–1849 spans three eras; Tsutaya's 47 years sit inside the golden age); the four censor regimes (before 1790, 1790–1842, 1843–52, 1853–75) as bands, because they are the margin tool's answer key; a price lane in mon with dots for sourced years, ranges for probable ones and the 1842 cap as a step, replacing bars of equal authority; print runs as bubbles sized by count (2,000 break-even, 8,000 Wave estimate, 12–15,000 Tōkaidō, 408,000 sheets); Smith's Prussian-blue import series as a sparkline in the 1820s, or no chart; the fire and the earthquake as marks; event cards that open as the playhead passes, never hover-only. Density follows Kräutli and Boyd Davis (same-size disks stacked vertically, spread within date uncertainty) and Faces of Sweden's cap of seven thumbnails per visible span ([Kräutli & Boyd Davis](https://researchonline.rca.ac.uk/1725/1/Kra%CC%88utli%20Boyd%20Davis%20-%20Revealing%20Cultural%20Collections%20Over%20Time%202015.pdf); [Faces of Sweden](https://github.laiyagushi.com/riksantikvarieambetet/Faces-of-Sweden)); the working rule, an inference, is one thumbnail per 120–160 px of track with the rest collapsed into density marks. Era labels are measured and dropped to a second row rather than overlapping. The playhead shows the Japanese era year beside the Western one (Kansei 2, Tenpō 13) because the edicts are dated that way, and the current year and era are always visible in the instrument header, not only on a 60 px strip at the bottom.

**The tools.** Reduce eight to five rebuilt to the case specification, absorb two into the instruments, cut one, and build the three the case designed and the prototype dropped.

Every tool receives a `scope` and may draw only while its beat is active; every autoplay has a pause; every check resolves on an instrument; no tool carries a learner-facing apology. Deep zoom is OpenSeadragon pointed straight at IIIF `info.json` (not through Mirador, which double-requests tiles) ([Omeka forum](https://forum.omeka.org/t/accessing-big-images-through-iiif-manifests-is-extremely-slow/17175)), with our own `vips dzsave --layout iiif3` pyramids baked for longevity.

**The visual system.** One material world: washi cream and sumi ink for both halves, the map drawn in ink lines on the same paper as the text, in the modern-period idiom Rekichizu proved legible, without the paper texture its designer rejected. The seal red is reserved for the censor's seal and the 1842 ban; points, routes, hotspots and sliders are ink or indigo. Typography uses the Japanese titles as display (名所江戸百景 as display, romaji as kicker, kanji on first mention) with Shippori Mincho (SIL OFL 1.1, a **21.9 MB** family that must be subset with `unicode-range`) or Zen Old Mincho for kanji, a Latin serif of similar colour, and Noto Serif JP as fallback, all self-hosted since the Google Fonts request failed in the offline audit ([Google Fonts specimen](https://fonts.google.com/specimen/Shippori+Mincho)). Portraits are uncropped at native aspect or uniformly cropped to one aspect anchored on the eyes with the catalogue-mark edge clipped as Faces of Sweden does, used once each, and the publishers' seals (Tsutaya's ivy-leaf-under-Fuji, Uoya's 魚栄) become the cast's marks. Every image carries a provenance line in TASL order, title, maker, holding institution and accession, rights, link ([CC attribution](https://web.archive.org/web/20230331185713/http://wiki.creativecommons.org/Best_practices_for_attribution)). Transitions are scroll-linked or, if timed, under about 600 ms with no autoplay longer than a beat; the same grade applies to every scene. Images ship as AVIF/WebP with `srcset` at 480/960/1400, portraits at card size, timeline thumbnails lazy, the city raster loaded on the first city beat, with a target under 3 MB for a full read.

**Mobile.** The desktop text column is about 33vw (at most 560 px) with a 5vw gutter, following the Mapbox storytelling template's values, and under 750–800 px it becomes 90vw overlay cards or stacked stills ([mapbox/storytelling](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mapbox/storytelling/main/index.html)). Step heights are pixels computed from `window.innerHeight`, never `vh`; Scrollama offsets switch to pixels on touch; CSS and JS breakpoints share one `matchMedia` ([Pudding responsive](https://pudding.cool/process/responsive-scrollytelling)). The decision to keep the scroll or stack is made per chapter: keep it where the transition shows change over time or spatial movement (the burn, the move, the shipments), stack where steps stand alone. The instrument pane is a collapsible strip of roughly 35–40% of `innerHeight` showing the current scene only; the timeline is a thin progress strip with the current year and era; tools stack with their own figure; the Explore button stays on screen; nothing is hover-only.

**Accessibility.** WCAG 2.2 AA plus the AAA motion criterion. Under `prefers-reduced-motion` or an in-page toggle, every transition becomes an instant state change with the same end state, autoplay is off, `scroll-behavior` is `auto`, and the stacked layout is the reduced-motion layout. The instrument has a parallel semantic layer: an `<h1>`, a heading per segment, one sentence describing the current map and timeline state per beat plus an expandable detailed description or table, places and events as focusable buttons or tabs with names, zoom and pan as buttons outside the canvas, no `role="application"`, the city labels not `aria-hidden`. Checks are real buttons with visible focus; verdicts and counters announce through `aria-live="polite"`; right and wrong are never colour or strikethrough alone; contrast is 4.5:1 for text and 3:1 for large text, and the 72% dimming of unread beats is measured against WCAG 1.4.3 or dropped. Any narration ships with captions and a transcript.

## A zero-cost pipeline: beats as data, assets by CI, narration by hash

The proven newsroom pattern is copy as structured data feeding a component framework with a sticky-graphic plus stepped-text component; The Pudding ships exactly this as an open SvelteKit starter with ArchieML, a `Scrolly` component, `Slider` and `Tap` for touch, a `reducedMotion` store and Style Dictionary tokens ([the-pudding/svelte-starter](https://github.com/the-pudding/svelte-starter)). For a 39-unit programme, Astro's content collections add what that starter lacks: Zod-validated YAML/JSON/Markdown collections, cross-collection `reference()` so no beat can point at a missing place or person, and build-time errors when "any file violates its collection schema" ([Astro content collections](https://docs.astro.build/en/guides/content-collections/)). The recommended beat schema is `unit.yaml → beats[]`, each with `id`, `text`, `scene` (an enum of registered scene components), `props` (map centre, zoom, layers, highlights; IIIF region; model and camera), `audio` (file id and transcript), and `sources[]` (claim ids), with people, places, events, objects and claims as separate collections keyed to Wikidata QIDs, World Historical Gazetteer and CODH ids, PeriodO ARKs for named periods and EDTF dates that allow uncertainty ([PeriodO](https://perio.do/technical-overview/); [WHG API](https://docs.whgazetteer.org/content/technical/apis.html)). The claims collection carries `confidence: high|medium|low|contested` and `sources[]`, and CI fails the build if a beat cites a contested claim without a visible hedge or a claim lacks a source; the fact-check log is the collection's git history. Authors write YAML and Markdown offline; everything that needs the internet (API fetches, Wikidata checks, model downloads, tiling) runs in GitHub Actions on push. Beat triggering is scrollama (MIT, IntersectionObserver) or forty lines of native IO, with CSS scroll-driven animations as progressive enhancement only, because MDN still marks `animation-timeline` "Limited availability ... not Baseline" and Firefox support remained behind a flag in early 2026 ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/Reference/Properties/animation-timeline); [Mozilla Connect](https://connect.mozilla.org/t5/ideas/implement-css-scroll-driven-animations-animation-timeline/idi-p/116931)). GSAP is now free including its former Club plugins, but under its own Standard License rather than an OSI licence ([gsap.com](https://gsap.com/scroll)), so it is reserved for scrubbed sequences and only after the licence is read.

The assets pipeline runs entirely in CI from one `assets.yaml` per unit listing source, id, crop and credit. The Met needs no key and asks for no more than 80 requests a second, with `/v1/search` retiring on 1 October 2026 in favour of `/v1.1/search` ([metmuseum.github.io](https://metmuseum.github.io/)); the Art Institute throttles anonymous users to 60 requests a minute and serves cached IIIF widths of 200/400/600/843 and 1686 px for public-domain works ([AIC API docs](http://aggregator-data.artic.edu/docs/)); Cleveland returns up to 1,000 records per call with a GitHub mirror ([CMA API](https://openaccess-api.clevelandart.org/)); Wikimedia asks for a descriptive `User-Agent`, serial requests and `maxlag` ([API:Etiquette](https://mediawiki.org/wiki/Special:MyLanguage/API:Etiquette)); Smithsonian and Library of Congress limits (1,000 an hour per key; 20 JSON requests a minute) come from secondary notes and must be verified before coding. The fetcher writes a sidecar JSON (title, maker, date, credit, licence, source URL, retrieved-at) and emits WebP/AVIF sizes, so no image can reach a page without its provenance line. Narration files are stored as `narration/<unit>/<beat-id>.<sha8-of-text>.opus` with a timed transcript, and CI regenerates only beats whose text hash changed, which keeps audio and captions provably in sync. Capacity arithmetic: 39 units at about 15 MB plus 60 reference pages is 0.6–0.7 GB, under GitHub Pages' cap with little headroom, so tile pyramids (a 10k×10k DZI is about 2,000 tiles) and audio go to R2.

What makes unit 39 cheap is already visible in this list: a fixed beat schema and scene registry so authors only write data; shared people, places and claims collections that grow with each unit; a manifest-driven fetcher; hash-keyed narration; diff-based review through snapshot, axe and Lighthouse gates; and Pudding-style checkpoints (question → sources → storyboard → production, with explicit continue, pivot or put-down decisions) to kill weak units early ([Pudding pivot](https://pudding.cool/process/pivot-continue-down/)). Gaps the stack note flags: the GSAP licence text, the `@allmaps/*` package licences, Voyager's and Mirador's licences, Cloudflare Pages' monthly build quota and Zen Old Mincho's OFL file were not read and must be before they are depended on.

## Sequence: fix ownership first, rewrite second, build the atlas third

The order matters more than the list. Items that answer the lead's verdict directly ("doesn't make a coherent thread", "the atlas should be interactive on a world map", "the timeline should be designed like one with animations") come first; the items that separate a correct piece from an award-level one come after; the detail fixes can be done in any order once the engine is sound. Effort figures combine the critique's day estimates with the production note's hour ranges and the benchmark teams' durations; **all of them are inferences from comparable work, not measured data**, and the first unit also builds the template, so the production note's rule that the first unit costs 3–5× a later one (40–65 hours after the template exists) is the basis for the total.

The range brackets the production note's 3–5× rule (120–325 hours) at the low end and the benchmark inference of 3–5 people over 2–4 months at the high end; a two-person core (one content lead, one producer-engineer) with an editor for fact-check, pronunciation and accessibility sign-off is the minimum, and AI agents fit the sweet spots The Pudding itself reports, drafting fetch scripts and prototyping scenes while humans own edge cases, because fixing those "can often take days on end" ([pudding.cool/2024/07/ai](https://pudding.cool/2024/07/ai/)). Phases 1 and 2 overlap deliberately: the storyboard from phase 1 is the specification for phase 2, and the instruments should not be built until every beat's state is written down, which is the single discipline that prevents the current prototype's decorative beats from recurring. Expect, per Reuters and SCMP, ten to twenty iterations of the hero chapter (Act II, the Wave) before the rest is built to its standard.

Three risks need naming. Licence scope: if the programme is ever commercial, the Rekichizu tiles (CC BY-NC-ND) and the NMAH Tokunō images (educational use only) drop out, which is why the specification defaults to OpenHistoricalMap and to our own layer reconstruction with the proofs embedded rather than copied. Source longevity: live IIIF from holders is a dependency on their servers, which is why pyramids and tiles are baked in CI and stored on R2. Reader evidence: nothing here has been tested with a learner, so phase 5 is not optional, and the unit's own completion data is the only measure that will tell the team whether 55–70 beats in ten segments is right.

## Conclusion

The deepest finding across the six notes is that the prototype's problems are one problem seen from several sides. A story without a protagonist produces beats without events; beats without events give the map nothing to change; a map that does not change invites widgets to fill the silence; widgets with their own timers fight the scroll driver and overwrite the scene; and a build that cannot say what state each beat is in cannot be tested, read backwards, made accessible or handed to the next author. The fix is therefore not more interactivity but a single discipline: every beat declares one event, one object and one instrument state in data, and everything else, the atlas, the timeline, the tools, the checks, the narration, the QA gates, is a function of that declaration. The award evidence, the learning evidence and the engineering evidence all point to the same place.

The second finding is that Edo is the best possible unit to carry this discipline for the whole programme, because its open substrate is unusually complete: CC0 impressions from five museums, IIIF kiriezu and city maps, a CC BY gazetteer of 7,918 Edo place names and townhouse polygons, open highway and coastline data, a 1889 process set that names the carvers, and a publisher-first scholarly spine that the best references already share. Once the beat schema, the scene registry and the fetcher exist, the thirty-ninth unit should cost a seventh of this one, and the question for the lead is no longer whether the atlas can be interactive on a world map or the timeline designed like one, but whether the team will hold the story-first checkpoint long enough to write every state down before anyone opens a code editor.
