# Edo Japan and ukiyo-e as a creative industry: what an expert unit must contain, and which open sources can carry it

Research note · 4 October 2026 · extends `research/edo-2026-10-04/teaching.md` (exhibitions, books, interactives, debates), `people_system.md`, `objects_events.md` and `EDO_FULL_CASE.md`. Everything below was read on pages opened today; nothing is repeated from those files except where a new source changes or sharpens it. Confidence words: **documented** (on the holder's/publisher's/author's own page or in a cited scholarly text), **probable** (secondary page or inferable), **contested/legend** (both readings given, or the sources themselves call it a legend). Quotations are kept under 25 words. No URL below was composed by hand.

## Key question 1 · Which chapter sequences and framings do the best references use, and where do they differ from those already recorded?

### Takeaway
The two reference works the earlier note lacked, the **Hotei Encyclopedia** (2005) and Marks's **Publishers compendium** (2011), are organised around exactly the ideas the unit needs: the Hotei's volume 1 runs *history → genres → publishing trade and censorship → materials, conservation and connoisseurship → collecting*, and volume 2 is the seal-and-signature apparatus a "read the margin" interactive would be built from; Marks makes the publisher the unit of lookup (over 1,100 publishers, 2,300 seals). The Met's "Japan: A History of Style" and the BM's "Kabuki Heroes" supply two framings the earlier note did not have: *acquisition-driven storytelling* and *fan culture as the engine of production*.

### Cited Findings
- **Hotei Encyclopedia (Newland gen. ed., 2005; eds Davis, Oikawa, Tinios, Uhlenbeck), 2 vols, 600 pp.** Vol. 1 order: *Historical perspectives* (Bolitho on Edo; Waswo on Meiji–Taishō) → *History of Japanese prints, Edo*: roots to mid-18th c. (Jenkins), book illustration (Brown), shunga (Screech), Kanbun bijin (Kobayashi), Chinese prints' influence (Thomsen), birth of full colour / Harunobu (Waterhouse), the Yoshiwara (Seigle), mitate (Tinios), kabuki (Leiter), Utamaro's generation (Davis), sumō prints (Bickford), kyōka and designers (Carpenter), **Tsutaya Jūzaburō and publishing (Suzuki Toshiyuki)**, meisho-e (Hickey), 1804–68 diversification (Tinios), **surimono (Keyes)**, **Nagasaki-e (Chaiklin)**, **Kamigata-e: Osaka and Kyoto (Kitagawa Hiroko)**, shini-e memorial prints (Takeuchi), warrior prints and the Suikoden (Robinson) → *Meiji* (Merritt; Oikawa; Yokohama-e; photography and prints; war reportage) → *1900s–1926* (Brown; Watanabe and shin-hanga; sōsaku-hanga magazines) → **Commerce and constraint: the publishing trade (Kornicki); censorship (Sarah E. Thompson)** → *Materials and techniques* (Sasaki), care of prints (Webber), collecting: quality, condition, rarity (Uhlenbeck), **"The original versus the genuine" (Uhlenbeck)** → *History of collecting*: Japan (Oikawa), Europe 1860–1930 (Put), postwar Europe (Schaap), America (Meech). Vol. 2: reference section, artist index, lineage charts, chronological tables, map of provinces and the Gokaidō, **signature facsimiles, censor seals, publisher seals**, Watanabe list, print formats, "Elements of a print", kanji concordance — documented — [WorldCat record with full contents](https://search.worldcat.org/title/61666175); publisher page: first section "divided into four main subject areas: historical background, the art history of Ukiyo-e prints, print production ... and the history of collecting" — [Brill](https://brill.com/edcollbook/title/17750).
- caa.reviews (Patricia Graham, 2007) on the Hotei: vol. 1 has eight long chapter essays and thirty-three shorter articles; vol. 2's artist entries are "grouped together by lineage" rather than alphabetically; the title omits "ukiyo-e" so readers may expect all Japanese woodblock printing when the text "focuses on prints of the ukiyo-e tradition" — documented — [caa.reviews](https://www.caareviews.org/reviews/975).
- **Andreas Marks, *Publishers of Japanese Woodblock Prints: A Compendium* (Hotei/Brill, 2011, 576 pp.)**: "over 1,100 publishers", publication lists for "572 artists", "facsimiles of over 2300 publisher seals", 1650s–1990s; each entry gives residence, clientele, period of activity and a chronological list of series — documented — [Brill](https://brill.com/display/title/18701).
- Marks's 2010 Tuttle survey (fifty artists, forty-nine publishers) review restates the thesis: publishers "owned the woodblocks and copyrights" and once "served as censors" in a self-regulation attempt — documented (review) — [Foreword Reviews](https://www.forewordreviews.com/reviews/japanese-woodblock-prints/).
- **Met, "Japan: A History of Style" (8 Mar 2021–24 Apr 2022; Galleries 223–232)**: 300+ works over four rotations; each of the ten galleries "a distinct genre, school, or style"; the framing is a decade of gifts and purchases that "transformed the Museum's ability to narrate" Japanese art; first rotation included 35 ukiyo-e from the Lee E. Dirks collection (1680s–1850s, figures: actors and courtesans), among them a Kiyomasu I actor print said to be the only surviving example, four Sharaku bust portraits, and Hokusai's *Spying with a Telescope*, "only three impressions ... known to survive"; later rotations: courtly calligraphy, popular Buddhist art, Maruyama-Shijō, modern printmaking; curators Carpenter, Bincsik, Rio — documented — [Met press release](https://www.metmuseum.org/press-releases/japan-a-history-of-style-2021-exhibitions); [Met exhibition page](https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/japan-history-of-style). Note: the Met's follow-on rotation "Infinite Artistry" runs 19 Jan 2026–8 Aug 2027 in the same galleries — documented (title only) — [Met press](https://www.metmuseum.org/press-releases/infinite-artistry-2026).
- **British Museum, "Kabuki Heroes on the Osaka Stage 1780–1830" (2005; then Osaka Museum of History and Waseda Theatre Museum)**, curated by Andrew Gerstle with Timothy Clark; AHRC-funded with Ritsumeikan; catalogue in English and Japanese with fifteen contributors; its organising idea was a "kabuki culture" in which **fan clubs (hiiki-ren) and poetry circles produced surimono, prints and books** promoting actors; the first Osaka-print exhibition since 1975 and the first in Europe; Clark says the "kabuki culture" model was reused for the 2013 *Shunga* show — documented — [REF 2014 impact case study](https://impact.ref.ac.uk/casestudies/CaseStudy.aspx?Id=42788); catalogue record — [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/kabukiheroesonos0000gers).
- Gerstle on Osaka vs Edo (EAJS 2017 abstract): "Kyoto and Osaka did not have the same commercial base for actor print production as in Edo"; Kamigata had well-developed fan clubs; poetry circles let actors, officially "riverbed beggars", mingle with literati under pen-names — documented — [EAJS panel](https://nomadit.co.uk/conference/eajs2017/p/5457).
- Akiko Yano's essay on Ryūkōsai gives the Osaka trade a datable power struggle: in the 8th month of 1793 the publisher **Shioya Chōbei** was granted exclusive right to publish actor likenesses in book and single-sheet form; in 1794 he bought the blocks of Ryūkōsai's third actor book; Ryūkōsai "never put his signature" on prints Shioya published, and withdrew; Shioya appointed Shōkōsai Hanbei chief artist by the 9th month of 1796; Yano reads Ryūkōsai, Nichōsai and Suifutei as literati "not commercially driven" — documented — [Yano, "Capturing the Body" (PDF)](https://www.colorado.edu/cas/sites/default/files/attached-files/akiko_yano_hr.pdf).
- **Rijksmuseum surimono catalogue (online, from Matthi Forrer's 2013 collection catalogue)**: "almost 4000 prints, more than 800 ... surimono"; Bierens de Haan bequest of 328 (1958) and Goslings gift of 345 (1991–99); each object page carries a scholarly catalogue entry with Keyes/Carpenter references — documented — [Rijksmuseum surimono catalogue](https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/research/collection-catalogues/surimono); sample entry (Toyohiro, RP-P-1991-568, with Forrer and Keyes citations) — [Rijksmuseum object](https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/collection/object/A-Woman-and-a-Playing-Child--733584ef259c47b52c0a165f480f0a14).
- **Ōta Memorial Museum on Katsushika Ōi**: "only about 10 of her works have been confirmed to exist" (2023 show) / "only about a dozen of her hand-painted works" (2026 show); *Night Scene in the Yoshiwara* (Yoshiwara kōshi-saki no zu) shown Nov 2023 and again 2026 with Harunobu, Hiroshige and Kiyochika night scenes and **export night-scene prints by publishers Hasegawa Takejirō and Watanabe Shōzaburō** — documented — [Ōta 2023](https://www.ukiyoe-ota-muse.jp/yoshiwarakoushi-eng/); [Ōta 2026](https://www.ukiyoe-ota-muse.jp/katsushikaoi2026-eng/). The Ōta dates the painting c.1818–60; the BM dates it 1844–54 (secondary) — [Art History Project](https://www.arthistoryproject.com/artists/katsushika-oi/night-scene-in-the-yoshiwara/).
- Julie Nelson Davis's lecture abstract on Ōi: Hokusai "is reported to have said" her bijin pictures were better than his; "only a handful of paintings and two illustrated books bear her signature"; thesis that her contributions were made to vanish "for the modern market, for reception abroad, and for profit" — documented (abstract) — [Deaf Spotlight / Saturday University listing](https://www.deafspotlight.org/events/saturday-university-katsushika-oi-and-the-hokusai-legacy).
- **University course structures found today**: Columbia's Burke Center lists AHIS GU4760 "Great Waves: Arts of the Floating World" (McKelway), GR8128 "Edo Period Painting", GU4763/GR8639 "Reading Places and Images in Edo-period Illustrated Books" (McKelway, with Noguchi), GR8614 "Ukiyo-e Prints, Paintings, and Illustrated Books" (Carpenter) and GR8675 "Ukiyo-e: Beyond the Great Wave" (Davis, visiting) — documented — [Columbia Burke Center courses](https://burkecenter.columbia.edu/courses). Harvard HAA 188P "Edo Painting" (Lippit, 2020): formats and materiality, connoisseurship, display, seasonality, poetry, "the influence of European pictorialism, and the status of the painter", taught in the galleries alongside the Feinberg show — documented — [Harvard HAA](https://haa.fas.harvard.edu/class/haa-188p-edo-painting). Rikkyo AL392 "Japanese Woodblock Prints in Context" (2024, English, 14 weeks): Floating World → Actors → **Firefighters** → Landscapes → Foreigners → **Catfish** → **News** → **Satire** → two hands-on sessions → museum visit — documented — [Rikkyo syllabus](https://sy.rikkyo.ac.jp/web/preview.php?kodo_2=AL392&nendo=2024). Pauline Ota, ARTH 290C (2024): ukiyo-e then sōsaku-hanga; themes "government censorship, the powerful entertainment industries of theatre & sex ... parody/word play" — documented — [syllabus abstract](https://exa.ai/library/publication/tdry69fc99h). Rare Book School "Japanese Printmaking, 1615–1868": explicitly rejects the older view of ukiyo-e as "the art of the brothel district" and the Yoshiwara as a "safety valve", and starts from Kyoto/Osaka/Sakai courtly-commoner culture — documented — [RBS course](https://rarebookschool.org/courses/details/i80).

### Inferences
- Across all these structures the recurring "spine" is: *market and city → process and trade (publisher first) → genres as market segments → censorship as a shaping force → the edition/state problem → export and collecting*. The Hotei is the only one that also builds in **conservation and connoisseurship** ("The original versus the genuine") as a chapter, which the unit's "one design, many originals" interactive should cite.
- The references differ on **where Osaka sits**: Hotei gives Kamigata-e one essay among ~40; Gerstle/Clark make it a whole exhibition and argue it is a different production model (fan-financed rather than publisher-financed). A unit on ukiyo-e "as a creative industry" should present Osaka as the control case: same medium, different financing, different outcome in 1842.
- The Met's 2021 framing (acquisitions as the engine of what can be narrated) is itself teachable: it shows that a museum's "history of Japanese art" is a function of what donors gave.

### Gaps
- The Hotei's actual essay texts were not opened (contents only); the Marks compendium's introduction was not read; Carpenter's *Reading Surimono* (2008) was not opened. No Yale syllabus on Edo prints surfaced in search.

## Key question 2 · Which stories and anecdotes do experts use to make the trade vivid, and which are documented versus legend?

### Takeaway
The best single fact-check found today is the Ōta Memorial Museum's 2021 note by chief curator Hinohara Kenji tracing the "ukiyo-e as packing paper" story to a 1905 memoir and showing how it drifted. Below, fifteen anecdotes with their documentation status; the unit should tell the legends *as legends* with their first printed source.

### Cited Findings
1. **Bracquemond finds the *Hokusai Manga* at Delâtre's (1856).** The earliest source is Léonce Bénédite, "Félix Bracquemond l'animalier", *Art et décoration*, Feb 1905, p. 39: at the printer Delâtre's, Bracquemond found "un petit livre bizarrement broché, à couverture rouge" that had been used **to wedge porcelain** shipped by Frenchmen living in Japan; Delâtre refused to give it up; about a year or eighteen months later Bracquemond obtained it via the engraver Lavieille. Hinohara's conclusions: the object was a **bound volume used as a divider**, not single sheets used as wrapping; "actually 1856" is itself disputed ("some point out it was a few years later"); Pennell's *Life of Whistler* (1908) repeats Bénédite; the "wrapping paper" reading appears in Kojima Usui 1914, Kobayashi Taichirō 1946 and Kondō Ichitarō 1958; Hinohara found **no evidence** that single-sheet ukiyo-e wrapped export ceramics. Status: core episode *documented only in a 1905 secondary memoir*; wrapping-paper version *a misreading*; date *contested* — [Ōta Memorial Museum note (Hinohara, 27 Feb 2021)](https://otakinen-museum.note.jp/n/n01248684801c). Museum labels still tell the packing version plainly, e.g. Minneapolis: "unusual packing material in a crate of porcelain" — documented (label) — [Mia, Bracquemond *The Swallows*](https://collections.artsmia.org/art/54149/the-swallows-felix-bracquemond).
2. **Monet's prints as wrapping paper (Le Havre or Holland).** Ōshima Seiji (1980) traces the Le Havre version to Monet's own later remark to Marc Elder that an Amsterdam merchant threw in a quantity of prints free when he bought Delft jars — i.e. not wrapping. Focillon's *Hokusai* (1925) relays Octave Mirbeau's story of a Dutch grocer wrapping parcels in Hokusai, Utamaro and Kōrin prints. Status: *legend with two incompatible versions* — [Ōta note](https://otakinen-museum.note.jp/n/n01248684801c).
3. **Zola's cheese wrapper** (a cheap nishiki-e, or waste sheets of the *Manga*) — recorded by Kojima Usui in 1931 as "a legend", source unknown. Status: *legend* — [Ōta note](https://otakinen-museum.note.jp/n/n01248684801c).
4. **Van Gogh given prints used as wrapping by a junk dealer** (Émile Bernard's 1891 preface): the Japanese translation says the wrapping was *Chinese pictures*; Hinohara flags the original for checking. Status: *contested* — [Ōta note](https://otakinen-museum.note.jp/n/n01248684801c).
5. **Ukiyo-e were "worthless" in Japan until the West noticed.** Davis's *Picturing the Floating World* (2021) "debunks this myth" with period appraisal documents — documented (publisher's description) — [Penn History of Art](https://arth.sas.upenn.edu/content/picturing-floating-world-ukiyo-e-context).
6. **The Great Wave is a tsunami.** Cartwright and Nakamura (*Notes and Records of the Royal Society*, 2009) conclude it is "more probable that Hokusai intended to depict an exceptionally large storm wave", a plunging breaker; Dudley, Sarano and Dias (2013) add an estimated height "around 10 m", location "3 km offshore", and that tsunamis "propagate ... at small amplitude" offshore, so the breaking crest is not tsunami behaviour; they even suggest the crews may be skilfully "navigating around the wave". Status: *the tsunami reading is a modern misattribution, documented as such* — [Cartwright & Nakamura 2009](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rsnr.2007.0039); [Dudley et al. 2013](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/full/10.1098/rsnr.2012.0066); USGS explainer: "tsunami waves typically do not crest or break" — [USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/media/images/hokusai-under-wave-kanagawa).
7. **Hokusai trained as a block-cutter.** Clark: "Between the ages of 16 and 19, Hokusai was training to cut the woodblocks" — documented (BM curator blog) — [BM blog, 10 May 2019](https://www.britishmuseum.org/blog/hokusai-father-manga).
8. **Hokusai said Ōi's pictures of women were better than his.** Davis: "is reported to have said" — *probable (period hearsay)* — [Davis lecture abstract](https://www.deafspotlight.org/events/saturday-university-katsushika-oi-and-the-hokusai-legacy).
9. **Publishers ordering from "Hokusai" could get Ōi's hand.** A Production I.G research page (for the film *Miss Hokusai*) says a Hokusai letter shows publishers knew an order to "Hokusai" might be executed by father or daughter, some specifically asking for Ōi, with the product signed "Hokusai"; birth-year debate 1791 (Hayashi) vs 1800/01 (most scholars) — *probable (secondary, uncited letter)* — [Production I.G](https://www.productionig.com/contents/works_sp/95_/s08_/001279.html). Ōta: about ten to a dozen confirmed works — documented — [Ōta 2023](https://www.ukiyoe-ota-muse.jp/yoshiwarakoushi-eng/).
10. **Osaka's 1793 monopoly.** Shioya Chōbei's exclusive licence for actor likenesses (8th month 1793), his purchase of a rival's blocks (1794), Ryūkōsai's unsigned protest and withdrawal — documented — [Yano (PDF)](https://www.colorado.edu/cas/sites/default/files/attached-files/akiko_yano_hr.pdf).
11. **Rikan vs Shikan: rival actors, rival theatres, playbills "saying who won", then prints and poems.** Gerstle, 2005 — documented (curator interview) — [The Independent preview](https://www.the-independent.com/arts-entertainment/theatre-dance/features/preview-kabuki-heroes-on-the-osaka-stage-17801830-british-museum-london-308760.html).
12. **"Osaka prints were not so well known even in Osaka" before 2005** — Osaka Museum of History curator Sawai, quoted in the REF case; dealer Ujlaki: some Osaka curators "had no idea that Kamigata-e ... ever existed" — documented — [REF case study](https://impact.ref.ac.uk/casestudies/CaseStudy.aspx?Id=42788).
13. **The state sent the trade abroad in 1889 and named the invisible workers.** The NMAH's Tokunō gift (c. 200 objects: 44 tools, 17 pigments, 21 blocks, 71 progressive proofs for a triptych, seven process watercolours, plus **35 progressive proofs for *Nandina domestica***) names the carver **Kido Kotarō** and printer **Yamamoto Iwakichi** (Nandina), printer **Nakamura Tsurusaburō** (centre sheet) and maker **Morikawa Kokichirō** (right sheet); the triptych is Utagawa Kuniteru II's *Inaka Genji*, publisher Daikokuya (Matsuki) Heikichi, c. 1870; Henry Smith suggests the colour blocks were cut later, possibly for the Smithsonian — documented — [Smithsonian Tokuno gift, about](https://www.si.edu/spotlight/tokuno-gift/about); [NMAH GA.03217.24](https://www.americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_788927); [NMAH GA.03213.05](https://www.americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_793553); centre-sheet manifest naming Nakamura — [IIIF manifest](https://ids.si.edu/ids/manifest/NMAH-ET2012-09716-000003).
14. **"Only the artists are remembered today."** A standard lecture line (Rindsberg, ARTH 2071), with print runs "from 200 to 10,000" and "the price of a moderate lunch" — *probable (teaching slides, unsourced)* — [ARTH 2071 slides (PDF)](https://helen.iglouhost.com/jah/C6B_16.pdf).
15. **Hokusai as "father of manga".** Clark: "Tezuka was watching Disney cartoons as much as he was looking at Hokusai prints" — documented (curator's own caveat) — [BM blog](https://www.britishmuseum.org/blog/hokusai-father-manga).

### Inferences
- Two of the most-repeated Japonisme stories (Bracquemond, Monet) rest on a single 1905 memoir and on Monet's own late recollection respectively; the unit can teach *source criticism* with them rather than drop them.
- The Tokunō gift is the one place where the trade's "nameless" carvers and printers are named in a museum record, because a state bureau documented them for export; that is the anecdote that closes the "invisibility" thread.

### Gaps
- Bénédite's 1905 article and Pennell 1908 were read only as quoted by Hinohara. The Hokusai letter about Ōi was not seen in a primary edition. Eidelberg's Burlington article on Bracquemond and Delâtre was not located (a 1974 Eidelberg Burlington item appeared in search but its subject could not be confirmed).

## Key question 3 · What do learners most commonly misunderstand, and how do experts correct it?

### Takeaway
Nine misunderstandings recur in the sources, and each has an authoritative correction a unit can quote: the single-artist fallacy, the tsunami, the packing paper, "father of manga", "Edo = ukiyo-e", "ukiyo-e = all Japanese prints", "an original is a unique object", the Yoshiwara as a free "safety valve", and "the artist chose the colours and printed it".

### Cited Findings
- **One artist made it.** Birmingham Museum of Art: "it actually took four specialists ... the artist, the publisher, the block carver, and the printer" — [BMA Culture Bridge](https://learn.artsbma.org/resource/quick-guide-to-japanese-woodblock-prints); Asian Art Museum: the artist "was not involved in the printing process", which "could sometimes lead to the artist's dissatisfaction" — [Asian Art Museum education](https://education.asianart.org/resources/the-ukiyo-e-woodblock-printing-process); Wikipedia's synthesis: over a thousand publishers known, peaking "around 250 in the 1840s and 1850s—200 in Edo alone" (secondary) — [Wikipedia, Ukiyo-e](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukiyo-e).
- **It is a tsunami.** See Q2 item 6 — [Cartwright & Nakamura](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rsnr.2007.0039); [USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/media/images/hokusai-under-wave-kanagawa).
- **Prints were worthless packing.** Davis 2021 and the Ōta note (Q2 items 1–5) — [Penn](https://arth.sas.upenn.edu/content/picturing-floating-world-ukiyo-e-context); [Ōta note](https://otakinen-museum.note.jp/n/n01248684801c).
- **Hokusai invented manga.** Clark's own blog title is a question, and he asks readers to "debate the somewhat different meanings of 'manga' in 1814 versus 'manga' in 1947" — [BM blog](https://www.britishmuseum.org/blog/hokusai-father-manga).
- **"Ukiyo-e" means all Japanese woodblock prints.** Graham's review notes the Hotei title creates exactly this expectation while the text covers only the ukiyo-e tradition — [caa.reviews](https://www.caareviews.org/reviews/975).
- **Prints are Edo's art; Osaka had none.** Gerstle/Clark (Q1) — [REF case study](https://impact.ref.ac.uk/casestudies/CaseStudy.aspx?Id=42788).
- **An "original" is one object.** The Hotei devotes an essay to "The original versus the genuine" (Uhlenbeck) and another to "quality, condition and rarity" — [WorldCat contents](https://search.worldcat.org/title/61666175). The NMAH progressive-proof records show the same sheet needing "23 separate impressions ... from 13 printing surfaces on seven blocks" — [NMAH](https://www.americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_793553).
- **The Yoshiwara was a free zone / safety valve.** Rare Book School's course text presents this as the older view "found in many" books and sets out to replace it — [RBS](https://rarebookschool.org/courses/details/i80).
- **Night and shadow were normal subjects.** Ōi's chiaroscuro is described as "totally unseen in any picture made in Japan at the time" (secondary) — [Production I.G](https://www.productionig.com/contents/works_sp/95_/s08_/001279.html); Ōta calls the painting "a masterpiece of ukiyo-e night scenes" — [Ōta 2026](https://www.ukiyoe-ota-muse.jp/katsushikaoi2026-eng/).

### Inferences
- Several corrections are themselves contested at the edges (Osaka's "literati" artists; Ōi's European exposure), so the unit should pair each correction with its confidence word rather than present a new orthodoxy.

### Gaps
- No museum-education page listing "misconceptions" as such was found; the list above is assembled from scattered expert statements.

## Key question 4 · Which open sources give IIIF deep zoom, 3D, georeferenced maps and structured data, and under what exact licence?

### Takeaway
Open, machine-readable, deep-zoomable material exists for every layer the unit needs **except** a fully CC0 Edo-period progressive-proof set and open period audio. Licences read today, in descending openness: CC0 (Met, AIC, Cleveland, Rijksmuseum, Smithsonian CC0-flagged media incl. ~5,000 NMAA images, Mia's Sketchfab netsuke, Smithsonian 3D CC0 set); public domain "free use, no application" (NDL Digital Collections items marked 保護期間満了; Tokyo Metropolitan Library TOKYO Archive items marked パブリックドメイン; LoC); CC BY 4.0 (ColBase images via Government Standard Terms v2.0; CODH Edo-map datasets; Japan Search platform); CC BY-SA (CODH/NIJL classic-book datasets); CC BY-NC-ND (Rekichizu tiles); non-commercial/key-gated (Harvard API; Smithsonian "usage conditions apply" media); permission-only (ARC Ritsumeikan images; Edo-Tokyo Museum Digital Archives).

### Cited Findings

**Images and IIIF (museums)**
- **Art Institute of Chicago**: IIIF Image API 2.0 at base `https://www.artic.edu/iiif/2`; **IIIF Presentation manifests "for all of our public domain artworks"** by appending `/manifest.json` to the artwork endpoint; API data CC0 except `description` (CC BY 4.0); images "may encounter images that are not public domain" so check `is_public_domain` — documented — [AIC API docs](https://api.artic.edu/docs/); open-access page: 50,000+ CC0 images, requested caption "Artist. Title, Date. The Art Institute of Chicago." — [AIC Open Access Images](https://www.artic.edu/open-access/open-access-images).
- **Cleveland Museum of Art**: "more than 64,000 artwork records ... for unrestricted commercial and noncommercial use" and images for "over 37,000 works" under CC0; `share_license_status` is CC0 / Copyrighted / Other and only CC0 records carry CC0 images; daily refresh; GitHub JSON/CSV dump — documented — [CMA Open Access API](https://openaccess-api.clevelandart.org/); [CMA page](https://www.clevelandart.org/open-access-api).
- **The Met**: Open Access since 7 Feb 2017, images of public-domain works CC0, basic data on all works CC0; API launched with "406,000 images"; no attribution required; look for the Open Access icon — documented — [Met Image and Data Resources](https://www.metmuseum.org/policies/image-resources); [Met FAQ](https://www.metmuseum.org/policies/frequently-asked-questions-image-and-data-resources); [Met API article](https://www.metmuseum.org/perspectives/met-collection-api-2). (The Met offers no public IIIF manifests on these pages; the earlier `objects_events.md` documents the direct image URL pattern and the 410 errors on search.)

## Key question 5 · What are the gaps no open source fills (process set, blocks, tools, video, audio), and what workarounds exist?

### Takeaway
There is **no CC0 Edo-period progressive-proof set** anywhere opened; the closest is the Smithsonian's 1889 Tokunō set, which is CC0 in metadata, IIIF-served, and reusable for education under "usage conditions", so it can be embedded for a non-commercial course but not sold. No openly licensed process video or openly licensed period music was found; pre-1923 US recordings (public domain since 1 Jan 2022) are the only legally clean audio route, and one 1902 tokiwazu side exists at the Library of Congress.

### Cited Findings
- **Progressive proofs**: NMAH holds 71 proofs for the *Inaka Genji* triptych and 35 for *Nandina domestica*, plus 21 blocks, 44 tools and 17 pigments, "most ... imaged"; an animated gif of the triptych building up exists on the Smithsonian page — documented — [SI Tokuno about](https://www.si.edu/spotlight/tokuno-gift/about). Rights as in Q4 (CC0 metadata; images "usage conditions apply"; IIIF manifests served) — [NMAH object](https://www.americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_793553). Nothing comparable surfaced at Harvard, Mia, LoC or the Rijksmuseum in today's search (absence of evidence, not proof).
- **The only period process account that is unambiguously public domain** is Tokunō's text as edited by S. R. Koehler for the Smithsonian Annual Report (noted in `objects_events.md` as 1892); the Smithsonian page says scholars (Marks in *Awash in Color*, Smart Museum 2012–13; Henry Smith) have re-studied the set — documented — [SI Tokuno about](https://www.si.edu/spotlight/tokuno-gift/about).
- **Process video**: all substantial films found are under standard YouTube licences, not CC: NMAA's "The Ukiyo-e Technique" (printer explains 14 blocks/15 colours, both-sided 100-year-old cherry, kentō adjustment) — [YouTube, NMAA](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yfqsTy3Yx24); Mokuhankan's real-time printing videos with carver and printer named (e.g. Kawasaki Noriko, Suga Natsuki) — [Mokuhankan falcon video page](https://youlibs.com/video/japanese-woodblock-printmaking-an-ukiyoe-falcon); Art Gallery of NSW with Matsuzaki Keizaburō — [YouTube, AGNSW](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t8uF3PZ3KGQ). Workaround: embed by link/iframe under the platform's terms; do not re-host.
- **Audio**: the LoC National Jukebox states that under the Music Modernization Act "all recordings published prior to 1923 will enter the public domain" on 1 Jan 2022, "free to use and reuse"; a 1902 Victor side *Tokiwazu Modoribashi* ("Female vocal solo, with shamisen", dated 1902-12-31) is in the Jukebox — documented — [LoC jukebox-326857](https://www.loc.gov/item/jukebox-326857/). A 2012 review notes Jukebox audio was stream-only then — [review](https://exa.ai/library/publication/z888bqhg6qn). Later sets (KBS *Album of Japanese Music*, 1940s–53; Ensemble Nipponia 1976) are **not** free — [Archives West](https://archiveswest.orbiscascade.org/ark:80444/xv247619); [Internet Archive Nipponia](https://archive.org/details/soloists-of-the-ensemble-nipponia-japan-o-traditional-vocal-instrumental-music-s). Commons' licensing page requires score, lyrics, performance and recording all to be free — [Commons licensing](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Commons:License). No CC0/CC BY koto or shamisen performance file was located today.
- **Blocks and tools in 3D**: none found CC0; the Mia netsuke and NMAA lacquer netsuke (2D, CC0) are the open 3D/object substitutes — see Q4.
- **Museum workarounds observed**: reconstruction at scale and replicas where originals cannot be shown (Edo-Tokyo Museum, Sumida; recorded in `teaching.md`); the Smithsonian's own gif of proofs; the Ōta's **export night-scene prints** as a way to show the afterlife with its own holdings — [Ōta 2026](https://www.ukiyoe-ota-muse.jp/katsushikaoi2026-eng/).

### Inferences
- For "Peel the colours" the honest design is: our own layer reconstruction from Met data (CC0) as the interactive, with the NMAH IIIF proofs embedded as the real sequence for educational viewing, and the gif/credit line pointing to si.edu. For a commercial release the NMAH images would need permission.
- For audio, a short 1902 tokiwazu excerpt (PD in the US) is defensible for an ambient cue; everything later needs a licence, or a newly commissioned CC0 recording.

### Gaps
- Whether the 1902 LoC side is now downloadable (not just streamable) was not verified. No search was made of Freesound or Musopen today. The Koehler/Tokunō 1892 plates were not opened on Internet Archive today.
