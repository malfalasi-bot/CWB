# Learning-design evidence and course structures for an interactive history/art-history unit (map + timeline + narrative + embedded tools, no typed answers, dense content, everyone learns everything)

Scope note: evidence gathered via Consensus (peer-reviewed index), Exa, Tavily and Parallel search on 2026-10-04. Scite was unavailable (paid plan required) so DOIs are given only where they appeared in retrieved results. Consensus URLs point at the paper record (title, authors, journal, abstract). Quotes are kept to <25 words.

Shorthand for effect sizes: d / g are standardized mean differences (0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large). "System-paced" = the material advances on its own (video, animation); "self/learner-paced" = the reader controls advance (web page, scroll, click).

## Key Question 1 — Segment length, words per screen, and the ratio of reading to doing for adult learners in a dense history unit

### Takeaway
The evidence supports cutting a dense unit into learner-paced, meaningful segments of roughly 3–8 minutes of material each (segmenting d≈0.70; median sustained video engagement ≤6 min), with a short retrieval/prediction check closing most segments; there is no direct experimental evidence on "words per screen", but platform conventions converge on ~280 characters per slide and ≤10–15 slides per story for image-led formats, and ~5–10 minutes with ~5 interactions + a 3-item check per lesson for interaction-led formats. Reading on a scrolling phone screen carries a measurable comprehension penalty (g≈0.35–0.48 vs paper) that disappears when text fits without scrolling, which argues for card-sized text blocks.

### Cited Findings
**Segmenting / chunking**
- Mayer's review of 12 principles reports segmenting d = 0.70, pre-training d = 0.46, signalling d = 0.46, coherence d = 0.70, redundancy d = 0.87, spatial contiguity d = 0.79, temporal contiguity d = 1.30, modality d = 0.72, personalization d = 0.79, voice d = 0.74, multimedia d = 1.67; some principles are stronger for low-knowledge learners — [Mayer 2017, J. Comput. Assist. Learn.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/5ca62b3d23f75783a489de775688f399/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Meta-analysis of the segmenting effect (56 investigations, 88 comparisons): significant small-to-medium effects for retention and transfer; segmentation reduces cognitive load and increases learning time; effects hold for system-paced segmentation; learners with high prior knowledge benefited more for retention — [Rey et al. 2019, Educ. Psychol. Rev.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/af8dc817f0875ce4b8d2d9da27b24741/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Overview of 29 reviews (1,189 studies, 78,177 participants): 11 principles with significant positive effects; largest for captioning L2 videos, temporal/spatial contiguity and signalling; robust evidence for modality, coherence/removing seductive details, segmentation, personalization, verbal redundancy; "Good design was more important for more complex materials, and in system-paced environments" than self-paced websites — [Noetel et al. 2021, Rev. Educ. Res.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/1f7d04c3f664533da5621eee37530353/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Systematic review of 113 instructional-video papers (28 principles): coherence, segmenting and learner control improved learning in varied contexts; redundancy and modality were not supported for video; "shorter, segmented, coherent and paired with learning activities" videos more likely to improve learning — [Fyfield et al. 2022, AJET](https://consensus.app/papers/details/babf68b9f957596086c10c159a30a8fb/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Mayer's third edition (2021) expands the set to 15 principles, adding embodiment, immersion and generative activity; all map to reducing extraneous, managing essential and fostering generative processing; effects often shrink for high-prior-knowledge learners — [Devlin Peck summary citing Mayer 2021/2024](https://www.devlinpeck.com/content/mayers-principles-of-multimedia-learning); table of contents confirming the 15 principles incl. Generative Activity Principle — [Google Books, Multimedia Learning 3rd ed.](https://books.google.com/books/about/Multimedia_Learning.html?id=jMfjDwAAQBAJ)

**How long a segment: video-engagement data**
- Across 6.9 million edX sessions (862 videos, 4 courses), "median engagement time is at most 6 minutes, regardless of total video length"; students often make it less than halfway through videos longer than 9 minutes; 0–3 min videos had the highest engagement (75% of sessions lasted over three-quarters of the video); share of sessions followed by a problem attempt fell with video length: 56%, 48%, 43%, 41%, 31% across five length buckets — [Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014, L@S, PDF](https://kixlab.github.io/website-files/2014/LAS2014-Engagement.pdf); DOI 10.1145/2556325.2566239 — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2556325.2566239)
- Counter-evidence: in 3.1M video views in biomedical courses, students regularly engage over multiple sessions, so single-session metrics underestimate engagement and "limiting videos to only 6 minutes… is not well-grounded"; number of graded assessments tied to a video, position in course and required viewing predicted engagement better than length — [Parker et al. 2024, Comput. Educ.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/fabad2eb470e59ec97e24a177eb96556/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Stanford CS lecture videos (50–75 min): ~90% watched entirety across multiple sessions; median single-session watch 12–13 min, mean 17–20 min; guideline: ideally <12 min, no more than 20 — [UCSD Multimedia summary of Lagerstrom](https://multimedia.ucsd.edu/best-practices/video-length.html)
- YouTube Education analysis (895 German, 524 English channels; 83,558/154,370 videos): short videos up to 14 or 22 min are common practice; segmented videos produced higher learning gains than the same video unsegmented (N=22); "The question of video length is not crucial… as long as the video can be provided in meaningful segments" — [Seidel 2024, Technology, Knowledge and Learning](https://consensus.app/papers/details/131cd522c4bc5b79975e194bbbcb77e9/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Flipped engineering course: short videos raised viewing time 24.7% and final exam score 9.0% vs long-video group — [Zhu et al. 2022, Humanit. Soc. Sci. Commun.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/6e9b1279f6a15f1aa6ddbf0f35ef3916/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Medical flipped classroom (152 students, 173 videos): videos under 5 minutes had higher audience retention and higher response rates to embedded questions — [Gutiérrez-González et al. 2025, Med. Educ. Online](https://consensus.app/papers/details/8a9f5b2803bb55c993fdc2ba9535c80c/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Learner control / pacing**
- Learner-paced segments gave higher test performance with lower cognitive load than system-paced, even though stop/play buttons were rarely used; differences appeared only on high-element-interactivity questions — [Hasler, Kersten & Sweller 2007, Appl. Cogn. Psychol.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/3e5c2b81d2415a85b483b36fa65df418/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Learner pacing replicated the interactivity principle (better transfer) but "coincided with a large increase in time-on-task"; large individual differences in use of controls — [Tabbers & de Koeijer 2009, Instr. Sci.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/a68871bdf9c85cd28c4e1c99823379bd/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Overall learner-control effect in ed-tech is near zero (g = 0.05) with high heterogeneity; adding a progress bar (temporal cue) and a table of contents (organizational cue) raised germane load and transfer; TOC improved topic recall, retention and monitoring accuracy — [Yu et al. 2023, BJET](https://consensus.app/papers/details/a51bd9f7aad455d0ae5531b42036c34b/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Meta-analysis of objective learner control in adult web-based instruction: control types are usually confounded; learner control helps skill training but varies in knowledge training; sequence control is the only type that generally does not harm learning or reactions — [Landers & Reddock 2016, J. Bus. Psychol.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/e3e8790995be5637b16d2221e1d65ffd/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Learner control over pace of a narrated animation improved transfer (not retention) in part-whole and part-part presentations — [Mayer & Chandler 2001, J. Educ. Psychol.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/ddc800e528ca560f8e2e8e247d83bd38/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Reading vs doing**
- Adding video to existing teaching: g = 0.80; swapping video for existing methods: g = 0.28 (105 RCTs, 7,776 students) — [Noetel et al. 2020, Rev. Educ. Res.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/668daad647b05fbda55adfc0c0b62cef/?utm_source=claude_desktop); critique argues benefits apply mainly to lower-level cognitive processing — [Trenholm et al. 2024, Educ. Sci.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/856e2b749b0b5c0ebf1127951bf48ba1/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- With learning time controlled (N=128), neither navigation features nor self-explanation prompts improved performance; prompts increased intrinsic and extraneous load; answer quality was low but correlated with comprehension — [Beege et al. 2024, Instr. Sci.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/800d6cf11c165211844e27dbc94a036b/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Explainer formats compared (N=381): scrollytelling and video produced significantly more recall than audio and partly text; "when considering exposure time, text turns out to be the most efficient medium" — [Schneiders 2020, Media and Communication](https://consensus.app/papers/details/cd639afb2ae5543cb568ef6eeb00d7d9/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Screen / mobile reading of long-form (relevant to words-per-screen)**
- Screen vs paper: g = −0.25 overall; limited to expository texts (g = −0.32), none for narrative (g = −0.04); readers better calibrated on paper (g = 0.20) — [Clinton 2019, J. Res. Reading](https://consensus.app/papers/details/52817a59c9f152cbb0d9195b5414cbaf/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Paper advantage g = −0.21 (171,055 participants); larger under time constraints; consistent for informational texts, absent for narrative only — [Delgado et al. 2018, Educ. Res. Rev.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/b75e0f68dd9d5900a4eca72e7b424498/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Network meta-analysis (56 studies): paper > tablets > e-readers > computers > smartphones; when scrolling was required paper was notably better (g 0.35–0.48); when no scrolling was needed differences were small (g 0.03–0.12) — [Clinton-Lisell et al. 2026, Educ. Inf. Technol.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/0f3221d886fe5edc8ee58c2bcb6dcd69/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Handheld devices vs print: small effect favouring print (between g = −0.113; within g = −0.103), larger for undergraduates — [Salmerón et al. 2023, J. Educ. Psychol.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/f3c0fd005d8d5d258f017bddd8418e9a/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Narrative texts: no screen penalty; "multimedia and interactive functions of digital texts affect reading comprehension positively" — [Schwabe et al. 2022, Media Psychol.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/302e39d9674459eda420592ce99c3ec3/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Platform conventions (words/screen and lesson size)**
- Google Arts & Culture Story Editor guidance: keep to 280 characters per slide and a maximum of 10–15 slides per story "for the best reading experience"; start with full/half-screen components; suggested video ~90 seconds — [Google Arts & Culture Platform Help](https://support.google.com/culturalinstitute/partners/answer/10460872?hl=en)
- Brilliant: lessons are "usually 5–10 minutes", a short sequence of interactive problems plus a Skills Check; a typical lesson has about 5 interactive problems plus a 3-problem skill check; a level ≈ 6 lessons + a 10–15 problem review; a course = 13 levels — [Brilliant help page](https://brilliant.org/help/schools-and-educators/how-brilliant-fits-into-a-math-lesson)
- Duolingo: material split into "bite-sized" chunks to decrease perceived effort; "a lesson takes no more than a few minutes" — [Duolingo Method whitepaper 2023](https://duolingo-papers.s3.amazonaws.com/reports/Duolingo_whitepaper_duolingo_method_2023.pdf)
- Crash Course: series generally 46 episodes of ~10-minute videos; formula = hook, credits, main narrative, "thought bubble" on one idea/person, conclusion + preview; consultant condensed each 50-minute lecture into a 10-minute highlight reel — [Marsh & Johnson 2020, Isis](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/711083); World History: 72 episodes, 12 min each, 14 h 24 min total — [myshows listing](https://en.myshows.me/view/54236/); running time 6–15 min (2–4 min for Kids) — [Wikipedia: Crash Course](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crash_Course_(web_series))
- Kurzgesagt videos normally 4–16 minutes — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Said_shortly); recent uploads average ~12:08 — [OutlierKit channel analysis](https://outlierkit.com/channel/kurzgesagt)
- MasterClass: courses 2–5 hours split into ~20 lessons; average lesson ~10 min (range 8–20 min across listed courses) — [Cursums review](https://cursums.com/masterclass-review/); typical lessons 10–15 min, courses 2–4 h — [Edwize](https://edwize.org/how-long-is-a-masterclass-course/)

### Inferences
- A 60–90 minute unit should be built as 8–14 learner-paced segments of ~4–8 minutes each (Guo median ≤6 min; Szpunar-type studies use ~5-min and 7–8-min segments; Brilliant/Duolingo lessons 5–10 min). Segment boundaries should be meaningful (coherent sub-stories), not arbitrary time cuts (Rey 2019; Seidel 2024).
- "Words per screen" has no experimental literature; the safest proxy is the mobile-scrolling penalty: keep each text block short enough to be read without scrolling on a phone (Clinton-Lisell 2026), which in practice is near the GAC 280-character convention for image-led cards, or 2–4 short paragraphs for narrative cards.
- Because our audience is self-selected adults with varied prior knowledge, segmenting helps the knowledgeable (retention, Rey 2019) while pre-training/glossary cards help novices (Mayer).
- Ratio of reading to doing: evidence favours interleaving a low-cost check every segment rather than large interactive blocks; expensive generative prompts (typed self-explanations) do not pay off when time is controlled (Beege 2024) and typing is excluded by our format anyway.
- Provide a progress indicator and a visible table of contents (unit map) — the two cues shown to make learner control pay off (Yu 2023).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study on optimal word count per screen/card for adult expository reading on mobile; only platform conventions.
- No experimental study of segment length specifically for history/art-history content; all evidence is STEM, medicine or generic.
- Great Courses lecture length and Domestika lesson structure were not retrieved in this pass (no sourced figures).

## Key Question 2 — Narration (audio) + visuals vs on-screen text + visuals: when does each win, and what about both together?

### Takeaway
Narration + visuals beats on-screen text + visuals mainly when the material is system-paced, short and uses dynamic pictures (modality d≈0.43–0.59 under those conditions; only ~0.24 overall); for a self-paced, scroll-driven page the modality advantage largely disappears, and for second-language readers (our worldwide English audience) text or narration+text wins. Verbatim narration + identical on-screen text + pictures is harmful (redundancy d≈0.87), but key-term/summary text with narration, or slightly re-worded text, is beneficial.

### Cited Findings
- Modality meta-analysis (43 effects): supported, with element interactivity and pacing as moderators; "strong observed modality effect under system-paced conditions" must be weighed against production cost — [Ginns 2005, Learning and Instruction](https://consensus.app/papers/details/f540bfa1ce9a5665858ae93a5f643754/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Modality meta-analysis (91 studies, 8,088 participants): narration > visual text on retention d = 0.24 and transfer d = 0.25; mainly under system-paced presentation (d = 0.43/0.44), dynamic pictures (d = 0.50/0.59) and short materials (d = 0.38/0.33); no reverse modality effect found — [Wang et al. 2016](https://consensus.app/papers/details/0dda3eb1b36b51aa8d1da62aa4e65b67/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Redundancy: adding on-screen text that summarized or duplicated narration to a narrated animation worsened retention and transfer — [Mayer, Heiser & Lonn 2001, J. Educ. Psychol.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/469b7d4c194252cdb81174d1f3bece38/?utm_source=claude_desktop); Mayer reports redundancy d = 0.87 — [Mayer 2017](https://consensus.app/papers/details/5ca62b3d23f75783a489de775688f399/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Verbal redundancy meta-analysis (57 studies): spoken+written vs written-only did not differ; spoken+written > spoken-only, especially for low-prior-knowledge learners, system-paced materials and picture-free materials; key-terms text extracted from narration outperformed verbatim text — [Adesope & Nesbit 2012, J. Educ. Psychol.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/c49f18068cfe535fbe566b5624dcb5ff/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Students comprehended a narrated explanation better with added on-screen text "provided there was no other concurrent visual material" — [Moreno & Mayer 2002, J. Educ. Psychol.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/a1249bc40aac5d6e8416f77476c16d13/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Identical on-screen text and narration impaired learning, but a small amount of discrepancy between them improved recall and transfer; too much discrepancy was as bad as identical text; learners preferred identical text despite it being worse — [Yue, Bjork & Bjork 2013, J. Educ. Psychol.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/9c8518d61c0c590fa9e73d5a1d3d76a5/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Korean students, 16-min English video: video + text scored higher than video + narration (contrary to modality) and video + narration + text outscored video + narration (contrary to redundancy); text versions rated less difficult — [Lee & Mayer 2018, Appl. Cogn. Psychol.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/5cf5ab3a24035836a4baf6151372d2b7/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Foreign-accented narration: with a native narrator, no-text beat summary text on transfer (redundancy effect); with an accented narrator, full text improved retention (word-level decoding) but not transfer — [Chan et al. 2020, Learning and Instruction](https://consensus.app/papers/details/315a14e7e7e85adea1c7c3c464c2f264/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Captioning second-language videos was among the largest benefits in the meta-meta-analysis — [Noetel et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/1f7d04c3f664533da5621eee37530353/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Literature review of 63 redundancy studies distinguishes content redundancy (positive, moderated by prior knowledge) from working-memory-channel redundancy: negative for visualization + written text, positive for narration + written text — [Trypke, Stebner & Wirth 2023, Front. Psychol.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/6fb0fa8b966a56a5a6fe504795a7b54e/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- For instructional video specifically, redundancy and modality principles "are not supported" in the 113-paper review — [Fyfield et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/babf68b9f957596086c10c159a30a8fb/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Seductive details were directly harmful under text but had an indirect motivational compensatory effect under narration — [Park et al. 2015, Comput. Hum. Behav.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/45e4c260542a5af1a4f210948beda200/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Mayer reports voice principle d = 0.74 and personalization d = 0.79 (conversational style) — [Mayer 2017](https://consensus.app/papers/details/5ca62b3d23f75783a489de775688f399/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

### Inferences
- For a self-paced, scroll-driven unit with a sticky map/timeline, the default channel should be on-screen text + visuals: the modality advantage is a system-paced phenomenon and vanishes or reverses for non-native readers, who are a large share of a worldwide English audience.
- Narration adds most value in the short, system-paced moments (e.g., an animated map transition or a 60–90 s "watch this happen" clip). In those moments avoid putting a full transcript beside the animation; use key-term labels or a slightly re-worded caption (Adesope; Yue).
- Offer narration as an optional layer with captions/transcript toggle rather than the primary carrier, since redundancy is only harmful when pictures compete for the visual channel at the same time (Moreno & Mayer; Trypke).
- Use a conversational, personal voice in narration and text; if narration is produced cheaply (TTS or non-native speaker), expect the voice effect to cut into transfer (Chan 2020), making text primary even more sensible for a zero-cost product.

### Gaps
- No study tested narration vs text for map/timeline-based history content with adult lifelong learners.
- No evidence retrieved on Japanese-name pronunciation or bilingual labelling effects.

## Key Question 3 — Do one-tap predictions/retrieval checks help, and where should they sit (before, during, after a segment)? What about the "argued" two-readings format?

### Takeaway
Yes: pre-questions produce a moderate benefit for the specific content asked about (g≈0.54) but almost none for untested content (g≈0.04); predictions before a reveal boost memory specifically for surprising outcomes; interpolated retrieval checks between segments reduce mind-wandering and improve integration across segments (effects small-to-moderate and variable); covert (unanswered-in-writing) pre-questions work as well as typed ones, which licences one-tap items. Recognition items yield smaller testing benefits than recall, so one-tap checks should be designed to require a decision (prediction, ordering, placing on map) rather than passive recognition.

### Cited Findings
**Pretesting / prequestions (before a segment)**
- Preregistered meta-analysis: prequestions produce a moderate specific effect (g = 0.54, k = 97) but a "virtually nonexistent general effect (g = 0.04, k = 91)"; attention-based account supported — [St. Hilaire, Carpenter & Jonsson 2023, Psychon. Bull. Rev.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/ae47f3256d405351ba11ef75d04f388d/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Review: pretesting benefits learning "if there is an opportunity to study the correct answers afterwards"; demonstrated with texts, videos, lectures, with/without feedback; three-stage framework; extent varies with procedure — [Pan & Carpenter 2023, Educ. Psychol. Rev.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/8d88a6d146c65df182d1bcda7966b8f3/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Learners generally lack awareness of pretesting benefits and believe reading is better even after experiencing both — [Pan et al. 2023, Mem. Cognit.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/bc694ac90ab25058b34d1692caa8623c/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Pretests reduced mind-wandering and improved final-test performance during a 26-min online lecture, whether interspersed throughout or given all at the start — [Pan et al. 2020, J. Appl. Res. Mem. Cogn.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/8aadeb7e35ae5df98d21947debeea2b1/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Classroom RCT: prequestions improved end-of-class memory only for the prequestioned information; a week later both groups benefited equally from end-of-class retrieval, so "prequestions add little, if any, enhancement" beyond retrieval practice — [Carpenter et al. 2018, J. Exp. Psychol. Appl.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/4b3118294fdb53919ed2593326233846/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Covert (mentally generated) prequestions were as effective as overt typed ones; prequestioning was as effective as reviewing preview statements — [Rivers et al. 2026, Appl. Cogn. Psychol.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/7cefb4970f7555e4bb4562426db79f3f/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Predictions (commit before reveal)**
- Generating a prediction (vs post-hoc evaluation) improved geography-fact learning; pupillary surprise appeared only after a prediction and predicted amount learned; predictions specifically improved memory for expectancy-violating events — [Brod, Hasselhorn & Bunge 2018, Learning and Instruction](https://consensus.app/papers/details/dbb6eb136ec658568e53fdb7bb2d5544/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Explicit prediction before numerical facts boosted memory for highly unexpected outcomes (U-shaped relation); post-hoc condition memory fell with unexpectedness — [Brod & Breitwieser 2022, Psychon. Bull. Rev.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/6aa6fa6943df527787f658eb24052f4d/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Predicting is argued to be a learning strategy in its own right: it changes how feedback is processed, boosting surprise and attention to the correct answer — [Brod 2021, Psychon. Bull. Rev.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/e4a58fa6dc025e23be1b3b24e17d835a/?utm_source=claude_desktop); generating a prediction stimulated curiosity and curiosity predicted memory — [Brod & Breitwieser 2019, npj Sci. Learn.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/57120b7c2a6c5e95b5879c36900d688d/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- In 7-year-olds, retrieval practice gave better overall outcomes but predictions raised attention to materials (d = 1.92); outcomes equal among attentive children — [Carvalho et al. 2024, J. Exp. Psychol. Appl.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/234539087f335235b2444e030eb121b7/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Retrieval checks during/after segments**
- Interpolating online lectures with memory tests reduced mind-wandering, encouraged note-taking, improved learning, and reduced anxiety about the final test — [Szpunar, Khan & Schacter 2013, PNAS](https://consensus.app/papers/details/4f799cd5603f52bcaf9c87199bf36fe2/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Interpolated tests in a 40-min lecture improved overall learning and integration of information both within segments and across the whole lecture; tested group reported more lecture-related thoughts — [Jing, Szpunar & Schacter 2016, J. Exp. Psychol. Appl.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/6db4882c16985de7af0b905a8637cf07/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Conceptual replication (N=195): interpolated testing reduced task-unrelated thought only with a small effect; no effect on learning; content-matched pretesting had no effect — [Welhaf et al. 2022, Cogn. Res. Princ. Implic.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/8e95d77e88af500da8c3840e42c5f479/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Korean study with 7–8 min segments: inserting interim tests lowered mind-wandering and improved immediate and 2-week delayed test scores — [Seo et al. 2020](https://consensus.app/papers/details/2d10ca8dff62579886bbb3774a56d9aa/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Classroom quizzing meta-analysis (222 studies, 48,478 students): g = 0.499; moderated by control strategy, format consistency, corrective feedback, repetitions and timing — [Yang et al. 2021, Psychol. Bull.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/d5865595581856df817e7452b18cbdc4/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Testing-effect meta-analysis: "initial recall tests yielding larger testing benefits than recognition tests" — [Rowland 2014, Psychol. Bull.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/04c7eb0ad2bf5eb6b122f94da91c5edb/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Transfer of test-enhanced learning: d = 0.40 vs restudy; strongest to application/inference questions, weakest to rearranged stimulus–response items and untested materials; response congruency, elaborated retrieval and initial test success strongly influence transfer — [Pan & Rickard 2018, Psychol. Bull.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/c5cee08032d85fd58244269b72d268a6/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Practice tests outperform restudy and all comparison conditions; moderated by test features and feedback — [Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan 2017, Rev. Educ. Res.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/33b375207e2f535a8d027feebc9600a6/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Guo et al.: post-video problem attempts dropped from 56% to 31% as video length rose — [Guo et al. 2014](https://kixlab.github.io/website-files/2014/LAS2014-Engagement.pdf)

**Guided vs discovery; worked examples (relevant to how much to tell before asking)**
- Unassisted discovery vs explicit instruction d = −0.38 (580 comparisons); enhanced/assisted discovery vs other instruction d = +0.30 (360 comparisons); "feedback, worked examples, scaffolding, and elicited explanations" help — [Alfieri et al. 2011, J. Educ. Psychol.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/277f0240046f5523b67f3cec440855bf/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Minimally guided instruction is less effective and efficient; advantage of guidance recedes only with high prior knowledge — [Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006, Educ. Psychol.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/9e307e8df28d5137867aeed6851419c5/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Guidance in inquiry learning: learning activities d = 0.66, performance success d = 0.71, outcomes d = 0.50 — [Lazonder & Harmsen 2016, Rev. Educ. Res.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/78c7d409d16150a7b93aba243cbef32c/?utm_source=claude_desktop); rebuttal arguing inquiry + direct instruction combined is often best — [de Jong et al. 2023, Educ. Res. Rev.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/261dac178bbb583aa657591b98f91ecc/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Worked examples in mathematics: g = 0.48; adding self-explanation prompts was a negative moderator; correct examples alone best — [Barbieri et al. 2023, Educ. Psychol. Rev.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/d59268ae831952f597db8ed574c9c1ed/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- In less-structured tasks (legal case reasoning) worked examples beat problem-solving for both novices and advanced students; no expertise-reversal — [Nievelstein et al. 2013, Contemp. Educ. Psychol.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/b097912b94af57218e5a6dfdec2a451a/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Multiple/argued readings in history**
- 246 students, 3 weeks: students who read multiple texts scored higher on history content and used sourcing and corroboration more than textbook readers — [Nokes, Dole & Hacker 2007, J. Educ. Psychol.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/34544af030fc5324a018263454f9bba4/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Reading Like a Historian (236 11th-graders, 6 months): significant effects on historical thinking, transfer to contemporary issues, factual knowledge and reading comprehension — [Reisman 2012, Cognition and Instruction](https://consensus.app/papers/details/24ebc232b74354ca93c70847c281a93c/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Students given intertextual aids wrote more integrated essays from 5 conflicting Civil War texts; no difference in single-text comprehension — [García-Serrano et al. 2023, Panta Rei](https://consensus.app/papers/details/00200a6953045571955fbc48c577e4ca/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Argument schemes and critical questions during discussion of 3 historical controversies improved historical content learning and reasoning quality — [Wissinger & De La Paz 2015, J. Educ. Psychol.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/f4e7ed38bae05403b81e1cda34aa3dae/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- College students reading conflicting Tonkin Gulf accounts shifted to seeing history texts "as arguments rather than truth" (12 of 13) — [Hynd et al. 2004, J. Lit. Res.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/4ee030e038005d86a438b673ead2d799/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Presence of primary documents changed how students rated and reasoned about documents — [Rouet et al. 1996, J. Educ. Psychol.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/62d6c1e975bd50618cba178342ac65fb/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Students trained in historical thinking sourced and corroborated more but tended to rate sources as a binary reliable/unreliable and to corroborate pairs rather than build a narrative — [Jay 2021, Cognition and Instruction](https://consensus.app/papers/details/b4addb8bfa765571919b13eb3e5195f3/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

### Inferences
- Placement: (1) open each segment with one one-tap prediction on the segment's most surprising fact (Brod: the benefit concentrates on expectancy-violating outcomes; St. Hilaire: benefit is specific to the asked content, so target the fact you most want remembered); (2) close each segment with 1–2 one-tap retrieval items that require a decision (place on map, order on timeline, choose cause) rather than yes/no recognition (Rowland); (3) add a short cumulative check at unit end that re-asks earlier items (Jing: integration across segments; Carpenter: end-of-session retrieval drove 1-week retention).
- Always reveal the answer with a one-line explanation immediately (pretesting works only with subsequent study of the correct answer; Yang: corrective feedback moderates).
- Expect modest, not transformative, effects per check (Welhaf) — the main value is attention regulation and encoding of the targeted facts, so keep checks to ~20–30 seconds and do not let them bloat the unit.
- The "argued" two-readings format is consistent with the strongest history-education evidence (multiple conflicting accounts improve both content knowledge and reasoning) but requires explicit intertextual scaffolding — show side-by-side claims, name the sourcing/corroboration move, and ask the learner to tap which reading a given piece of evidence supports. Without that scaffold, learners tend to flip to binary reliable/unreliable judgments (Jay 2021).
- Because unassisted discovery underperforms (Alfieri; Kirschner), embedded tools (map/timeline explorers) should be preceded by a worked example: show one complete "reading" of the map before asking the learner to make one.

### Gaps
- No study of a one-tap "which of two readings does this evidence support?" item format; evidence is for essay/discussion-based multiple-document instruction with school students.
- No direct comparison of one-tap recognition vs tap-to-place (map/timeline) retrieval formats.
- Pretesting literature is mostly STEM/psychology lectures with undergraduates; no adult-lifelong-learner history replication found.

## Key Question 4 — Scrollytelling with a sticky map/timeline: does the parallel instrument help or distract, and how should transitions be paced?

### Takeaway
Scrollytelling reliably increases engagement and perceived understanding; its effect on measured comprehension/recall is neutral-to-positive and depends on layout and linking: explicit text-to-visual linking raises engagement and (with slideshow layout) recall, slideshow/stepped layouts beat continuous vertical scrolling for comprehension in one 180-person study, animated transitions raise engagement while continuous vs discrete control does not matter, and interactivity can hide content that readers then skip. Spatial/temporal contiguity are among the largest multimedia effects, so a sticky instrument that stays aligned with the text it illustrates is well-founded, but the parallel panel must change only when the text refers to it.

### Cited Findings
- Design space of narrative visualization (author-driven vs reader-driven, "martini glass", interactive slideshow, drill-down) — [Segel & Heer 2010, IEEE TVCG](https://consensus.app/papers/details/f1bb1f26ac0d5e86ac762a834fe46a61/?utm_source=claude_desktop) (DOI 10.1109/TVCG.2010.179 cited in Zhi et al.)
- 42 professional narrative visualizations analysed; transition-cost model; "support for memory and subjective rating benefits of visualization sequences that use parallelism" — [Hullman et al. 2013, IEEE TVCG](https://consensus.app/papers/details/742421aef621520b82cdff5be46e790b/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- 80 stories analysed into seven flow-factors; crowdsourced study (N=240): "visuals and navigation feedback (e.g., static vs. animated transitions) have an impact on readers' engagement, while level of control (e.g., discrete vs. continuous) may not" — [McKenna et al. 2017, Computer Graphics Forum](https://consensus.app/papers/details/2f4e146279a15c1fafdddee07467cd5b/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- 180-participant study: comprehension significantly better with slideshow layout than vertical; recall better with slideshow + linking; bidirectional text–visual linking significantly increased engagement and was preferred — [Zhi, Ottley & Metoyer 2019, Computer Graphics Forum](https://consensus.app/papers/details/a5fa9a98d4d75aa290901f45dc7c8334/?utm_source=claude_desktop); DOI 10.1111/cgf.13719 — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cgf.13719)
- Three web field experiments: adding introductory "stories" to exploratory visualizations did not increase exploration — [Boy, Detienne & Fekete 2015, CHI](https://consensus.app/papers/details/56db60f9eba056c7a00d00859abf21f7/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Map-based stories (N=125, 2×2×2): genre and, more weakly, tropes influenced retention; participants performed better with longform infographic/"scrollytelling" than dynamic slideshow and with leader lines; theme did not affect retention — [Song et al. 2022, Cartographic Perspectives](https://consensus.app/papers/details/9cc0ce3893865c7f82e4e8ce69cee745/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Eye-tracking (N=45) of science news with static vs interactive vs interactive-animated map visualization: interactive-animated drew the longest attention and increased fixation on the following text, which mediated higher knowledge acquisition; but participants in the static condition looked at significantly more of the nine sub-diagrams (M = 8.4) than in the interactive condition (M = 4.9); 94% watched animations to the end — [Greussing, Kessler & Boomgaarden 2020](https://exa.ai/library/publication/g5twrs95yhv)
- Within-subject test (n=12, under-35s): significant difference in perceived engagement favouring scrollytelling on 9 of 11 statements; strength is emotional response and fundamental understanding; should be avoided when readers want to find specific information — [Impact of Scrollytelling on Long-Form Journalism, ACM 2023](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3605655.3605683) (DOI 10.1145/3605655.3605683); the same paper cites Seyser & Zeiller 2018, DOI 10.1109/iV.2018.00075
- Swedish thesis prototype comparison: scrollytelling increased engagement and perceived understanding, but the knowledge test showed slightly better retention for the static format — [Erixon & Björklind 2024](https://exa.ai/library/publication/80d0y95dph3)
- HPV pilot (n=24): scrollytelling users made fewer comprehension errors (28% vs 39%), not significant; similar NASA-TLX workload; higher hedonic UX ratings — [Scrolling with Purpose pilot](https://showreel.mdh.ustp.at/eposter/scrolling-with-purpose-a-pilot-study-on-usability-and-comprehension-effects-of-scrollytelling-in-hpv-vaccination-communication/)
- Privacy-policy study (N=454): scrollytelling gave higher engagement, lower cognitive load and greater perceived clarity than text, and matched other formats on comprehension accuracy — [Such 2026, CHI](https://consensus.app/papers/details/74e06d5190725cc19265b64557135401/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- UX-designer onboarding (N=25): interactive (scrollytelling or chatbot) conditions had higher guideline adherence and engagement; comprehension did not differ — [Chen et al. 2026, arXiv](https://consensus.app/papers/details/18a7d2f243d75bd99ac29254e22fee74/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Data storytelling vs traditional visualization: no significant differences in short- or long-term recall — [Zdanovic et al. 2022, CHIIR](https://consensus.app/papers/details/3e679fb9cb1c5fe390689f259171e502/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Scrollytelling and video produced more recall than audio and partly text; text most efficient per unit time — [Schneiders 2020](https://consensus.app/papers/details/cd639afb2ae5543cb568ef6eeb00d7d9/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Timeline storytelling design space: 14 design choices in representation, scale, layout; 20 viable combinations; "smooth animated transitions between narrative points allow for the presentation of a cohesive story" — [Brehmer et al. 2017, IEEE TVCG](https://consensus.app/papers/details/fa89113261fc533d851a22d15be0a785/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Scrollytelling authoring with interactive maps and scrolling as "familiar gesture" giving control and discoverability; 12-participant study — [Mörth et al. 2022, IEEE TVCG (ScrollyVis)](https://consensus.app/papers/details/71626a103d5f5605be0847b2b6c90818/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Contiguity effects (keeping words next to the pictures they describe; presenting them simultaneously) are among the largest in multimedia research: spatial d = 0.79, temporal d = 1.30 — [Mayer 2017](https://consensus.app/papers/details/5ca62b3d23f75783a489de775688f399/?utm_source=claude_desktop); temporal/spatial contiguity among the largest benefits — [Noetel et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/1f7d04c3f664533da5621eee37530353/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Signalling (cues to the critical element) d = 0.38 across 29 experiments, larger in higher-quality studies — [Alpizar, Adesope & Wong 2020, ETR&D](https://consensus.app/papers/details/acf7fda6dc485737a4b158c22d2757d0/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Narrative visualizations can frame interpretation through additions/omissions at data, visual, annotation and interactivity levels — [Hullman & Diakopoulos 2011, IEEE TVCG](https://consensus.app/papers/details/23448345b33a529695c28672532f9359/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

### Inferences
- The sticky map/timeline is justified as a contiguity device: it keeps the referent next to the sentence that mentions it. It becomes a distraction when it moves or animates while the reader is still reading unrelated text (split attention), or when it offers exploration that hides essential content behind taps (Greussing: interactive readers saw ~5 of 9 panels vs ~8 of 9 static).
- Pacing rule derived from the evidence: one visual state per text step; change the instrument only at a step boundary that the text explicitly references; animate the transition (McKenna: animated feedback raises engagement) but keep it short and signal the changed element (Alpizar); don't rely on continuous scroll-scrubbing because discrete vs continuous control did not affect engagement and stepped layouts aided comprehension (Zhi).
- Prefer a stepped ("martini-glass"/slideshow-like) structure inside each segment with linked highlighting between text and map/timeline; allow free exploration only at segment ends (Boy et al.: stories do not by themselves make people explore, so make exploration optional and the author-driven path complete on its own).
- Essential content must never live only inside an optional interaction; everyone must learn everything from the linear path.
- Expect scrollytelling to raise engagement and perceived understanding more than measured recall; pair it with the retrieval checks in KQ3 to convert engagement into retention.

### Gaps
- No study specifically by "Dou et al." on scrollytelling was found; the closest matches are Zhi et al. 2019, McKenna et al. 2017, Seyser & Zeiller 2018 and Mittenentzwei et al. 2023 (medical visualization; abstract not retrievable).
- No controlled study of a persistent (sticky) map+timeline dual panel versus a single panel; all evidence is single-visualization stories.
- No published guidance with numbers on transition duration or scroll distance per step; only qualitative design-space work (Brehmer; McKenna).

## Key Question 5 — How do the best courses structure a 60–90 minute unit, and what completion data exists?

### Takeaway
Leading free/low-cost formats converge on modules of ~1–1.5 hours built from 5–10 min items (videos 1–12 min, readings ~10 min each, one short check per item or per module), with whole courses of 5–7 hours; completion data for open online courses is poor (3–6% of all registrants; ~46–56% of paying/verified learners), while image-led museum stories and short interactive lessons publish only reach (views, enrolments, ratings), not completion.

### Cited Findings
**Smarthistory / Khan Academy art history**
- 60 million views in 2024; 50 editors; 800 academic contributors; 3,500 essays and videos; 335,000 YouTube subscribers; 60 museum partners; 600 institutions link to it; official provider of art history for khanacademy.org; CC non-commercial, ad-free — [Smarthistory: Our mission](https://smarthistory.org/about/)
- Videos are unscripted conversations between experts recorded in front of the artwork; 880+ videos and 2,000+ essays; Webby Award for Education 2009 — [Wikipedia: Smarthistory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smarthistory)
- In 2016 Smarthistory (two staff) had more YouTube subscribers than every US/UK art museum except MoMA and Tate; 13.5 million views in 2015; 224 assets published Jan 2015–Mar 2016 generated 2.72 million views; the Met's Timeline of Art History averaged one million visits per month in FY2015 — [Smarthistory blog 2016](https://smarthistoryblog.org/2016/04/08/how-smarthistory-stacks-up-and-why-museums-should-care-about-youtube/)

**MoMA on Coursera**
- Modern Art & Ideas: 492,131 enrolled; 6,961 reviews; 6 modules; "7 hours to complete"; module 1 = 1 video (1 min) + 5 readings (50 min total, ~10 min each) + 1 assignment (15 min); a content module = 7 videos totalling 20 minutes + 6 readings — [Coursera course page](https://www.coursera.org/learn/modern-art-ideas); the 3-course specialization is rated 4.8 from 12,528 reviews — [Coursera specialization](https://www.coursera.org/specializations/modern-contemporary-art-design); described as a 5-week course requiring ~2 hours of study and one quiz per week — [Open Culture](https://www.openculture.com/2018/01/enroll-in-seven-free-courses-from-the-museum-of-modern-art.html); MoMA says courses use video/audio from artists and curators, readings for context, "quizzes and discussion forums offer opportunities to check your knowledge" — [MoMA online courses](https://www.moma.org/teaching/classes)

**Khan Academy Big History (OER Project)**
- Course organised as units of 4–6 lessons (Unit 1: 4 lessons; Unit 2: 6; Unit 4: 5; Unit 5: 6) with explicit skills ("scale switching and claim testing") — [OER Project: Big History](https://www.oerproject.com/Big-History); mirrored on Khan Academy as unit/lesson/video hierarchy — [Khan Academy Unit 1](https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/oer-project-big-history/xc3079f0f2dd307e4:what-is-big-history)

**Crash Course / Kurzgesagt / MasterClass / Brilliant / Duolingo / Google Arts & Culture** — see KQ1 citations: Crash Course ~10–12 min episodes in 42–72-episode series with a fixed episode formula; Kurzgesagt 4–16 min; MasterClass ~10-min lessons, 2–5 h courses; Brilliant 5–10 min lessons of ~5 interactive problems + 3-item check, 6 lessons per level; Duolingo lessons of a few minutes with spaced repetition; GAC stories 10–15 slides, 280 characters each, ~90 s videos.

**Explorables**
- Nicky Case's workshop notes: "Use interactivity only when interactivity works best, otherwise, supplement it with text & images"; start with a hook that needs no prior knowledge; embed "homework problems" in the explanation — [Nicky Case blog 2014](https://blog.ncase.me/explorable-explanations); further patterns include starting shallow and going deeper — [Nicky Case blog 2018](https://blog.ncase.me/explorable-explanations-4-more-design-patterns); Case's site stack lists spaced-repetition flashcards (Orbit) and expandable explanations (Nutshell) as components — [Nicky Case: The Plan](https://blog.ncase.me/explorable-explanations-the-plan)
- Ciechanowski publishes long interactive articles about science and engineering with inline simulations and narrative text — [ciechanow.ski](https://ciechanow.ski/)

**Museum learning resources**
- Met Heilbrunn Timeline of Art History: thematic, chronological and geographical; "more than 1,000 essays and 300 chronologies" authored by Met experts — [Met Museum](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/timeline-of-art-history); launched 3 October 2000 — [Met press release](https://www.metmuseum.org/press-releases/metropolitan-museum-of-art-debuts-timeline-of-art-history-on-its-web-site-october-3-2000-exhibitions)
- Google Arts & Culture hosts museum-authored stories, e.g., Tokugawa Art Museum's Emakimono story, with a single-paragraph text per slide — [GAC story](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/emakimono-illustrated-handscrolls-the-tokugawa-art-museum/QQVRePrklPIhJg?hl=en)

**Completion and satisfaction data**
- HarvardX/MITx 2012–2018 (5.63 million learners, 12.67 million registrations): 3.13% of all participants completed in 2017–18, down from ~4% the two prior years and ~6% in 2014–15; among verified learners 46% completed in 2017–18 vs 56% in 2016–17 — [Inside Higher Ed on Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente 2019](https://www.insidehighered.com/digital-learning/article/2019/01/16/study-offers-data-show-moocs-didnt-achieve-their-goals); the Science article states most MOOC learners never return after their first year and low completion rates "has not improved over 6 years" — [Reich & Ruipérez-Valiente 2019, Science](https://consensus.app/papers/details/7fc23391a08e5d6a947ae56145aff8c9/?utm_source=claude_desktop); DOI 10.1126/science.aav7958 — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30630920)
- First-year HarvardX/MITx: 43,196 certificates; 35,937 explored half or more without certifying; 469,702 viewed less than half; 292,852 never engaged; 841,687 registrations — [Ho et al. 2014](https://consensus.app/papers/details/dfa22837b0405473968e6f2c2db79a86/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Average MOOC enrols ~43,000, 6.5% complete; completion negatively correlated with course length — [Jordan 2014, IRRODL](https://consensus.app/papers/details/fa22732c141e5d8780295951c8e0816a/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Learner-centric MOOC model across 15 courses: average completion 36.35% (±25.61) and 59.38% of active learners — [Shah et al. 2022, ETR&D](https://consensus.app/papers/details/706862b632dc56fb8b48a8d19f84cd2c/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Intrinsically motivated learners outperformed extrinsic ones in a HarvardX course (10,726 students) — [Moore & Wang 2020](https://consensus.app/papers/details/201559d3a9855bfcb2c98838b682bb83/?utm_source=claude_desktop); academic motives most important for retention in a 50-study review — [Badali et al. 2022](https://consensus.app/papers/details/0e0e4c5a9694510ca1458bc2e9956dea/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Learner reviews of MOOC videos (4,534 reviews): top valued characteristics "organized", "detailed", "comprehensible", "interesting", "practical"; helpful supports included embedded questions and post-video assessments; "duration" was the most salient production feature — [Deng et al. 2023, Behav. Sci.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/b4aab835a94b594884096100b16673eb/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Video style: media-centric and balanced styles increased engagement; speaker-centric offered no advantage; effect sizes .03–.07 — [Deng 2023, Technol. Pedagog. Educ.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/e3a7e18253515109b88e543074ab1098/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Guo et al.: informal talking-head and Khan-style tablet drawing more engaging than studio lectures; videos alternating talking head and slides engaged more — [Guo et al. 2014](https://kixlab.github.io/website-files/2014/LAS2014-Engagement.pdf)

### Inferences
- A MoMA-style module (~70 min: ~7 short videos ≈ 20 min, ~6 readings ≈ 10 min each, 1 quiz) is the closest published analogue to a 60–90 min art-history unit; Crash Course shows a 50-min lecture's content can be compressed to ~10–12 min of narrated story, implying a dense unit is 5–8 "episodes" worth of narrative plus checks and tools.
- Nobody publishes completion for free image-led stories; GAC's 10–15 slide ceiling is itself a proxy for drop-off risk. For our product, instrument per-segment completion ourselves and expect MOOC-like funnel shapes unless the unit is short enough to finish in one sitting.
- Satisfaction signals that matter to learners: organisation, comprehensibility, visible duration, embedded questions (Deng 2023) — show segment durations up front.

### Gaps
- No completion/satisfaction data published for Smarthistory, Khan Academy humanities, GAC stories, Brilliant, Kurzgesagt or MasterClass; only reach metrics.
- Great Courses, Domestika, British Museum, Rijksmuseum and Tokyo National Museum e-learning structures were not retrieved with sourced specifics in this pass (searches returned only generic museum pages).

## Key Question 6 — Accessibility and inclusion requirements (WCAG 2.2, reduced motion, screen readers for maps/timelines, captions) that award-level educational products meet

### Takeaway
Award-level interactives meet WCAG 2.2 AA and voluntarily the AAA motion criterion: any scroll- or interaction-triggered animation must be disableable (SC 2.3.3, via prefers-reduced-motion), auto-moving content must be pausable (SC 2.2.2), and complex maps/timelines need a keyboard-navigable semantic alternative (tab/button roles, text summary, data table) with controls placed outside the map canvas; several open-source libraries (Olli, Data Navigator, MAIDR) now provide screen-reader navigation layers for visualizations.

### Cited Findings
- WCAG 2.2 was initiated to improve guidance for users with cognitive/learning disabilities, low vision and mobile users; SC 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions (Level AAA): "Motion animation triggered by interaction can be disabled, unless the animation is essential" — [W3C WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/)
- Understanding 2.3.3: vestibular reactions "include nausea, migraine headaches, and potentially needing bed rest"; add non-essential animation to scrolling "in a responsible way"; sufficient techniques C39 (CSS prefers-reduced-motion) and SCR40 (JS prefers-reduced-motion) — [W3C Understanding SC 2.3.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html); technique C39 — [W3C C39](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/css/C39)
- SC 2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide applies to content the page starts automatically; moving content is a barrier to slow readers, to tracking moving objects and "can also cause problems for screen readers"; content starting from an indirect interaction may also fail 2.3.3 — [W3C Understanding SC 2.2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html)
- Hong Kong Digital Policy Office sample: a banner button lets users disable all non-essential animation site-wide — [DPO handbook A.16](https://www.digitalpolicy.gov.hk/en/our_work/digital_infrastructure/digital_inclusion/accessibility/promulgating_resources/handbook/appendix_a/a16_233_animation_from_interactions.html)
- Accessible web maps for visually impaired users: place interaction tools (zoom/pan, layer selectors) outside the map component; provide discrete keyboard zoom/pan steps; provide both a brief (~125-character ALT) and a detailed description, with relationships and distances; among severely impaired respondents 89% used Braille displays/voice-over and 79% screen readers — [Cartographic Perspectives: Accessible Web Maps](https://cartographicperspectives.org/index.php/journal/article/download/cp88-pr/1574/7625)
- Timeline WAI-ARIA pattern (WCAG 2.2 AA-compliant component): horizontal timelines as role=tablist with each event a tab and its card a tabpanel; vertical timelines as a collection of focusable buttons with aria-expanded; tested with NVDA and JAWS — [Telerik Kendo Timeline ARIA](https://www.telerik.com/kendo-angular-ui/components/layout/timeline/accessibility/aria-support_timeline)
- Timeline SDK guidance: if a chart is not fully accessible, use aria-hidden plus an aria-label/visible description, and supply the information another way (data table, text summary, dataset); hiding is "only a temporary measure" — [Cambridge Intelligence blog](https://cambridge-intelligence.com/blog/build-accessible-data-visualization-apps-with-keylines/); never rely on colour alone; add markers/tooltips — [KronoGraph accessibility docs](https://developers.cambridge-intelligence.com/docs/kronograph/react/accessibility)
- Open-source accessible-navigation layers: Olli converts visualizations into keyboard-navigable tree views with multi-level descriptions — [Olli](https://umwelt-data.github.io/olli/); Data Navigator renders semantic HTML underlays over SVG/Canvas/WebGL supporting keyboard, touch, voice — [Data Navigator](https://dig.cmu.edu/data-navigator/); MAIDR adds keyboard navigation, sonification, braille and text to charts; 11 blind participants interpreted charts accurately (CHI 2024, DOI 10.1145/3613904.3642730) — [MAIDR](https://maidr.ai/); VoxLens improved screen-reader users' information-extraction accuracy by 122% and interaction time by 36% (N=21) — [VoxLens, CHI 2022](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1145/3491102.3517431)
- Captioning second-language videos was among the largest learning benefits in the multimedia meta-meta-analysis (inclusion and learning align) — [Noetel et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/1f7d04c3f664533da5621eee37530353/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- MasterClass lists subtitles in 10+ languages, adjustable speed and audio-only lessons as accessibility features — [Cursums review](https://cursums.com/masterclass-review/)
- GAC Story Editor supports audio captions on immersive slides and offers optional pan-and-zoom and depth animations that can be turned off per panel — [GAC animations help](https://support.google.com/culturalinstitute/partners/answer/15871068)

### Inferences
- Build the scroll-driven map/timeline so that with prefers-reduced-motion (or an in-page toggle) every transition becomes an instant state change with the same end state; this satisfies 2.3.3 and keeps the contiguity benefit.
- Give the sticky instrument a parallel semantic layer: a heading per segment, the current map/timeline state described in one sentence (brief) plus an expandable detailed description or table (detailed), with event markers exposed as buttons/tabs reachable by keyboard; keep zoom/pan/layer controls outside the canvas.
- One-tap checks must be real buttons with visible focus, not canvas hotspots; answers and explanations should be announced via live regions.
- Provide captions and a transcript for any narration; because captions also help L2 learners (Noetel), this is a learning feature, not only compliance.

- WCAG 1.2.x caption criteria pages were not fetched directly; the requirement is cited via the WCAG 2.2 TR and secondary sources.

## Supplementary findings — other principles asked for (coherence/seductive details, signalling, personalisation, dual coding, pre-training, cognitive load from parallel panels, map/timeline learning studies)

### Takeaway
Remove or clearly label "fun facts" (seductive details: small-to-medium retention and medium transfer cost, dose-dependent, but harmless or even helpful for voluntary learners), cue the one thing to look at (signalling d≈0.38–0.46), speak conversationally (personalisation d≈0.79), pair every claim with its picture (multimedia principle), and pre-train names/terms before dense narrative; the small school-based studies on interactive history maps/timelines show medium normalized gains (N-gain ≈0.5–0.7) but are methodologically weak.

### Cited Findings
- Seductive details: significant effect, small-to-medium on retention and medium on transfer (39 effects) — [Rey 2012, Educ. Res. Rev.](https://consensus.app/papers/details/6f37507caaf951b1977e6d40172cfd94/?utm_source=claude_desktop); meta-analysis with moderators (image type, delivery format, learner pacing, language, subject) — [Sundararajan & Adesope 2020, Educ. ### Inferences
- Because our learners are voluntary, a small number of clearly set-apart "aside" cards is defensible and may aid interest (Wesenberg 2024), but each aside should be labelled as optional and kept off the linear path; never attach valenced commentary to contested topics inside the core narrative.
- Pre-train the ~10–20 Japanese names/terms per unit in a glossary card before the dense segments (pre-training principle), and show them on the map/timeline as the cue when first mentioned (signalling).
- Treat the Indonesian/Greek school map–timeline studies as encouraging but weak evidence; the strongest justification for the map+timeline instrument remains contiguity/dual-coding (text + diagram) rather than history-specific trials.

### Gaps
- No RCT of interactive map/timeline instruments with adult learners.
- No study found on cognitive load of two simultaneous parallel panels (map and timeline) alongside text; closest evidence is split-attention/contiguity and the Greussing eye-tracking study.
