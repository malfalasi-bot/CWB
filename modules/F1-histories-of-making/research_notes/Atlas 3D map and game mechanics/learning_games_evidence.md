## 1. Gamification meta-analyses: what do points, badges, leaderboards, progress, streaks and fiction actually do?

### Takeaway
Gamification gives a small-to-moderate, fairly robust **cognitive** benefit (g ≈ .42–.50). The **motivational and behavioural** benefits are small and **not robust**: they disappear when only rigorous designs are counted, and they are inflated by quasi-experiments. Competition on its own is the weakest configuration. Competition combined with collaboration, and fiction tied to the activity, are the moderators that show up as positive. Learners who dislike gamification name two reasons: it adds nothing useful, and it causes anxiety or jealousy.

### Cited Findings
- **Sailer & Homner 2020 (Educational Psychology Review 32, 77–112):** cognitive g = .49, 95% CI [.30, .69], k = 19, N = 1,686; motivational g = .36 [.18, .54], k = 16, N = 2,246; behavioural g = .25 [.04, .46], k = 9, N = 951. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/S10648-019-09498-W)
- Sailer & Homner, high-rigour subsplit (experimental, or quasi-experimental with pretest): cognitive stays significant (g = .42, k = 9, N = 686). Motivational becomes non-significant (g = .22, p = .20, k = 7, N = 1,063), and so does behavioural (g = .27, p = .22, k = 5). The authors: "summary effects of gamification on motivational and behavioral learning outcomes are not robust." — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/S10648-019-09498-W)
- Sailer & Homner moderators:
  - **Game fiction** was significant only for behavioural outcomes, and those outcomes were "almost exclusively measured *during* interventions."
  - **Competition combined with collaboration** beat solitary or purely competitive set-ups on behavioural outcomes, and also on motivational outcomes in the high-rigour subsplit. The authors warn that "mere competition might be problematic for fostering learners' motivation and performance."
  - **Duration:** interventions longer than one month had medium motivational effects; single-day interventions had none.
  - **Randomisation:** quasi-experiments produced significantly larger motivational and behavioural effects than RCTs.
  - Points, badges and leaderboards could not be tested as separate moderators because the literature names them inconsistently.
  — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/S10648-019-09498-W)
- **Bai, Hew & Huang 2020 (Educational Research Review 30, 100322):** g = 0.504 across 30 interventions in 24 quantitative studies (3,202 participants), plus 32 qualitative studies. Learners liked gamification because it fosters enthusiasm, gives performance feedback, meets recognition needs and supports goal-setting. They disliked it for a "perceived lack of practical value or utility" and for "anxiety or jealousy." — [UZH GBL summary](https://www.gbl.uzh.ch/quartz/references/Bai-et-al.-(2020)); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1747938X19302908)
- **Huang, Hew et al. 2020 (ETR&D):** g = .464 [.244, .684], 30 studies, N = 3,083. Fourteen game elements were examined, and "each leads to different effects." — [Springer ETR&D](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11423-020-09807-z)
- **Zeng et al. 2024 (BJET):** 22 experimental studies (2008–2023), academic performance g = 0.782. This is larger than the earlier meta-analyses. Moderator details were not in the abstract. — [ERIC EJ1443146](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1443146); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/380162875_Exploring_the_impact_of_gamification_on_students'_academic_performance_A_comprehensive_meta-analysis_of_studies_from_the_year_2008_to_2023)
- **Hamari, Koivisto & Sarsa 2014 (HICSS):** review of 24 empirical studies. Gamification "does work, but some caveats exist": most quantitative studies found positive effects "only in part of the considered relationships," and effects are "greatly dependent on the context… as well as on the users." — [IEEE](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/6758978); [Tampere portal](https://researchportal.tuni.fi/en/publications/does-gamification-work-a-literature-review-of-empirical-studies-o)
- **Hanus & Fox 2015 (Computers & Education 80, 152–161):** a longitudinal classroom study. The course gamified with leaderboards, badges and competition did not improve motivation, empowerment or satisfaction, and actually harmed them. — [summary](https://educ201.wordpress.com/2014/11/24/assessing-the-effects-of-gamification-in-the-classroom-a-longitudinal-study-on-intrinsic-motivation-social-comparison-satisfaction-effort-and-academic-performance/); [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/265644737_Assessing_the_effects_of_gamification_in_the_classroom_A_longitudinal_study_on_intrinsic_motivation_social_comparison_satisfaction_effort_and_academic_performance)
- **Streaks (Silverman & Barasch, Journal of Consumer Research, "On or Off Track: How (Broken) Streaks Affect Consumer Decisions"):** after a streak breaks, people disengage. In the primary exercise-app study, about 66% repeated the exercise when the streak was intact and about 58% when it had been broken (by a staged "glitch"). The effect replicated in other domains, including language learning. Peloton's workaround is a second, weekly streak. — [Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation); [INSEAD record](https://www.insead.edu/faculty-research/publications/journal-articles/or-track-how-broken-streaks-affect-consumer)

### Inferences
- Gamification shows no reliable motivational payoff, and the cognitive payoff is about the same size as ordinary good instructional design (retrieval, feedback). That supports the existing "no confetti, no leaderboards" decision. Points, badges and leaderboards (PBL) add risk (anxiety, jealousy, "no utility") without a demonstrated benefit for adults.
- Of the gamification moderators, "game fiction" and "competition + collaboration" have the best support. For F1, fiction means the unit's own historical framing: the learner as print-shop apprentice, censor, collector or dealer. Collaboration means cohort contribution, not ranking.
- Streaks are a liability for a course that adults dip into irregularly. A broken streak reduces return behaviour, and that is exactly the outcome that matters here.
- The large Zeng 2024 g (0.78, k = 22) sits beside g ≈ .46–.50 in larger syntheses. Treat it as an upper bound, not a design target.

### Gaps
- No verified element-level effect sizes for points, badges, leaderboards or progress bars in isolation. Sailer & Homner say the literature cannot separate them. The 2025 ETR&D element-combination meta-analysis could not be read (rate-limited).
- Exact Hanus & Fox numbers (N ≈ 80, 16 weeks, lower final-exam scores in the gamified section) are recalled, not verified this session.
- Tsay, Kofinas & Luo 2018 (novelty and participation effects) was not retrieved. No meta-analytic estimate of novelty-effect decay was found. The duration moderator in Sailer & Homner points the other way (longer interventions had more motivational effect), so "novelty decay" is contested in the aggregate data.

## 2. Overjustification: do rewards undermine intrinsic motivation, and does that apply to adults?

### Takeaway
Expected, tangible rewards contingent on engaging with or completing a task reliably reduce later free-choice engagement (d ≈ −.36 to −.40). Unexpected rewards and task-noncontingent rewards do not. Informational verbal feedback **increases** intrinsic motivation, and does so most strongly for college-age learners (d = .43). Badges and points that are announced in advance and awarded for finishing a unit work like completion-contingent rewards. Informational, specific feedback after a learner's attempt works like verbal reward.

### Cited Findings
- **Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 (Psychological Bulletin 125, 627–668), 128 studies.** All rewards, free-choice behaviour: d = −0.24, k = 101. Self-reported interest: d = 0.04 (n.s.). — [PDF](https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf)
- Expected tangible rewards, free-choice: d = −0.36, k = 92. Unexpected tangible rewards: d = 0.01 (n.s.). Task-noncontingent: d = −0.08 (n.s.). — [same](https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf)
- By contingency: engagement-contingent d = −0.40, k = 27; completion-contingent d = −0.36, k = 33; performance-contingent d = −0.28, k = 25. — [same](https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf)
- Verbal rewards (positive feedback): free-choice d = +0.33, k = 21. College students d = +0.43, k = 14; children d = +0.11 (n.s.), k = 7. Self-reported interest d = +0.31. — [same](https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf)
- The findings are contested: Cameron, Eisenberger and colleagues published a critical comment ("uses and abuses of meta-analysis"). — [PubMed 10589298](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589298)
- Nicholson's "meaningful gamification" (RECIPE) builds on this. Reward-based gamification means "once you start giving someone a reward, you have to keep her in that reward loop forever." The six meaningful elements are **R**eflection, **E**xposition (narrative tied to real settings), **C**hoice (autonomy), **I**nformation (why actions matter), **P**lay ("play must be optional") and **E**ngagement (social connection and calibrated challenge). — [Nicholson preprint](https://scottnicholson.com/pubs/recipepreprint.pdf)

### Inferences
- For adult learners, the best-supported "reward" is **specific, informational feedback** delivered as part of the activity. For example: "your dating from the censor seal is right; the *kiwame* seal puts it before 1842." That is the d = +.43 college-age category.
- If any collection or recognition exists, make it **unexpected and non-contingent**: a surprise reveal, not an advertised quota. Never make it engagement-contingent, such as "visit 5 units to earn…". Unexpected rewards had d ≈ 0, so they do no harm, but they are not a lever either.
- Completion badges ("Unit 12 complete") are the completion-contingent category (d = −.36 on free choice). That is the wrong mechanism for a course whose goal is voluntary return.

### Gaps
- No adult-specific gamification meta-analysis of overjustification was found. The college-student split in Deci et al. is the closest proxy.

## 3. Game-based learning meta-analyses: Clark et al. 2016, Wouters et al. 2013, Mayer 2019

### Takeaway
Digital games beat non-game instruction modestly (g ≈ .29–.33), and only across **multiple sessions**. A single session shows no effect: g = .08 versus .44. Games are **not reliably more motivating** than conventional instruction (Wouters d = .26, n.s.). Single-player competitive designs showed no benefit (g = −.06). The features that add learning are instructional, not cosmetic: self-explanation, coaching and feedback, pre-training, spoken narration (modality) and conversational personalisation. Mayer finds games most promising for science, mathematics and second languages. History is not on that list.

### Cited Findings
- **Clark, Tanner-Smith & Killingsworth 2016 (Review of Educational Research 86(1), 79–122), 69 samples, 6,868 participants:**
  - Games vs non-game: g = 0.33 [0.19, 0.48], k = 57, 209 effect sizes. Cognitive outcomes g = 0.35; intrapersonal g = 0.35.
  - Value-added (enhanced vs standard design): g = 0.34 [0.17, 0.51], k = 20.
  - Single session g = 0.08 [−0.24, 0.39] versus multiple sessions g = 0.44 [0.29, 0.59]; b = −0.37, p = .03.
  - Average intervention: 11 sessions over 38 days, 347 minutes in total.
  - Single player with no collaboration or competition: g = 0.45. Single player competitive: g = −0.06 [−0.61, 0.48], k = 4.
  - Supplementary non-game instruction: 0.36 with, 0.32 without (n.s.).
  - Quasi-experiments gave larger effects than RCTs (marginal, p = .051). Restricting value-added studies to the most rigorous gave non-significant or small effects (g = 0.21; g = 0.11 with only 2 studies).
  — [SAGE / DOI](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654315582065)
- **Wouters, van Nimwegen, van Oostendorp & van der Spek 2013 (Journal of Educational Psychology 105(2), 249–265):** learning d = 0.29 (k = 77, N = 5,547); retention d = 0.36; motivation d = 0.26, **n.s.** (k = 31, N = 2,216). Games worked better when supplemented with other instruction, when spread over multiple sessions, and when played in groups. — [ERIC EJ1008015](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1008015); [TU/e portal](https://research.tue.nl/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-the-cognitive-and-motivational-effects-of-seri/)
- **Mayer 2019 (Annual Review of Psychology 70):** three research genres.
  - Value-added research identifies five promising features: **modality, personalization, pretraining, coaching, self-explanation**.
  - Cognitive-consequences research: first-person shooters train perceptual attention, and spatial puzzle games train 2D mental rotation.
  - Media comparisons favour games for **science, mathematics and second-language** learning.
  - Supporting citations include Adams & Clark 2014, "Integrating self-explanation functionality into a complex game environment," and Adams, Mayer et al. 2012, "Narrative games for learning: testing the discovery and narrative hypotheses."
  — [Annual Reviews abstract](https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/abs/10.1146/annurev-psych-010418-102744)

### Inferences
- "Make it a game" is not itself a learning intervention. The measurable value comes from the **added instructional features**. F1's labs are already value-added: comparing impressions, dating by seal and peeling colour layers are discovery tasks. They should be paired with a short **self-explanation prompt** ("Why does the *aratame* seal date this after 1853?") and **coaching feedback**.
- The single-session null is a strong argument for designing repeated, spaced short encounters (per-unit labs plus a recurring Atlas "case file") rather than one large set-piece game.
- History's absence from Mayer's "promising domains" and the narrative-hypothesis work (Adams et al. 2012, title only verified) both caution against heavy narrative wrapping for its own sake.

### Gaps
- Mayer's per-feature median effect sizes (recalled as roughly d = 1.4 for modality, 1.5 personalisation, 0.8 pretraining, 0.7 coaching, 0.8 self-explanation) were **not verified**. The full text was 403. Do not quote them without checking.
- The Clark et al. value-added breakdowns by feature (visual realism, narrative, scaffolding, mechanics sophistication) were not extracted. The full table was not in the excerpt.

## 4. Intrinsic integration, fantasy, SDT, flow and productive failure

### Takeaway
The cleanest design evidence is Habgood & Ainsworth's Zombie Division. When the learning content is the core mechanic, rather than a quiz bolted onto a game, children learned more and voluntarily played **about 7× longer**. Problem-solving before instruction ("productive failure") improves conceptual learning for secondary and university students, which supports the existing "instrument-native guess first, explanation second" pattern.

### Cited Findings
- **Habgood & Ainsworth 2011 (Journal of the Learning Sciences 20(2), 169–206):**
  - Study 1: 58 children aged 7–11, randomised to intrinsic (n = 20), extrinsic (n = 20) or control (n = 18), about 2.25 hours of play plus teacher reflection. Delayed test scores: intrinsic 58%, extrinsic 41%, control 38%, with a significant time × game interaction.
  - Study 2, free choice: about 75.7 minutes on the intrinsic version versus 10.3 minutes on the extrinsic one (a seven-fold difference; t = 7.38, p < .001). Children avoided the extrinsic version's quiz.
  - Intrinsic integration means learning delivered "through the parts of the game that are the most fun to play," with content "embodied within the structure of the gaming world and the player's interactions."
  — [SHU repository PDF](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/3556/1/Habgood_Ainsworth_final.pdf); [ERIC EJ922627](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ922627)
- Wouters et al. 2013 situate this in Malone's (1981) motivational triad of **challenge, curiosity and fantasy**, and in the finding that integrating content with game mechanics "was more motivating" than non-integrated versions. — [APA PsycNet](https://psycnet.apa.org/fulltext/2013-03484-001.html)
- **Productive failure (Sinha & Kapur 2021, Review of Educational Research 91(5), 761–798):** 53 studies, 166 comparisons of problem-solving-then-instruction against instruction-then-problem-solving. Benefits were stronger for secondary and university students than for primary students. In an ETH Zurich course of 650 students, productive-failure preparation was associated with a 20% improvement in pass rates. — [Phys.org summary of ETH release](https://phys.org/news/2021-09-productively-wiser.html); [RER PDF host](https://janfasen.nl/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/Sinha-and-Kapur-PS-I.pdf)
- Nicholson's RECIPE, Choice and Play elements put Self-Determination Theory's autonomy into practice ("play must be optional"). His Engagement element combines social connection with challenge calibrated to skill (flow). — [Nicholson preprint](https://scottnicholson.com/pubs/recipepreprint.pdf)

### Inferences
- The "chocolate-covered broccoli" failure is the extrinsic Zombie Division condition: a quiz gate inside a fun wrapper. The old chapter-opening multiple-choice cards were exactly that. The earlier decision to replace them with instrument-native guesses is the intrinsic-integration move.
- Most F1 labs are already intrinsically integrated. Dating by censor seals, comparing impressions and counting editions are real historian or connoisseur practices, so the mechanic is the content. This is the strongest asset in the course and should be extended, not decorated.
- Endogenous fantasy (Malone & Lepper) should be historical role and stake, such as "you are a *hanmoto* deciding a reprint run." It should not be an invented fantasy world.

### Gaps
- The Sinha & Kapur pooled effect sizes (recalled as roughly g ≈ .36 overall, rising to about .87 under high fidelity to productive-failure design) are **not verified**. Phys.org reported them only qualitatively.
- No sources retrieved this session for Ryan & Rigby's "Glued to Games"/PENS data, Deterding's critiques, or Csikszentmihalyi on flow. These are theoretical framings, so cite them from the primary books if used.

## 5. History-specific game-based learning and the ethics of playing painful histories

### Takeaway
The best-known history-game evaluations (the Assassin's Creed Discovery Tour studies) find that the game teaches facts **somewhat less well than a short teacher lecture**. Students said a teacher helped them see what mattered. History games earn their value from historiographic practice (evidence, perspective, contingency), not from immersive recreation. With painful histories, turning suffering into a mechanic ("Slave Tetris") produces public backlash and is judged educationally tone-deaf. Community co-authorship (Never Alone) and testimony-led, read-not-played structures (Attentat 1942) are the reference models.

### Cited Findings
- **Assassin's Creed Origins Discovery Tour (Université de Montréal, Éthier, with Ubisoft historian Maxime Durand):** recruited from 300 students aged 12–17 in nine schools; experimental comparison of 2 × 20 students. Knowledge improved 44% after the Discovery Tour guided mode versus 51% after a 12-minute teacher lecture. Students found it "easier to understand what parts of the information they were receiving was important when a teacher was present," and said they would not use the Discovery Tour on their own. — [Variety, 2018](https://variety.com/2018/gaming/news/assassins-creed-origins-discovery-tour-effectiveness-1202861325)
- The peer-reviewed French write-up compared 20 students on Discovery Tour with 20 at a lecture (same content, duration and age). "Students who were given a 'traditional' lecture memorized more factual knowledge than those who used the game." — [ResearchGate (Utilisation d'Assassin's Creed Origins en classe d'histoire…)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362182596_Utilisation_d'Assassin's_Creed_Origins_en_classe_d'histoire_retention_de_connaissances_declaratives_et_interet_des_eleves_pour_l'Egypte_antique)
- A follow-up chapter: Péloquin & Éthier 2024, "The Discovery Tour as a Mediated Tool for Teaching and Learning History," in Champion & Hiriart Vera (eds.), *Assassin's Creed in the Classroom* (De Gruyter), pp. 151–168. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/376233541_Peloquin_K_and_Ethier_M_2024_The_Discovery_Tour_as_a_Mediated_Tool_for_Teaching_and_Learning_History_In_E_Champion_J_Hiriart_Vera_Ed_Assassin's_Creed_in_the_Classroom_History's_Playground_or_a_Stab_in)
- The same French article cites Gilbert 2019 (Theory & Research in Social Education 47(1)) on students' historical empathy while playing Assassin's Creed, and Metzger & Paxton 2016 (TRSE 44(4)), "Gaming history: A framework for what video games teach about the past." — [ResearchGate (reference list)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362182596_Utilisation_d'Assassin's_Creed_Origins_en_classe_d'histoire_retention_de_connaissances_declaratives_et_interet_des_eleves_pour_l'Egypte_antique)
- **Chapman 2016, *Digital Games as History* (Routledge).** Five analytical categories: simulation/epistemology (a spectrum from "realist" to "conceptual" simulation; conceptual uses abstractions like maps and presents history as constructed), time, space ("narrative gardens" versus "space as canvas"), narrative, and affordances (heritage experience, reenactment, counterfactual "historying"). A reviewer notes the book lacks empirical reception or learning data. — [Thersites review](https://thersites-journal.de/index.php/thr/article/download/93/164/671); [Routledge](https://www.routledge.com/Digital-Games-as-History-How-Videogames-Represent-the-Past-and-Offer-Access-to-Historical-Practice/Chapman/p/book/9781138597822)
- **Playing History 2: Slave Trade (Serious Games Interactive, 2013).** A minigame had players stack enslaved people Tetris-style in a ship's hold. Backlash came when the game reached Steam (2015). The developer removed the level and apologised. Katherine Cross (Gamasutra) called the game a "dismal, tone-deaf failure at education." — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playing_History_2_-_Slave_Trade); [SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2015/09/03/dev-pulls-controversial-slave-tetris-minigame-from-steam-after-backlash/)
- **Never Alone (Kisima Inŋitchuŋa):** the first game made with the Cook Inlet Tribal Council. Nearly 40 Alaska Native elders, storytellers and community members contributed. Players unlock "cultural insights," short documentary clips of Iñupiaq people telling their stories. — [Games for Change](https://www.gamesforchange.org/games/never-alone-kisima-ingitchuna/). It received a Peabody Award. — [ICT News](https://ictnews.org/the-press-pool/cook-inlet-tribal-councils-never-alone-video-game-receives-prestigious-peabody-award/)
- **Attentat 1942 (Charles Games / Czech Academy of Sciences)** has a published teacher's guide and education programme. A scholarly article analyses how it adapts "contested national history for global audiences." — [Teacher's guide PDF](https://content.charlesgames.net/attentat1942/Teachers_Guide.pdf); [attentat1942.com/edu](https://attentat1942.com/edu/); [Journal article, T&F](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2040350X.2022.2071522)

### Inferences
- **Against a full 3D "game" version of F1:** the only controlled history-game evidence found shows an expensive, high-fidelity 3D recreation underperforming a 12-minute lecture on facts. Students also needed a guide to know what mattered. Clark's single-session null adds to this. 3D earns its cost only where **spatial structure is the content**: the stacked colour blocks of a woodblock print, the layout of a print-shop or publisher's district, route geometry on the Atlas globe. That is Chapman's "conceptual simulation." It does not support a walkable recreated Edo street, which is the realist "narrative garden."
- **Yoshiwara indenture unit:** keep "read, not played." Slave Tetris shows that mechanising the bodies or labour of the exploited reads as trivialisation, whatever the intent. Acceptable interactivity is historiographic only: comparing the two scholarly readings, tracing the contract's clauses to sources, perhaps marking which party benefits from each clause. No points, no timers, no "outcomes" for the women, no collection reward. The design precedent here is Attentat 1942's testimony-based structure, and this inference rests on design knowledge rather than measured evidence.
- **Collection as cultural-insight unlock:** Never Alone's model works because the unlock is documentary testimony, framed as respect rather than loot. An F1 "codex" of objects, makers and places should follow that model: entries open because you encountered them, never awarded as a score.
- Design precedents (from domain knowledge; no learning evaluations retrieved this session) show deduction or interpretation as the core mechanic. *Return of the Obra Dinn*: deduce identities from evidence, with confirmation in batches of three to stop guessing. *Heaven's Vault*: translating an unknown script, with hypotheses later confirmed or revised. *Pentiment*: interpretation with no single correct verdict. *80 Days*: route choice as a networked journey. Each maps onto an F1 lab type (seal dating, inscription reading, contested readings, Atlas routes).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed learning-outcome studies were retrieved for Attentat 1942, Through the Darkest of Times, Pentiment, Heaven's Vault, Return of the Obra Dinn or 80 Days. Their relevance is as design precedent, not evidence.
- Squire's Civilization III classroom studies (2004 dissertation and later work) were not retrieved.
- The Ubisoft and Montréal follow-ups on the Ancient Greece and Viking Age tours, and any "Poirier et al." study, were not found in this session.
- No empirical study was found on learner responses to gamified versus non-gamified treatment of sexual-labour or indenture histories specifically.

## 6. Museum and cultural-heritage gamification

### Takeaway
Heritage gamification has little published evaluation. The best-known recent example, The Met Unframed (Verizon, 2021), was criticised as "low-level edutainment" and advertising: puzzle "unlocks" gating artworks, viewed on screens too small for the art. No evaluation report with learning measures was found for Met Unframed, British Museum/Google "Museum of the World", or Google Arts & Culture games.

### Cited Findings
- **The Met Unframed** (Met + Verizon, January 2021): a virtual 3D museum with 50 artworks. Players solved a puzzle game per work to "unlock" it, then could place it at home in AR for 15 minutes. — [Met press release](https://www.metmuseum.org/press-releases/the-met-unframed-2021-news); [Artnet review](https://news.artnet.com/art-world/two-hours-inside-met-unframed-1936671)
- Artnet's Ben Davis judged the puzzles uneven, from engaging detail-hunts to poorly explained technical challenges. He said the phone format "is actively making the experience worse" for detailed paintings, called the AR placement gimmicky, and summed it up as "low-level edutainment" and Verizon advertising. — [Artnet](https://news.artnet.com/art-world/two-hours-inside-met-unframed-1936671)
- The project won an advertising and design award (ADC/One Club), which is evidence of craft recognition, not of learning. — [One Club](https://www.oneclub.org/awards/adcawards/-award/40445/the-met-unframed/)

### Inferences
- The puzzles critics liked in Met Unframed were **detail-hunts that make you look closely at the object**, which is intrinsic integration. The parts they rejected were gating and AR novelty. F1's "compare impressions" and "peel the colour layers" are detail-hunts and are on the right side of that line.
- "Award-winning" in this sector usually rewards craft and novelty, and the evidence base does not support treating that as a proxy for learning. For credible award entries (Webbys, Museums + Heritage, MuseWeb GLAMi, Games for Change), the strongest pitch is craft plus a demonstrated learning or historiographic purpose, which these references suggest juries increasingly ask for.

### Gaps
- No evaluations found for British Museum/Google "Museum of the World", Rijksmuseum Rijksstudio, the Smithsonian Learning Lab, Google Arts & Culture games (Puzzle Party, Odd One Out, Art Coloring Book, Visual Crossword), MoMA or National Gallery games. These may exist as internal or unpublished reports.

## 7. Adult learner attitudes: streaks and dark patterns, confidence ratings, collection mechanics

### Takeaway
For adults, the evidence-backed motivational levers are informational feedback, autonomy (optional play) and visible utility. Learners' main complaints about gamification are "no utility" and "anxiety/jealousy." Broken streaks reduce return behaviour. Collection is best used as Never Alone-style **unlockable testimony or context**, not as quota badges.

### Cited Findings
- Verbal or informational feedback raises intrinsic motivation for college-age learners (d = +.43, k = 14). Expected, completion-contingent rewards lower it (d = −.36). — [Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf)
- Learners dislike gamification for lack of utility and for anxiety or jealousy. — [Bai et al. 2020 summary](https://www.gbl.uzh.ch/quartz/references/Bai-et-al.-(2020))
- Broken streaks lower continuation (about 66% versus 58%), including in language-learning contexts. — [Psychology Today on Silverman & Barasch](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation)
- In Nicholson's framework, "play must be optional," and reward loops have to be maintained "forever." — [Nicholson](https://scottnicholson.com/pubs/recipepreprint.pdf)

### Inferences
- Use **confidence ratings** on the instrument-native guesses: "How sure are you of this date? Guess / fairly sure / certain." This gives calibration feedback and is a meaningful, non-competitive metric. It needs a source check (see Gaps), but it fits the "Information" and "Reflection" elements of RECIPE.
- A personal **Atlas codex** (objects, makers, places and routes the learner has encountered, plus their own annotations) is collection as knowledge-building. That is the graph itself. It is not an advertised reward, so it falls outside the overjustification categories.

### Gaps
- Not retrieved this session: confidence-based marking (Gardner-Medwin), the hypercorrection effect (Butterfield & Metcalfe 2001: high-confidence errors are corrected more readily), and metacognitive-calibration meta-analyses. They are recalled as supportive but unverified.
- No peer-reviewed study of Duolingo-specific dark patterns was found. Search results were blogs and product teardowns, deliberately excluded.

## 8. Ranked mechanics list and design rules for F1's narrative scrollytelling labs

### Takeaway
Rank mechanics by how closely they make the **historian's or maker's practice the core action**, and by whether they include **self-explanation, feedback and repetition**. Avoid anything that is advertised in advance and contingent on completion, competitive, streak-based, or that turns suffering into a mechanic.

### Cited Findings (basis for the ranking)
- Intrinsic integration: about 7× voluntary play and better delayed learning. — [Habgood & Ainsworth 2011](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/3556/1/Habgood_Ainsworth_final.pdf)
- Value-added features (self-explanation, coaching, pretraining, modality, personalisation). — [Mayer 2019](https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/abs/10.1146/annurev-psych-010418-102744); value-added g = .34. — [Clark et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654315582065)
- Multiple sessions: g = .44 versus .08 for one session. — [Clark et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654315582065)
- Solo competitive play: g = −.06. — [Clark et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654315582065). "Mere competition might be problematic"; competition combined with collaboration is better. — [Sailer & Homner 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/S10648-019-09498-W)
- Game fiction is a positive moderator (behavioural outcomes only, measured during the intervention). — [Sailer & Homner 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/S10648-019-09498-W)
- Rewards: expected and completion-contingent are negative; verbal and informational feedback are positive. — [Deci et al. 1999](https://home.ubalt.edu/tmitch/642/articles%20syllabus/Deci%20Koestner%20Ryan%20meta%20IM%20psy%20bull%2099.pdf)
- Problem-solving before instruction (productive failure). — [Sinha & Kapur 2021 via Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2021-09-productively-wiser.html)
- Broken streaks reduce engagement. — [Silverman & Barasch](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation)
- Leaderboards and badges harmed motivation in a semester-long course. — [Hanus & Fox 2015](https://educ201.wordpress.com/2014/11/24/assessing-the-effects-of-gamification-in-the-classroom-a-longitudinal-study-on-intrinsic-motivation-social-comparison-satisfaction-effort-and-academic-performance/)
- Painful-history mechanics backfire. — [Slave Tetris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playing_History_2_-_Slave_Trade)
- The high-fidelity 3D history tour underperformed a lecture. — [Variety](https://variety.com/2018/gaming/news/assassins-creed-origins-discovery-tour-effectiveness-1202861325)

### Inferences: ranked list

**A. Strong evidence. Use these.**
1. **Intrinsically integrated "connoisseur instruments"** as the core mechanic: seal dating, impression comparison, colour-layer peeling, edition counting, provenance tracing on the Atlas. Extend the pattern to every unit with a primary-source instrument. (Habgood & Ainsworth; Malone)
2. **Predict, then reveal (productive failure and prequestions).** The learner commits to an instrument-native guess *before* the explanation. Already adopted; keep it optional and specific. (Sinha & Kapur; the prior prequestion review)
3. **Self-explanation prompt after each reveal.** One line, free text or a choice of reasons: "Which clue decided it?" (Mayer value-added; Adams & Clark 2014)
4. **Coaching and informational feedback** that names the evidence: specific, non-evaluative, never "Great job! +10." (Mayer coaching; Deci verbal reward d = +.43 for college age)
5. **Spaced, repeated encounters**, such as an end-of-act "Rebuild" recall and recurring case files across units, instead of one large game. (Clark: multiple sessions g = .44 versus .08)

**B. Mixed evidence. Use only under the stated conditions.**
6. **Endogenous fiction or role** (apprentice, *hanmoto*, censor, dealer). Use only where it frames a real historical decision; never invented fantasy. (Sailer & Homner fiction moderator, behavioural only; Malone & Lepper)
7. **Collaborative cohort contribution** ("quiet density": cohort dating attempts shown as a distribution; a shared Atlas annotation layer). Never rank individuals. (Sailer & Homner competition + collaboration; Clark solo-competitive −.06)
8. **Codex or collection** of encountered objects, makers and places. Unlock by encounter, framed as testimony or context (Never Alone style), with no quotas, counts-to-goal or rarity tiers. (Deci: unexpected and non-contingent rewards ≈ 0 harm)
9. **Progress indicators**, kept navigational ("Act II of III", a map of the walk) rather than evaluative. No completion percentage tied to rewards.
10. **Confidence ratings and calibration feedback** on guesses. Plausibly strong, but unverified this session.
11. **Selective 3D**, only where spatial structure is the content: exploded woodblock stack, print-shop workflow, routes on the Atlas globe. No walkable recreations. (Discovery Tour underperformance; Chapman's conceptual versus realist simulation)
12. **Branching or deduction mini-cases** (Obra Dinn-style batch confirmation, Pentiment-style contested readings). Design precedent only, with no measured outcomes.

**C. Avoid.**
13. **Leaderboards and individual ranking.** (Hanus & Fox; Sailer & Homner; Clark −.06; Bai: anxiety and jealousy)
14. **Points, XP and completion badges announced in advance.** (Deci: completion-contingent d = −.36, engagement-contingent d = −.40)
15. **Daily streaks and loss-aversion nudges.** (Silverman & Barasch; Nicholson's "reward loop forever")
16. **Quiz gates and "chocolate-covered broccoli" quizzes** that interrupt the walk. (Habgood extrinsic condition: 10 minutes versus 76)
17. **Confetti, celebration effects and timers** in reflective or primary-source moments. These are extrinsic and add no feedback information.
18. **Any mechanic applied to exploitation, bodies or suffering** (the Yoshiwara indenture, enslaved or coerced labour). Read, not played; historiographic comparison only. (Slave Tetris)
19. **A full "animated 3D game" of F1 as the main learning vehicle.** It is expensive, history is not one of Mayer's promising domains, and it underperformed a lecture in Discovery Tour testing.

**Design rules for combining mechanics in scrollytelling:**
- R1 **Mechanic = method.** Every interaction must be something a historian, maker or connoisseur actually does with the evidence.
- R2 **Guess → reveal → explain → revisit.** Optional guess, specific feedback, a one-line self-explanation, and spaced recall at the end of the act.
- R3 **Optional by default.** Play must be optional (Nicholson), and the scroll must never be blocked by a mechanic.
- R4 **Inform, don't reward.** Feedback names the evidence. Any recognition is unexpected and non-contingent.
- R5 **Cohort as company, not competition.** Show distributions and shared annotations, never ranks.
- R6 **Fiction serves a decision.** Role-play only where the historical actor faced a real choice the learner can reason about.
- R7 **Sensitivity gate.** Units on coerced or exploited people use read-not-played, multiple scholarly readings and source tracing, with no score, outcome or collectible. Where living communities are involved, consult or co-author with them (the Never Alone model).
- R8 **3D only when space is the argument.** Use conceptual, map-like 3D for structures and routes, not realist walk-throughs.
- R9 **Measure it.** The evidence base is weak on motivation outcomes, so A/B test optional mechanics on return visits and delayed recall, not on time-on-page during the session.

### Gaps
- No direct evidence for adult, informal, self-paced design-history audiences. Nearly all studies involve K-12 or university students in formal courses.
- Element-combination effects (2025 ETR&D meta-analysis) were unread due to rate limiting.
