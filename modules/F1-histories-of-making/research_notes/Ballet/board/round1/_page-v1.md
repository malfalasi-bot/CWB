# Ballet, as a history of making · 1 of 4

## The shoe

**By the end you will be able to**
- say what a pointe shoe is made of, how long it lasts, and who makes it;
- explain why a ballet's makers are rarely named, and name three of them;
- tell a date a record supports from a figure a company gives about itself.

Ballet looks like the least material of the arts: a body, a light, almost nothing else. It runs on some of the most labour-intensive making in the theatre. The shoe is the place to start, because the body, the craft and the money meet in it.

### Paper, paste and satin

Pick up a pointe shoe and the hard toe feels like wood. There is no wood in it. The block is layers of paper and hessian, a coarse jute cloth, stuck with flour-and-water paste and covered in satin. At Freed of London, which supplies the Royal Ballet, the paste is mixed once a week. The shoe is made inside out on a wooden foot-shaped form called a last, then turned the right way round. The leather sole goes on at about four thousand pounds of pressure, and a night in a warm room dries the paste hard.

The stiffener under the arch is the shank: leather or layered card, full, three-quarter or half length, sometimes slit by the dancer to bend where she wants. She stands on the platform, the flattened front of the block. Nothing about the shoe is permanent. A pair lasts about ten to twenty hours of dancing by the usual estimate; a principal can finish a pair in one performance.

**Before you read on, choose one.** The hard toe of a pointe shoe is made from:
- wood, shaped and padded
- moulded plastic
- paper, cloth and flour paste

*If you chose paper, cloth and paste:* that is what has been in the block since, probably, the 1870s, when Italian shoemakers reinforced the toe with newspaper and pasteboard. *If you chose wood or plastic:* wood was never used, and a moulded plastic shank arrived with one maker only, probably in 1993; most companies still dance in paste.

### The mark on the sole

Turn the shoe over and there is a small symbol on the sole: a crown, a wine glass, a fish, a letter. Frederick Freed began the marks after he opened his shop in Cecil Court in 1929, so a returned shoe could be traced to the hand that made it. Dancers began to order by the mark. The maker known as Crown had made shoes for twenty-four years when a journalist visited the Hackney factory in 2013 and counted forty-one pairs a day at his bench. His name is not in the record. His mark is on thousands of feet.

That is the pattern these pages follow. The dancer is on the poster. The designer is in the programme. The maker is a symbol on a sole, and even that is unusual: most theatres name no shoemaker, cutter or dyer at all. How many makers Freed has depends on who you ask and in which year, somewhere between twenty and thirty. The company's own pages put its output anywhere from 150,000 to 330,000 pairs a year. Heritage Crafts, which keeps Britain's list of endangered crafts, calls pointe-shoe making critically endangered: two businesses with two or more makers in the whole country.

### What a company pays, by its own account

The Royal Ballet says it uses more than six thousand pairs a year at about fifty pounds a pair, a figure it publishes to raise money for them. New York City Ballet's shoe-room supervisor, Linnette Roe, put her company's shoe bill at about 780,000 dollars a year in 2018. These are the companies' own numbers, told to donors and journalists, and nobody has audited them. They are worth knowing because no other part of ballet's economy is counted in public at all.

The factory supplies the shoe and nothing else. The dancer sews her own ribbons and elastics, darns or glues the platform and breaks the pair in, on her own time. When the Paris Opéra's dancers struck in December 2023, their union said they spent about forty-two hours a month preparing for the stage against an allowance that covered six. That is a union's figure in a dispute. It is also the only count of this labour anyone has made.

### Where the shoe came from

The idea came before the technology. Dancers were rising onto the tips of their toes in the early 1820s, Amalia Brugnoli in Vienna among the earliest known, in soft slippers held by strength alone; the date is contested, and who did it earliest keeps moving. Marie Taglioni danced *La Sylphide* at the Paris Opéra on 12 March 1832 in darned slippers with a leather sole and no block, and made standing on the toe mean something: weightlessness, a creature of air. The block that made it safe came half a century later, from Italy. The firms came later, and their own dates are probably right but not yet checked at source: Capezio's shop opposite the Metropolitan Opera from 1887, Grishko in Moscow from 1988, Gaynor Minden's moulded shank in 1993. Its foam liner was patented in 1998. Every one of those is a firm. The hands are still unnamed.

### What this page could not show you

A worn pair with its mark, signed by Darcey Bussell, sits in the Royal Opera House's collection, but the house does not release its images: a link, no picture. The lithograph of Taglioni on her toes is free to use and is being fetched. The sixteen people a journalist named at the Hackney factory in 2013, from the hand laster to a finisher of forty years, appear in no company document. Nothing here comes from the workshops of Paris, Moscow or St Petersburg, which publish their work and not their names.
