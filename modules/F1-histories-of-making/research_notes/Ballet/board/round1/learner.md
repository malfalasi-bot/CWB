# Learner's read of "The shoe" (page 1 of 4)

Who I am: 24, graphic designer, never been to a ballet. Read it on my phone on the train, about twenty minutes, nobody making me finish.

## Where I stopped

I did not stop in the middle, but I did not read all of it either. I read properly down to the end of "The mark on the sole" and the money section. Then I skimmed "Where the shoe came from", because it turns into a list of company names and years with "probably" after half of them, and I could feel it was not going to tell me a story. I stopped for real at "What this page could not show you". That heading told me the section was the writer talking to whoever is checking their work, not to me, so I scrolled past it, past the sources, and read the colophon only because the small grey type looked like a secret. That was about fifteen minutes in. The last quarter of the page on my phone is links and a note about who drafted it, which is a strange way to end something.

## The main point, in my words

The thing that makes a dancer look weightless is a shoe made of paper, cloth and flour paste by people whose names nobody writes down.

## The first thing I learned, and how far down

That the hard toe is not wood. It is paper and sacking stuck with flour paste. That was the first sentence under the first proper heading, about one and a half screens down. Everything above it (the title, the three "you will be able to" bullets, the opening paragraph) was throat-clearing. I almost did not get past the bullets.

## Sentences I had to read twice

- "tell a date a record supports from a figure a company gives about itself." Read it three times. I think it means "know the difference between a real date and a company's marketing number", but I had to guess.
- "The stiffener under the arch is the shank: leather or layered card, full, three-quarter or half length, sometimes slit by the dancer to bend where she wants." Too many commas for one sentence on a phone. I got lost between "card" and "full".
- "How many makers Freed has depends on who you ask and in which year, somewhere between twenty and thirty." I understood the words but not why the writer was telling me this instead of just saying "about twenty-five".
- "Dancers were rising onto the tips of their toes in the early 1820s, Amalia Brugnoli in Vienna among the earliest known, in soft slippers held by strength alone; the date is contested, and who did it earliest keeps moving." That one I gave up on and moved to the next sentence.

## Words I did not know

- Hessian: explained, "a coarse jute cloth". Good.
- Last: explained, "a wooden foot-shaped form". Good, and I liked it.
- Shank and platform: explained, though the shank sentence is the one I got lost in.
- Principal: not explained. I guessed it means a top dancer. I think I am right.
- Darned: not explained. I know darning socks, so I assumed sewn over to make it tougher, but I was not sure that was the point.
- La Sylphide: not explained. I take it this is a ballet. Nothing told me why it matters.
- Lithograph: not explained, but I know it from work, so fine for me. My friend would not.
- Heritage Crafts: explained in the same sentence. Good.

## The question in the box

No. It asked "The hard toe of a pointe shoe is made from" about three paragraphs after the page had already said "There is no wood in it. The block is layers of paper and hessian". So it was not a question, it was a memory test on the paragraph I had just read, and it felt like being quizzed by a worksheet. On the phone the three choices are buttons and nothing under them shows what happens when you tap. If the reply is the one in the text (newspaper and pasteboard in the 1870s, one plastic maker in 1993), that is actually a nice fact and I would rather have just read it in the paragraph. If the box had come *before* the paper-and-paste paragraph, I would have got it wrong (I would have said wood) and then the page could have surprised me. That would have been worth answering.

## Did I want a picture

Yes, badly, in three places:

1. Right at "Pick up a pointe shoe and the hard toe feels like wood." I wanted a shoe cut in half so I could see the layers. The writing is doing the job a photo would do in a second.
2. At "a crown, a wine glass, a fish, a letter." I am a designer. Those marks are the single most interesting thing on the page to me and I cannot see one. Even a row of little line drawings would do.
3. The Taglioni picture it says is "free to use and is being fetched". Then fetch it. Telling me a picture exists but is not here is worse than not mentioning it.

There is not one image on the whole page. For a subject that is literally about how a thing looks and is built, that is the biggest problem.

## Sentences written for someone other than me

- The whole "By the end you will be able to" list. That is for a teacher or a syllabus. I do not want to be told what I will be able to do before I have decided to read.
- "That is the pattern these pages follow." The writer talking about their own project.
- "These are the companies' own numbers, told to donors and journalists, and nobody has audited them." Half for me, half for an examiner.
- "their own dates are probably right but not yet checked at source". That is a note to a fact-checker left in the text.
- All of "What this page could not show you". It reads like an apology to the project for missing pictures.
- The colophon: "No human has yet read this page; the register is unsigned." I honestly did not know what to do with that.

## Numbers I remember five minutes later

Forty-one pairs a day at one man's bench. Ten to twenty hours per pair, one show for a top dancer. 1929 for the shop. Fifty pounds a pair, six thousand pairs a year (I remember the Royal Ballet uses a lot and they are about fifty quid each). Forty-two hours a month against six paid. I do not remember the 780,000 dollars, I do not remember the 150,000 to 330,000, and I only half remember 1832. The numbers I kept were the ones attached to a person or a comparison. The ones I lost were the big round company figures, which is funny, because the page spent a lot of words warning me about those exact numbers.

## Did I trust it

Mostly yes, and the trust came from the little specific things: paste mixed once a week, four thousand pounds of pressure, a crown on the sole, a man counted at his bench. That is detail you do not invent. It lost a bit of trust in two directions. First, it hedges so often ("probably", "contested", "who you ask", "not yet checked") that by the history section I stopped believing it was sure of anything, even the parts it is sure of. Second, the colophon says it was drafted by an AI and no human has read it. I would rather know that than not, but reading it at the bottom, after I had already trusted it, made me go back over the numbers in my head wondering which ones were checked. If that note is going to be there, I think it belongs where I see it before I start, not as a footnote.

## Would I read page 2

Probably, yes. "The tutu, taken apart" is a good title and I would open it. Whether I would finish it depends on whether it has a picture by the second screen.

## Score

3 out of 5 as something I would send to a friend. I would send it to one specific friend who likes how things are made, with the message "skip the top, start at Paper, paste and satin". I would not post it generally. The middle of the page is a 4; the top and the bottom drag it down.

## Three things I would tell the writer to change

1. Put the question before the answer, or cut it. Right now the box tests me on the paragraph above it. Ask me what the toe is made of *before* you tell me, let me guess wood, then tell me paper. That is the whole page in one trick and you are wasting it.
2. Give me pictures: a shoe cut open at the top, the maker's marks at "The mark on the sole", and the Taglioni print you say you have. Stop telling me what you could not show and show what you can.
3. Delete everything that is addressed to the project instead of to me: the "by the end you will be able to" list, "the pattern these pages follow", the "not yet checked at source" asides, and the "could not show you" section. Say the honest caveat once, up top, in one line ("some of these company dates are unchecked"), and then just talk to me. The page is at its best when it says things like "His name is not in the record. His mark is on thousands of feet." Do more of that and less of the other.
