# Ballet, as a history of making · 1 of 4

## The shoe

**By the end you will be able to**
- say what a pointe shoe is made of, how it is built, and how long it lasts;
- name three people who make them, and say who writes their names down and who does not;
- tell a date a source supports from a figure a company gives about itself.

Ballet looks like the least material of the arts: a body, a light, almost nothing else. It runs on some of the most labour-intensive making in the theatre. The shoe is the place to start, because the body, the craft and the money meet in it.

**Before you read on, choose one.** Pick up a pointe shoe and the hard toe feels like wood. It is made from:
- wood, shaped and padded
- moulded plastic
- paper, cloth and flour paste

*If you chose paper, cloth and paste:* right, and it has been since Italian shoemakers started stiffening the toe with newspaper and pasteboard, probably from the 1870s on. *If you chose wood or plastic:* no wood appears in any maker's account; one firm has sold a moulded plastic shoe since 1993, and most companies still dance in paste.

### Paper, paste and satin

@@FIGURE@@

At Freed of London, which supplies the Royal Ballet, a pointe shoe begins as a satin upper pulled over a last, a wooden form the shape of a foot. Inside the toe the maker builds the block from layers of hessian, a coarse jute cloth, and paper, each brushed with a flour-and-water paste that the factory mixes once a week. The satin is gathered into pleats under the toe where it meets the sole. The shoe is made inside out, then turned the right way round, and the leather sole is pasted and hammered on at the bench. The shoes go into an oven overnight to dry the paste hard, at about 70 degrees by one account.

The stiffener under the arch is the shank: leather or layered card. What the dancer stands on is the platform, the flattened front of the block. Nothing about the shoe is permanent: sweat softens flour paste. A pair lasts about ten to twenty hours of dancing by the usual estimate, and a principal can finish a pair in one performance.

### The mark on the sole

Turn the shoe over and there is a small symbol stamped on the sole: a crown, an anchor, a butterfly, a letter. Each of Freed's pointe-shoe makers has one. Ray, whose mark is the crown, had made shoes in the Hackney factory for twenty-six years when a writer and a photographer visited his bench in 2013. He told them he makes forty pairs a day, and that the marks exist "so they know who to blame" if a shoe comes back. Dancers learn the marks and ask for a maker by one. Taksim's mark is an anchor; Fred's is the letter F; Daniel, four months at the bench, had the butterfly. Training takes two to three years. In the finishing rooms, where the shoes are bound and paired, the people the visit named were women: Sheila Goodman, a finisher of about forty years, and Ozel Ahmed, a supervisor.

None of those names is on the company's own pages. That is the pattern these pages follow. The dancer is on the poster. The designer is in the programme. The maker is a symbol on a sole, and you learn the name behind it from a journalist, if at all. It is not only a London habit: it was a news agency, not the firm, that named Sergei Murza at his bench at Grishko in Moscow, and Olga Monakhova, twenty-seven years in its sewing room.

How many makers Freed has depends on who you ask and in which year, somewhere between twenty and forty. Freed itself says it makes about 250,000 pairs a year; visitors have reported 150,000 in 2013 and 333,000 in 2019. Heritage Crafts, which keeps Britain's list of endangered crafts, calls pointe-shoe making critically endangered. Its reason is not a lack of demand but the supply of materials and the closing of workshops.

### What a company pays, by its own account

The Royal Ballet says it uses more than six thousand pairs a year at about fifty pounds a pair, a figure it publishes to raise money for them. New York City Ballet's shoe-room supervisor, Linnette Roe, put her company's shoe bill at about 780,000 dollars a year in 2018. These are the companies' own numbers, told to donors and journalists, and nobody has audited them. The one number missing from every account is what a maker is paid for a pair.

The factory supplies the shoe and nothing else. The dancer sews her own ribbons and elastics, darns or glues the platform and breaks the pair in, on her own time. Pointe is a women's technique, so this unpaid hour is women's work almost without exception. In December 2023 the dancers of the Paris Opéra gave notice of a strike over preparation time. Their union said they spent about forty-two hours a month preparing for the stage, make-up and dressing included, against an allowance that covered six. The notice was lifted and the Christmas performances went ahead. That is a union's figure in a dispute, and it is the nearest thing to a count of this labour that exists.

### Where the shoe came from

The idea came before the technology. Amalia Brugnoli rose onto the tips of her toes on a Vienna stage in 1823, in soft slippers held by strength alone; whether she was the earliest is contested. Marie Taglioni danced *La Sylphide* at the Paris Opéra on 12 March 1832 in slippers that, by the usual account, were darned satin with a leather sole and no block. She made standing on the toe mean something: weightlessness, a creature of air. The block that made it safer came half a century later, from Italy. Then the firms: Salvatore Capezio's shop near the Metropolitan Opera from 1887; Frederick and Dora Freed's shop in Cecil Court from 1929; Grishko in Moscow from 1988 or 1989, a contested year. Gaynor Minden sold a moulded shank in 1993 and had its foam liner patented in 1998. Every one of those is a firm with a founder's name. The makers' names are the ones you have to go looking for.

### What is not here

The pair of shoes Darcey Bussell wore and signed sits in the Royal Opera House's collection, but the house does not release its images: a link, no picture. The makers at Freed's factory are known from one visit by one writer and one photographer in 2013; the company has not published their names, so this page carries them as a journalist's record. Nothing here comes from the workshops of the Paris Opéra or the Mariinsky. And no source read for this page gives the figure that would complete it: the price of a maker's hour.
