# Ballet, as a history of making · 1 of 4

## The shoe

**By the end you will be able to**
- say what a pointe shoe is made of, how it is built, and how long it lasts;
- name three people who make them, and say who wrote their names down;
- spot which numbers on this page are a company's own.

Ballet looks like the least material of the arts: a body, a light, almost nothing else. It runs on some of the most labour-intensive making in the theatre. The shoe is the place to start, because the body, the craft and the money meet in it.

**Before you read on, choose one.** Pick up a pointe shoe, the shoe for dancing on the tips of the toes, and the hard toe feels like wood. It is made from:
- wood, shaped and padded
- moulded plastic
- paper, cloth and flour paste

*If you chose paper, cloth and paste:* right, and it has been since Italian shoemakers started stiffening the toe with newspaper and pasteboard, probably from the 1870s on. *If you chose wood or plastic:* no wood appears in any maker's account; one firm has sold a moulded plastic shoe since 1993, and Freed, which supplies the Royal Ballet, builds in paste.

### How it is built

At Freed of London a pointe shoe is made inside out on a last, a form the shape of a foot. Inside the toe of the satin upper the maker builds the block from layers of hessian, a coarse jute cloth, and paper, each brushed with a flour-and-water paste that the factory mixes once a week. The satin is gathered into pleats under the toe where it meets the sole, the leather sole is pasted and hammered on at the bench, and the finished shoe is turned the right way round. The shoes go into an oven overnight to dry the paste hard; one account gives about 70 degrees.

The stiffener under the arch is the shank: leather or layered card. What the dancer stands on is the platform, the flat front of the block. Nothing about the shoe is permanent: sweat softens flour paste. A pair lasts probably ten to twenty hours of dancing, and a principal can finish a pair in one performance.

### The mark on the sole

Turn the shoe over and there is a small symbol stamped on the sole: a crown, an anchor, a butterfly, a letter. Each of Freed's pointe-shoe makers has one. Ray, whose mark is the crown, told a writer who visited his bench in 2013 that he had been there twenty-six years and makes forty pairs a day. He is paid by the pair. The marks exist, as Ray put it, "so they know who to blame" if a shoe comes back. A dancer chooses a maker and then, the same visit was told, "will wear no other".

Taksim's mark is an anchor; Daniel, four months at the bench, has the butterfly, and training takes two to three years, the series says. In the binding and finishing rooms the visit named Sheila, a finisher of forty years, Varsha Bahen and Rashimi Patel. At Freed, "maker" means the bench; the benches in 2013 were men's, and nobody in binding or finishing has a mark.

None of those names is on the company's own pages. That is the pattern these pages follow. The dancer is on the poster. The designer is in the programme. The maker is a symbol on a sole, and you learn the name behind it from a journalist, if at all. It was a news agency, not the firm, that named Sergei Murza at his bench at Grishko in Moscow.

### What a company says about itself

How many makers Freed has is contested, somewhere between twenty and forty depending on who counted and when. Freed's own figures for pairs a year run from about 160,000 in a film it made in 2015 to "nearly 250,000" on its website, so even the company's count is contested; visitors reported 150,000 in 2013 and 333,000 in 2019. Heritage Crafts lists pointe-shoe making as critically endangered, two businesses in the country, yet says trade is buoyant; its worry is dearer materials and fewer dance shops.

The Royal Ballet says it uses more than six thousand pairs a year at about fifty pounds a pair, a figure it publishes to raise money for them. New York City Ballet's shoe-room supervisor, Linnette Roe, put her company's shoe bill at about 780,000 dollars a year in 2018. These are the companies' own numbers, told to donors and journalists; no one outside the companies has checked them. The one number missing from every account is what a maker is paid for a pair.

The factory supplies the shoe and nothing else. The dancer sews her own ribbons and elastics, darns the platform and breaks the pair in, on her own time; pointe is danced almost entirely by women, so this unpaid work is women's work. In December 2023 the dancers of the Paris Opéra gave notice of a strike over preparation time, which their union put at about forty-two hours a month, make-up and dressing included, against an allowance of six. The notice was lifted. The figure is a union's in a dispute, and the nearest count of this labour that exists.

### Where the shoe came from

The idea came before the technology. Amalia Brugnoli rose onto the tips of her toes on a Vienna stage in 1823, in soft slippers held by strength alone; whether she was the earliest is contested. Marie Taglioni danced *La Sylphide* at the Paris Opéra on 12 March 1832 in slippers that were probably darned satin with a leather sole and no block. She made standing on the toe mean something: weightlessness, a creature of air. The block that made it safer probably came half a century later, from Italy. Then the firms, each by its own dating: Capezio in New York from 1887, Frederick and Dora Freed in Cecil Court from 1929, Grishko in Moscow from 1988 or 1989, a contested year, Gaynor Minden's plastic shank in 1993. Every one of those is a firm with a founder's name. The makers' names are the ones you have to go looking for.

The people at Freed's factory are named here from two visits in 2013 by a local writer and a photographer. They spoke on the record for a portrait series, not for this course, and the makers are given by first name because that is how the series recorded them.
