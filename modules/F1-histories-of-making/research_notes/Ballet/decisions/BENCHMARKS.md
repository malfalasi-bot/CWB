## Decision 1. Where should a large case live?

**The fact that frames the decision [documented in repository].** F1 as a whole is 34 learner hours (4 / 14 / 16) in 35 units, and its threshold concept is "a web with obscured strands, not a line". The ballet plan is 44 hours: **larger than all of F1**. And about half of what the plan asks learners to make belongs to other modules: F1's Concept DNA role is SRC (sources), while the acts' products (a premise, a livret's rules and refusals, a composer's plan as obligations, a remake proposal, a credit chain) fill PRM (M1), RUL, ANT, OBL and GRM (M2), ADJ (M4), RGT (R2) and LCY (R4) (`schema/concept-dna.schema.json`, as read in `S_structure_study.md` §4).

**Options.**

| Option | What it is | For | Against |
|---|---|---|---|
| (a) Inside F1 only | The 44 hours as F1's Practice and Mastery extension | Simplest; the history stays whole; one owner | One case would outweigh F1's global web, the very "line" F1 argues against; its products would be made in a module that does not own those fields; Method modules would rebuild their own examples |
| (b) Standalone deep-case module | An elective of 44 hours | Room for depth; enthusiasts get the whole story | 44 hours is a long course by every benchmark (L2); most learners would never meet ballet at all; nothing feeds the Method modules |
| (c) Program-wide case world | Cards (works, systems, objects, people) in the world set, tagged by unit and Concept DNA field; each module draws one to three sittings for its own purpose | Matches the program's planned world set of about 1,200 cases tagged by unit; meets ballet where each discipline needs it (F3 body, F4 material, S1 stage, S2 the shoe as product, S3 the tutu, S5 the credit line and poster, R2 rights, R4 the Paris Opéra report) | Needs a keeper, a record of which module used which card, and a comparison rule; risks shallow, scattered meetings |
| (d) Historical exemplar world | *Giselle*'s concept, reconstructed field by field as a Concept DNA, beside the planned contemporary exemplar DNA and the three seed worlds | The program already plans "the exemplar Concept DNA … now, before M2" and "cognitive apprenticeship (worked exemplar, then the learner's own)"; a historical exemplar paired with a contemporary one is exactly a two-case comparison | Ballet's concept does not fit every field; the reconstruction must be marked as a reading, not a record |
| (e) Combinations | — | — | — |

**Evidence.**
- *Comparison beats repetition.* Comparing two cases doubled transfer over studying the same two cases separately, and separate study was "no better than no training at all" for teams (Gentner, Loewenstein and Thompson 2003; Loewenstein et al. 2003) [documented]. Across 57 experiments, case comparison beat single, sequential and non-analogous case study, d = 0.50, with larger effects when learners look for similarities and the principle is given after (Alfieri et al. 2013) [documented]. Analysing contrasting cases twice without new instruction was the weak condition (Schwartz and Bransford 1998) [documented]. **So the question is not "one rich case in many modules or once in depth" but "is each meeting a comparison?"** A rich case met five times alone is the separate-cases condition five times [argued].
- *Recurring cases across a curriculum are established practice with weak outcome evidence.* Virtual families in nursing and medicine (Sharp and Primrose 2003; Giddens's *Neighborhood*; EVMS CareForward, 40+ characters drawn on by every organ-system module; ECU, 82% said the shared family helped them link units) report satisfaction and perceived integration, not learning outcomes [documented, Kirkpatrick levels 1–2]. Strathclyde's Ardcalloch hosted six modules' transactions in one fictional town and reported better coursework and examination performance alongside a "high work rate for staff" [documented as the project's report]. Business schools' comprehensive cases cap each course at two or three classes so students are not "bored or annoyed", and appoint a case champion (Markulis et al. 2004) [documented]; a five-discipline shared case broadened students' judgements but its gains "may not accrue equally" (Cruz et al. 2022) [documented]. The OU built one common study pack for three modules because a core idea "was being taught differently across the various modules"; it reached 7,000 students and then the public [documented].
- *Integration level can vary by module.* Harden's ladder places (c) at "nesting" and "correlation" and (d) close to Dundee's top-step pattern of one task met from each specialty's angle with a study guide; a later *Medical Teacher* guide adds that full integration may not be "possible or even advisable" everywhere [documented].
- *Worked examples first.* Novices learning designers' styles learned more from worked examples than from problems (Rourke and Sweller 2009, in the structure study) [documented]. A filled *Giselle* DNA is a worked example of the learner's own artifact [argued].

**Benchmarks.** Ardcalloch: one world, six modules, 272 students in 2007–08. CareForward: one cast, every organ-system module and clerkship. OU T551: one pack, three modules. Markulis: one case, about ten courses, two or three classes each. Cooper Union: one problem, two studio years. None of these precedents put a 44-hour case inside one module.

**Recommendation [argued].** Combine (c) and (d), with a small (a), and hold (b) in reserve:
1. **The case world (c).** Publish the research as a "Ballet" collection in the world set: about 20 work cards on the case card already designed (MODULE_PLAN §5), plus system, object and person cards, each tagged with the units and Concept DNA fields it serves. This is where the 119,000 words go, and nothing is lost.
2. **The historical exemplar (d).** Write *Giselle* (1841) as a full Concept DNA, the program's historical exemplar, and pair it on purpose with the contemporary exemplar DNA when M2 is built. Every Method unit that uses it asks the learner to compare the two exemplars field by field (the analogical-encoding condition).
3. **F1's share (a).** F1 keeps a ballet history core of about 6 hours (the Prologue and the *Giselle* 1841 case, plus one non-European case such as *El-Leila El-Kebira* or *The White-Haired Girl*), filed as SRC precedents. This keeps ballet at the scale of one F1 strand among many, consistent with the module's threshold concept.
4. **Rules for every borrowing module.** One to three sittings per module; each ballet card set beside a non-ballet case with a "what do these share?" prompt and the principle given after; a keeper (the Historian voice's knowledge pack can hold the record of which card each module used, the Dundee study-guide role); no module re-tells the history, it links to the card.
5. **The deep case (b), later.** Assemble the six acts as an optional Mastery path from the same cards only after the slice (Decision 4) has been tested, so the extra cost is the act products and the joins, not new research.

**Risks.** Scattered meetings without comparison would give fragments; mitigated by the comparison rule. Coordination cost (Ardcalloch's "high work rate for staff"); mitigated by building cards once. The exemplar may be read as a model to copy; mitigated by always showing it beside a second, different exemplar. The deep-case path may never be built; that is acceptable, because the case world already carries the research.

## Decision 2. How long, and how many parts?

**Options.** Four acts of about 10 hours (merging II with III and V with VI); six acts of 5–8 hours (the plan); more, shorter parts (e.g. twelve of about 3 hours).

**Evidence.**
- *Length costs starts more than finishes.* Each added week multiplied MOOC completion by about 0.90, but among *active* learners completion was unrelated to length: longer courses "put off starting" (Jordan 2014) [documented]. Participation falls steeply in week one and then flattens (Evans, Baker and Dee 2016) [documented].
- *Smaller modules pass more often; breaks help.* At the Open University, 30-credit modules had 42% higher odds of passing than 60-credit ones, and each added break week raised the odds by 28% (Nguyen, Thorne and Rienties 2018; observational) [documented].
- *Boundaries are exits.* In the MITx Supply Chain MicroMasters, "most attrition occurs between, not within, courses"; dropout per course fell from 48% in the first to 16% in the last; putting the most demanding course first drove learners away (programme team; Borrella et al. 2019) [documented]. Coursera Specialisation completion is reported at 15% or less, mostly 5% or less (EDUCAUSE 2019, administrators' report) [documented].
- *Serial media need two to four episodes.* No Netflix first season hooked viewers with the pilot; the hooked episode fell at 2 to 4 in most series (Netflix 2015, company data) [documented].
- *Goal gradient and its limits.* Effort accelerates near a finish line, and slackens after a reward before re-accelerating (Kivetz et al. 2006); motivation can sag mid-way (Bonezzi et al. 2011); progress markers help when distance is uncertain and can breed complacency when it is not (Amir and Ariely 2008) [documented].
- *Uneven parts cost.* FutureLearn analytics found real weekly durations varying up to tenfold within one course, which "may discourage" engagement (Ballesteros Mesa and Wilde 2016) [documented].

**Benchmarks.** FutureLearn course: 2–6 weeks at 2–6 hours a week. Coursera course: 4–12 weeks at 1–2 hours of video a week. Microcredential (CMF): 100–150 hours. OU: 30 credits about 300 hours. F1: 34 hours. Proposed ballet module: 44 hours, in parts of 2, 5, 6, 6, 8, 6, 8 and 3 hours.

**Recommendation [argued].**
- **Six acts, not four.** Four 10-hour acts would each be longer than a typical FutureLearn course, with a deeper "middle"; the history's own turning points (1661, 1832, 1890, 1909, 1945, 1980) argue for six; and the MITx data show that later boundaries cost little once learners are through the first ones.
- **Even them out: 5–6 hours each** (15–18 sittings, three to four weeks at five sittings a week), so no act is twice another.
- **Cut the whole to about 32–36 hours** by moving the epilogue's premise work to M1 (where PRM lives) and turning the "systems" lists into library cards reached on demand rather than required sittings.
- **Protect the first two weeks.** At five sittings a week, the Prologue plus the first two-thirds of Act I would fill them, and the court is the least concrete material in the module. Either run the default route Prologue → Act II → Act I (the court as "what Paris inherited", motivated by the white act) or cut Act I to about 3 hours and open it with a making task (the 16-count notation). A task in the first sittings matters: week-one learners who only watched dropped out three times as often as those who did the quiz (Ye and Biswas 2014) [documented].
- **Make every act end open the next.** Show the next act's first case on the last screen of each act and let the learner continue without re-enrolling (the MITx lesson); plan a deliberate break week between acts rather than an accidental one (the OU finding).

**Risks.** More acts mean more product briefs to write and test. The "start at Act II" route breaks chronology; the anchor-date map in the Prologue has to carry it. All the completion evidence is from open courses and a distance university, not from a self-paced program whose learners already chose the program; it predicts direction, not size.

## Decision 3. One returning work, or many?

**Options.** *Giselle* as the braid (the plan); *Swan Lake* as the braid; a rotating braid (a different work per act); no braid (many works).

**Evidence.**
- *Variation needs an invariant.* "To discern an aspect, the learner must experience … variation in a dimension corresponding to that aspect, against the background of invariance in other aspects" (Marton and Pang 2006); learning studies built on this report large gains (70% against 30%; 85% against 25%; 74% against 40% in self-study materials) [documented; mostly small quasi-experiments]. A braid holds the story and score constant while maker, city, body and politics vary: a textbook variation design [argued]. A rotating braid removes the invariant and with it the reason for having a braid [argued].
- *Generalisation needs more than one instance.* Variation theory's second step is generalisation: the same aspect seen in different instances (Kullberg, Runesson Kempe and Marton 2017) [documented]. Low-variability input is learned fast and generalises poorly (Raviv, Lupyan and Green 2022) [documented]. Comparison of two cases beats either alone (Gentner et al. 2003; Alfieri et al. 2013) [documented]. This is the evidence for the narrowing risk: if the five modes of remaking are seen only in *Giselle*, learners learn *Giselle*'s history, not remaking [argued].
- *Seeing versions side by side.* Interleaving helps tell visual styles apart (Brunmair and Richter 2019, in the structure study) [documented].

**Data.**
- Bachtrack listings: *Swan Lake* second most performed ballet in every year seen (2015, 2016, 2023, 2024, 2025; third in 2017); *Giselle* fourth to seventh (sixth in 2023 and 2025) [documented for 2023 and 2025; probable for 2015–17].
- Remake range: both works have remakes in all five modes. *Giselle*'s ladder is unusually global and documented by its makers (Gautier's own account; Havana, Louisiana, Stockholm, London, Johannesburg), and three versions toured the UK at once in 2019. *Swan Lake*'s is wider across media and holds the design-led remake design learners know (Bourne 1995) and a film (*Black Swan*). Ek and Masilo each remade **both** works [documented in W3 and press; *Swan Lake* dates probable].
- Neither work reaches the court period; Act I has no braid either way.

**Recommendation [argued].** **Keep *Giselle* as the braid**, because its origin is told by its originator (the premise, the rejected first draft and the team are on record), it was lost in Paris and returned from St Petersburg (the record decides what the work is), and it already has the program's only filled case card. **Add *Swan Lake* as a counter-braid at three fixed points:** (1) Act III, *Swan Lake*'s Moscow failure beside *Giselle*'s Petipa revision (two kinds of afterlife); (2) Act VI, Ek's and Masilo's *Giselle* beside their own *Swan Lake* (the maker held constant, the work varied: a clean contrast); (3) one comparison wall of both works' versions as images. Teach each of the five modes of remaking in at least two works. Add one sitting that says plainly what *Giselle* is *not* typical of (most ballets are not Romantic, not French-born, not lost and recovered).

**Risks.** *Swan Lake* is the work learners are likeliest to know; a learner who meets only *Giselle* may feel the module ignored the obvious one; the counter-braid answers this. The braid can tire; it appears once per act and never as a full retelling. Canon capture remains; the non-braid cases from Beijing, Tokyo, Havana and Cairo carry the counterweight.

## Decision 4. Which part to build first?

**Options.** One stand-alone unit (as "The shoe" already is); the opening (Prologue + Act II, the plan's proposal); a middle act; the library cards first.

**Evidence.**
- The first week or two decide engagement in open courses (Jordan 2015; Evans et al. 2016) [documented]; one week of behaviour predicts persistence (AUC about 0.7 in MITx 6.002x) [documented]; doing a task in week one separates stayers from leavers (Ye and Biswas 2014) [documented]; even well-built serials need two to four episodes to hook (Netflix 2015) [documented].
- MITx's first course in sequence had the highest attrition, and the team regretted placing the hardest course first [documented].
- Small iterative tests: about 31% of usability problems per tester, five testers finding most of them, three rounds of five better than one of fifteen (Nielsen and Landauer 1993; Nielsen 2000) [documented]; contested as optimistic for heterogeneous users (Schmettow 2012) [documented].
- In the repository, one ballet page ("The shoe") has been through a six-persona reader board in three rounds; every fault was found by a reader, not a lint (`../board/SUMMARY.md`) [documented]. That proves the page method; it does not test an opening, a case card or a braid.

**Recommendation [argued].** Build **a vertical slice of about 4–5 hours (12–14 sittings): the Prologue and the complete *Giselle* 1841 case**, with its first task in sitting two or three and its Maker and Briefer products. Reasons: it is the opening under every placement option in Decision 1; it produces the historical exemplar DNA the program needs before M2; it tests the case card, the first appearance of the braid and the hook in one build. Test with five learners per lane, in two rounds. Measure: minutes per sitting against the twenty-minute budget; whether the first task is done; whether learners choose to start sitting seven unprompted (the hook); and a transfer task (fill a case card for an unseen work, such as *La Sylphide*, from supplied sources). Build nothing else at scale until round two is clean.

**Risks.** A slice cannot show completion; that needs a whole act and a cohort of dozens. Testers who volunteered are more motivated than real learners. The slice is the plan's most canonical material; one non-European card (Cairo or Tokyo) should be built next, so the second test is not also Paris.

## Decision 5. The Gulf strand

**The rule [documented in repository].** Principle 11: "Every module's last unit is a regional slot; … the Gulf, MENA and SWANA vertical fills the slots when it is built." Regional readers ("history, materials, colour and light, dress") are planned "later, with the regional vertical".

**What exists (details and sources in L5).**
- *Gulf ballet is routes and institutions, not works.* No resident professional ballet company and no Gulf-made ballet with a documented concept, team and afterlife was found in six states. What exists: émigré-founded private schools (Fantasia Ballet, Abu Dhabi, 1998, from a 1997 Cultural Foundation course; Kuwait School of Dance, 1974; Bahrain Ballet Centre, 1982); examined syllabi (RAD in Muscat, Doha, Kuwait); presenting houses (Doha 2010, Muscat 2011, Manama 2012, Dubai and Kuwait 2016) with touring seasons; and since 2020 a Saudi state commission that licenses ballet, targets 4,500 graduate performers by 2030, and in 2024 ran a 38-day theatrical-dance training programme in Jeddah "in partnership with Caracalla Dance School" (contractor's project page; corrected 25 Oct 2026, "bought" overstated). Individual dancers are documented in the press (Alia Al Neyadi; Samira AlKhamis; Halaa Nagadi).
- *Arab ballet elsewhere is institutions plus a few works.* Cairo (Soviet-built school 1958, company 1966, *El-Leila El-Kebira*, already in W3); Baghdad (School of Music and Ballet, 1968/69, Russian teachers until about 1990); Damascus (courses 1988, school 1992, Russian experts); Beirut (Gebara, 1964); Tunis (National Ballet 1992–96, Opera ballet 2018); Amman (MISK, 1996); Algiers (Ballet National, by decree 1992).
- *Scholarship.* On Gulf ballet: none found beyond opera-house studies (Petrocelli; Hennessey). On Arab ballet: Barsova (2023, Russian), Dawisha (1975), Aka (1987), Moine (2023, Algiers), Shay (2006, male dancers); nothing on the Baghdad or Damascus schools.
- *Arabic sources add.* Arabic press supplied facts absent in English (the 1998 French ballet billed as a Middle East first; the Damascus dates) and a contradiction (Svetlana Al Neyadi "Russian" in Arabic, "Ukrainian" in English).

**Options.** (i) A Gulf *work* case now; (ii) a Gulf *route* case now, with a MENA work case; (iii) a commissioning brief in place of the missing work; (iv) wait for the regional vertical.

**Recommendation [argued].** (ii) + (iii) now; (iv) for depth.
- **The regional slot for ballet, now:** "How ballet reached the Gulf", built on the module's own five export models, each with one Gulf or Arab instance: Soviet state teachers (Baghdad, Damascus, beside Cairo); the émigré teacher (Abu Dhabi 1997–98, beside Kamakura and Tunis); the examined syllabus (RAD in Muscat and Doha); the presenting house (Muscat 2011; Dubai's Bolshoi *Nutcracker*); the state commission (TPAC, and its 2024 training programme run in partnership with a Beirut school). The Arab *made work* is *El-Leila El-Kebira* (Cairo), already researched. Every Gulf fact carries its source type ("as the school describes itself", "as reported by"), and "firsts" become "earliest known" or "described as the first".
- **The missing work as a brief:** a Maker and Briefer task to write the commission a Gulf house *could* issue (premise, roots, which of the five modes, what must not change), using the act products. It is labelled as a design exercise, never as history.
- **Before the regional vertical:** an Arabic reader for Gulf and Levant press and ministry sources; interviews (a Fantasia Ballet founder, a ROHM or Dubai Opera programmer, a TPAC officer, a Baghdad or Damascus teacher), with consent recorded; a Russian reader for the Soviet side (Baghdad, Damascus, Cairo archives).

**Risks.** Press-only sourcing and self-described "firsts" (scope every claim). Living, named women in conservative settings, some of whom have chosen anonymity (the Eastern Province school owner); the protocol for living people applies, and the unit should name only those who are publicly on record. Presenting houses can read as "importation and consumption" only (Hennessey's argument); the émigré-teacher and state-commission instances show local agency and should be given equal weight.

## Research log (raw, appended as found; the decisions above it are written from this)

### L1. Decision 1: one rich case in several modules, or once in depth

- **Comparison of cases beats separate study of the same cases.** Gentner, Loewenstein and Thompson (2003, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 95(2): 393–408; DOI 10.1037/0022-0663.95.2.393), three experiments with novices learning negotiation: comparing two cases gave "a marked advantage … over studying the 2 cases separately"; in Experiment 2 the comparison group showed "a two-fold transfer advantage over the separate-cases group"; with a simple instruction to compare, 70% transferred the principle, against 55% for separate study and 37% with no cases [documented: APA abstract https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2003-00780-016 and author PDF https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/gentner/papers/GentnerLoewensteinThompson03.pdf]. Loewenstein, Thompson and Gentner (2003, *Academy of Management Learning and Education*): teams that analysed the same two cases separately did "no better than no training at all"; separate study left "inert knowledge", encoded "in a contextually specific manner" [documented: https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/gentner/papers/LoewensteinThompsonGentner03.pdf]. Gentner et al. (2004, CogSci): "fewer than one in five Separate cases participants linked the two cases in any way, despite the fact that they occurred contiguously and were analogous"; comparison also improved *retrieval* of earlier analogous cases [documented: https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/gentner/papers/GentnerLoewensteinThompson04.pdf].
- **Meta-analysis of case comparison.** Alfieri, Nokes-Malach and Schunn (2013, *Educational Psychologist* 48(2): 87–113), 57 experiments, 336 tests: case comparison beat sequential, single-case and non-analogous case study and traditional instruction, d = 0.50 [0.44, 0.56]; moderators: asking learners to find *similarities*, giving the principle *after* the comparison, perceptual content; with principle given after, far transfer d = 0.98 [documented: T&F abstract https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00461520.2013.775712; author PDF https://lrdc.pitt.edu/nokes/documents/alfieri_et_al.,_2013.pdf].
- **Bearing [argued].** Meeting one rich case in several modules helps only if each meeting *sets it beside another case* and asks what they share. A case met five times, each time alone, is the "separate cases" condition five times over: it produces vivid, context-bound memory of ballet, not transferable principles. This is the strongest single finding for the placement decision.
- **Virtual families and longitudinal cases (medicine and nursing).** Sharp and Primrose (2003, *Nurse Education Today*): a BSc nursing programme used fictional "virtual families" across PBL scenarios; students valued "the longitudinal aspects" and the "integration of course work elements" [documented: PubMed 12672390]. Giddens's *Neighborhood* virtual community (five baccalaureate programmes; Carlson-Sabelli et al. 2011, *IJNES*, DOI 10.2202/1548-923x.2136): benefits and challenges "grow together", net benefit rises with faculty use; the main challenge was "busy work … unrelated to enhancing conceptual clarity" [documented: abstract]. Eastern Virginia Medical School's CareForward curriculum: 40+ virtual-family characters drawn on by every organ-system module and clerkship; student agreement that the cases were "worthwhile" rose from about 47% to 87% between the first and second 18 weeks after revisions [documented: AMA consortium poster; AMA news 27 Apr 2017]; 146/150 first-year respondents, over 80% agreed they introduced social determinants (FASEB abstract 2018) [documented]. Edith Cowan University nursing "Virtual World": 82% agreed that cases all drawn from the virtual family "helped me to link the content of different units and remember things more easily" (n = 74, 22% response) [documented: ECU paper https://ro.ecu.edu.au/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=3512&context=ecuworkspost2013]. George et al. (2013, *Teaching and Learning in Medicine*): a 6-case, 24-hour, three-generation virtual-family course; rating 5.17/6; all passed the OSCE [documented: abstract].
- **Bearing [argued].** Every evaluation found is at the level of satisfaction and self-reported linking (Kirkpatrick level 1–2); none compares a recurring-case curriculum with unlinked cases on a learning outcome. The recurring-case pattern is **established practice with weak outcome evidence**: it reliably improves perceived coherence, and its main failure mode is busy work when faculty attach tasks the case does not need.

- **Contrasting cases prepare learners to be told.** Schwartz and Bransford (1998, *Cognition and Instruction* 16(4): 475–522), three classroom studies: analysing contrasting cases *then* receiving a lecture or text beat reading about the distinctions plus lecture, summarising plus lecture, and **analysing the cases twice without a lecture** [documented: abstract, https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1604_4; author PDF https://aaalab.stanford.edu/papers/time_for_telling.pdf]. **Bearing [argued]:** a second meeting with the same material adds little unless something new is told or a new contrast is set; repetition alone is the weak condition.
- **Harden's integration ladder** (2000, *Medical Education* 34: 551–557): eleven steps, isolation, awareness, harmonisation, nesting, temporal co-ordination, sharing, correlation, complementary, multidisciplinary, interdisciplinary, transdisciplinary; higher steps need "a central curriculum, organizational structure and … greater participation by staff in curriculum discussions" [documented: abstract, https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2923.2000.00697.x; full text via https://www.susme.org/conferenceAP3/materials/ladder.pdf]. His example of the top step: Dundee's final years, where 113 clinical tasks are each met "from the perspective of the different attachments" (abdominal pain in surgery, medicine, gynaecology and general practice), held together by a study guide [documented: same full text]. A *Medical Teacher* twelve-tips article on integration (2010; authors not captured in this pass) adds that "it may not be possible or even advisable to have a completely integrated curriculum" and that the level "may vary from module to module" [documented: https://www.med.unc.edu/aoe/wp-content/uploads/sites/519/2017/11/integrating-your-curriculum.pdf]. **Bearing [argued]:** option (c) is "nesting" (step 4: a module borrows content from elsewhere to serve its own aim) plus "correlation" (step 7); the Dundee pattern, one problem met from each specialty's angle with a study guide, is the closest medical model of a ballet case met in F3, F4, S3 and R2. The ladder is a planning taxonomy, not evidence that higher steps teach better.
- **Law: one virtual town across a whole programme.** Glasgow Graduate School of Law (Strathclyde), from 2000: the fictional town of Ardcalloch, with a civic history, a map and a directory of several hundred institutions, businesses and people, hosted the simulated transactions of six modules of the Diploma in Legal Practice (conveyancing sale and purchase, private client, personal injury, civil court action, practice management), 272 students in 68 "firms" in 2007–08 [documented: UKCLE case page https://resources.ials.sas.ac.uk/ukcle/78.158.56.101/archive/law/resources/enhancing-learning-through-technology/simple2/index.html; Maharg 2007 http://hdl.handle.net/1885/14274]. The SIMPLE final report: "an identical platform to run the simulations across the curriculum, so that what had hitherto been a series of subject 'silos' now had a common core"; outcomes reported as "high work rate for staff, improved student performance in coursework and also in examinations" (citing Barton and Maharg 2006) [documented: Hughes et al. 2008, Strathprints]. **Bearing [argued]:** the closest precedent for option (c): a *world* shared by modules, each module running its own task inside it. Its stated costs were staff workload and software, not learner boredom.
- **Business schools: the comprehensive case.** Markulis, Strang and Howe (2004, ABSEL 31): one composite insurance-company case introduced in the first year and "used in all subsequent primary business courses" (accounting, statistics, law, OB, finance, POM, MIS, marketing, strategy); rules: no course spends "more than two or three classes (at most)" on it, so students are not "bored or annoyed"; a case "champion"; a handout of what earlier courses covered [documented: https://absel-ojs-ttu.tdl.org/absel/article/download/646/615]. No outcome data (a prototype). Cruz et al. (2022, *Innovative Higher Education* 48: 415–432), "Top Chief": one 2,000-word case on Danny Meyer used in five courses in five disciplines (n = 104); post-survey judgements moved from 38% neutral to 3.5% neutral and from short to argued answers; integrative-thinking gains "may not accrue equally across all student populations" [documented: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8978504/]. Eynesbury's Coglin Clothing Company "living case" ran across eight courses, 2010–13 [documented: abstract, DOI 10.4018/978-1-5225-3153-1.ch012]. Emory Goizueta's 2025 "Growth Week" closes the first semester with one real company (Coca-Cola) across all core subjects [documented: emorybusiness.com, 14 Jan 2026]. **Not found:** a source showing HBS itself reusing one case across required courses by design; HBS cases are reused across programmes and years, and the A/B/C series is the house form for a continuing case [probable; not verified at source this pass].
- **Open University: a common resource across modules.** In 1999 the OU Systems department found "basic systems principles and practices were being taught differently across the various modules", so it built T551, a study pack "used across a number of separate modules"; it served three modules, at least 7,000 students, then was licensed (Arab Open University) and opened on OpenLearn (32,329 unique visitors by January 2012) [documented: Lane, "Reuse and repurpose: the life story of an (open) educational resource", https://oro.open.ac.uk/33244/1/OER_lifestory_paper.pdf]. OpenLearn hosts "over 800 free online courses developed from excerpts of its paid-for modules" [documented: Law and Jelfs, Open Praxis 2016, https://openpraxis.org/articles/363/files/submission/proof/363-1-1084-1-10-20211019.pdf]. **Bearing [argued]:** the OU's reason for a shared resource was consistency of a core idea across modules, and its gain was reach; the same object served formal and free learners at different depths, which is the program's three-depth rule.

- **Design schools: an anchor problem that returns.** The nine-square grid problem, devised by Hejduk, Slutzky and Hirsche at the University of Texas (1954–56) and carried to Cooper Union, ran as a year-long first-year studio (Deamer, *AD* 2023, DOI 10.1002/ad.2941) and returned in the second-year Design II studios as "a brilliantly inventive three-dimensional transformation of the School's own 'Nine-Square' program" [documented: Cooper Union, *Architecture at Cooper* 2010–11, https://cooper.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/assets/architecture/files/ArchAtCooper_05_2010-11.pdf; MoMA catalogue *Education of an Architect*, 1971]. **Bearing [argued]:** design education's own anchor is a *problem* that returns transformed at a higher level, which is Bruner's spiral in studio form; the ballet equivalent is a work that returns with a new question each time, not the same reading again.
- **Not searched in depth this pass:** museum object libraries and handling collections (object-based learning is in `S_structure_study.md` via Chatterjee and Hannan); Coursera's internal guidance on splitting long material.

### L2. Decision 2: how long, and how many parts

- **Length and completion (open courses).** Jordan (2014, *IRRODL* 15(1), DOI 10.19173/irrodl.v15i1.1651), 91 MOOCs for enrolment and 39 for completion: median completion 6.5% of enrolment; course length (weeks) predicted completion: ln(% completed) = 2.648 − 0.100 × weeks, R² = 0.237 [documented: ERIC full text https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1024344.pdf]. Read as arithmetic: **each added week multiplies completion by about 0.90** (e^−0.1). But measured against *active* learners, completion (median 9.8%) showed **no relationship with length**: "enrolled students may be put off starting longer courses, but this is less of an issue for those who do become actively engaged" [documented: same]. Longer courses also drew *more* enrolments (R² = 0.055) [documented]. Jordan (2015) on 221 courses is in `S_structure_study.md` and not repeated.
- **Bearing [argued].** Length costs completion mainly at the door, through starting, not through the middle. For a self-paced module inside a program, where the learner has already "enrolled" in the program, the cost of length falls on the *first act's* promise and the *visible size* of the whole, not on a per-week decay.
- **Sequences of courses: attrition is between parts.** MITx Supply Chain Management MicroMasters (five courses and a final exam): of 17,871 verified registrants (2014–19), 8.9% earned the credential; 47.6% of leavers dropped before finishing the first course and 25.3% after one course [documented: Moreno, MIT thesis 2019, https://hdl.handle.net/1721.1/122250]. The programme team: "Most attrition occurs between, not within, courses in a MicroMasters sequence"; placing the most demanding course first "discouraged those learners … right off the bat", so they moved learners to start with the domain course and "explain the math when it is first being applied" [documented: "MicroMasters are not just a Bunch of MOOCs", https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-2356/experience_short18.pdf]. Verified dropout per course in 2017 fell along the sequence: SC0x 48%, SC1x 37%, SC2x 32%, SC3x 22%, SC4x 16% [documented: Borrella et al. 2019, DOI 10.1145/3330430.3333634]. Learners "further along in their course sequence are more likely to return to take the next course", and taking courses in order raised return rates [documented: Park, MIT thesis 2022, https://hdl.handle.net/1721.1/144685]. Coursera Specialisations and MicroMasters: 30–40% of starters *planned* to earn the credential; administrators reported Specialisation completion of 15% or less, most 5% or less [documented: EDUCAUSE Review, June 2019, https://er.educause.edu/articles/2019/6/mooc-based-alternative-credentials-whats-the-value-for-the-learner].
- **Bearing [argued].** Each boundary between parts is a door out. More parts give more finish lines but also more exits; the MITx data say survivors become steadily more likely to continue, so the number of parts matters most at the first one or two boundaries. Put the hardest material (here, the court treatise) later, explain it where it is first needed, and make the step from one act to the next a continuation, not a new enrolment.
- **Serial media.** Netflix (September 2015) analysed first seasons of 25 series in 16 countries and named, for each, the "hooked" episode after which 70% of viewers finished the season: **no series hooked viewers with the pilot**; the hook fell at episode 2 for eight series, 3–4 for ten, 5–6 for five, and 8 for two (*Arrow*, *How I Met Your Mother*); the hooked episode "had no correlation to overall viewership numbers or viewer attrition" [documented: Netflix release via PR Newswire; Variety, 23 Sep 2015, https://variety.com/2015/digital/news/netflix-tv-show-data-viewer-episode-study-1201600746/; The Verge, same day]. Caveat: self-reported company data, method not published; on-demand viewing only [documented as Netflix's own number].
- **Bearing [argued].** Even professionally built serials need two to four episodes to hold an audience. A module's opening must deliver its "hook" inside the first few sittings, and the prologue should be short enough that the hook arrives inside it.
- **Goal gradient and progress.** Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng (2006, *Journal of Marketing Research* 43(1): 39–58): café reward members bought more often as they neared the reward (inter-purchase times fell 20% across a card); a 12-stamp card with two bonus stamps was completed in 12.7 days against 15.6 for a plain 10-stamp card; and members **slowed after the first reward** before accelerating again ("post-reward resetting") [documented: author PDF https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/1200/goalgradient.pdf; Columbia summary]. Nunes and Drèze (2006, *Journal of Consumer Research* 32(4): 504–512): converting an eight-step task into a ten-step task with two steps done increased completion and cut completion time [documented: abstract]; the widely cited figures (34% against 19% completion in a car-wash field test) [probable: not seen at source]. Bonezzi, Brendl and De Angelis (2011, *Psychological Science*): motivation can sag "about halfway to the end state" ("stuck in the middle") [documented: abstract]. Amir and Ariely (2008, *JEP: LMC*): discrete progress markers help when distance to the goal is uncertain but "may generate complacency" and reduce effort when progress is already visible [documented: abstract and author PDF https://people.duke.edu/~dandan/webfiles/PapersOther/Resting%20on%20Laurels.pdf].
- **Bearing [argued].** Parts with their own finish line exploit the goal gradient twice per part (start and end) and shrink the "middle". But each completed part is a reward after which effort resets, which is the same thing the MITx between-course data show. The design answer is not fewer parts but a *visible next step* at every finish line (the next act's opening already in view) and parts short enough that their middles are short.

- **Module size inside a credit-bearing distance university.** Nguyen, Thorne and Rienties (2018, *Behaviormetrika* 45: 597–614, DOI 10.1007/s41237-018-0060-1), 205 Open University undergraduate modules, 123,916 students who completed (withdrawals excluded): students had **42% higher odds of passing a 30-credit module than a 60-credit module** (OR 1.42), and **each added study-break week raised the odds of passing by 28%** (OR 1.28, 95% CI 1.14–1.43); modules averaged 29 weeks (21–35) [documented: Springer full text, DOI above]. OU sizing: most modules are 30 or 60 credits; a 30-credit module needs 8–9 hours a week over 30 weeks or 14 over 22; a 60-credit module 16–18 hours a week [documented: OpenLearn "How will I find time to study?"]; a 30-credit module is planned at about 300 hours (SDK125 estimated at 296–312) [documented: Davey and Dommett, eSTEeM report]. **Caveat:** observational; 30-credit modules may differ in other ways [argued].
- **Platform norms (benchmarks, not evidence).** FutureLearn: courses "typically … two to six hours per week" [documented: FutureLearn FAQ]; courses run from 2 to 6+ weeks, 12 to 150+ steps [documented: UConn FutureLearn course-builder guide]; on average 22% of active learners "fully participate" (12% of joiners) [documented: FutureLearn blog, 21 Aug 2014, a company figure]. Common Microcredential Framework (European MOOC Consortium, 2019; FutureLearn, FUN, Miríadax, EduOpen, OpenupEd): a microcredential is **100–150 hours** (4–6 ECTS) [documented: https://emc.eadtu.eu/images/CMF_for_MOOCs_and_SLPs.pdf; FutureLearn CMF page]. Coursera courses typically four to twelve weeks at one to two hours of video a week (already in `S_structure_study.md`). FutureLearn analytics: real weekly durations varied up to tenfold between weeks of one course, which "may discourage" engagement [documented: Ballesteros Mesa and Wilde, St Andrews 2016, https://hdl.handle.net/10023/12199].
- **Bearing [argued].** Against these benchmarks, F1 as a whole (34 hours) is a short course; the proposed ballet module (44 hours) is **larger than all of F1** and about a third of a microcredential; each 5–8 hour act is the size of one FutureLearn course of two to three weeks. Unevenness between parts is itself a cost: acts of 2, 5, 6, 6, 8, 6, 8 and 3 hours are less even than they need to be.

### L3. Decision 3: one returning work or many

- **Variation theory.** Marton and Pang (2006, *Journal of the Learning Sciences* 15(2): 193–220): "To discern an aspect, the learner must experience potential alternatives, that is, variation in a dimension corresponding to that aspect, against the background of invariance in other aspects" [documented: abstract, DOI 10.1207/s15327809jls1502_2]. The recommended sequence is contrast, then generalisation (the same aspect seen in different instances), then fusion (several aspects varying together) [documented: Kullberg, Runesson Kempe and Marton 2017, *ZDM*, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11858-017-0858-4]. Learning studies report large differences: 70% against 30% reaching the target understanding of an economics concept (Pang and Marton 2003, 2005), 85% against 25% in accounting (Rovio-Johansson) [documented as reported in Åkerlind's review, *HERDSA Review of Higher Education* 2 (2015), https://herdsa.org.au/system/files/HERDSARHE2015v02p05_0.pdf]; in self-study materials, 74.4% against 40.0% (Pang and Marton, PMC7089119) [documented]. A 2025 trial with 375 kindergarten children found contrast examples beat same-category examples (Lam, *IJLLS*) [documented: abstract]. **Caveat:** most learning studies are small and quasi-experimental [argued].
- **Bearing [argued].** A returning work is a variation design: the story and score stay invariant while the maker, the city, the body, the record and the politics vary. That is exactly the condition under which "what a remake changes" becomes discernible. It is the strongest argument *for* a braid. But variation theory also says that generalisation needs the same aspect seen *in different instances*: the five modes of remaking must be seen in more than one work, or the learner learns "what happened to *Giselle*", not "what remaking is".
- **Variability and generalisation.** Raviv, Lupyan and Green (2022, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 26(6): 462–483), review across categorisation, perception, motor learning, language and machine learning: "Learning from less variable input is often fast, but may fail to generalize to new stimuli; learning with more variable input is initially slower, but typically yields better generalization" [documented: highlights and PubMed abstract 35577719]. With Gentner et al. (L1) and Alfieri et al. (d = 0.50 for comparison), this is the evidence for the narrowing risk: one exemplar met many times makes a fast, vivid and narrow category [argued from documented findings].
- **Interleaving** (Brunmair and Richter 2019) is already in `S_structure_study.md`: it helps telling visual styles apart (paintings g = 0.67), not arguments. **Bearing [argued]:** the braid's versions should be shown *side by side* as images (Paris 1841, St Petersburg, Havana, Louisiana, Khan) at least once, as a comparison wall, not only one version per act.
- **What is most performed (Bachtrack listings; "not exhaustive", counts are of listed performances).** 2025 (6,267 dance performances listed): 1 *The Nutcracker*, 2 *Swan Lake*, 3 *Romeo and Juliet*, 4 *The Sleeping Beauty*, 5 Gutiérrez *Le Bal de Paris*, **6 *Giselle***, 7 Bourne *The Midnight Bell*, 8 *Don Quixote*, 9 *Cinderella*, 10 Scott *Hänsel und Gretel* [documented: Bachtrack PDF https://cdn.bachtrack.com/files/467510-Annual%20classical%20music%20statistics%202025.pdf]. 2023: *Nutcracker*, *Swan Lake*, *Sleeping Beauty*, *Romeo and Juliet*, *Cinderella*, **6 *Giselle***, *Don Quixote*, *Revelations*, *Night Creature*, *For Four* [documented: https://cdn.bachtrack.com/files/350970-Annual%20classical%20music%20statistics%202023.pdf]. 2024: *Nutcracker* about 400 performances ahead of *Swan Lake* in second; Wheeldon's *Alice* entered the top ten [documented: Violin Channel and International Arts Manager reports of Bachtrack's 2024 statistics]. 2015: *Swan Lake* 2, *Giselle* 7; 2016: *Swan Lake* 2, *Giselle* 4; 2017: *Swan Lake* 3, *Giselle* 6 [probable: Bachtrack PDFs, but the 2017 PDF's dance counts duplicate the opera counts, so only ranks are used]. **Pattern [documented across the years seen]:** *Swan Lake* is the second most performed ballet every year listed; *Giselle* sits between fourth and seventh.
- **Bearing [argued].** On repertory weight, *Swan Lake* is the ballet a learner is most likely to see, stream or have heard of; *Giselle* is the oldest work in the top ten and the only Romantic one. Popularity argues for *Swan Lake*; history (an 1841 origin with documents; a lost-then-returned work) argues for *Giselle*.
- **Remakes (no census exists; ranges from this repository's own works files and press).** *Giselle*: Paris 1841; Petipa's St Petersburg revisions and Stepanov notation; Sergeyev in Paris 1924; Alonso, Havana (1943 role; Paris Opéra 1972); Ek, Cullberg 1982 (asylum; taken into the Paris Opéra 1993 and La Scala 1997); Franklin's *Creole Giselle*, Dance Theatre of Harlem 1984; Lacotte's reconstruction with 1841 designs; Khan, ENB 2016 (broadcast in five countries and screened in more than ten, 2019); Masilo 2017; Ratmansky reconstruction 2019 [documented: works/W3; Fyinpaper 2020; Voci dell'Opera 2020; Guardian 23 Sep 2019; Demerson, Project MUSE]. In autumn 2019 three *Giselle*s toured the UK at once (Khan, Masilo, Bintley–Samsova) [documented: Guardian]. *Swan Lake*: Moscow 1877; Petipa–Ivanov 1895; Gorsky; Burmeister 1953; Grigorovich; Nureyev 1984; Tokyo 1946; Neumeier's *Illusions – like Swan Lake* 1976; Ek 1987; Bourne 1995; Masilo 2010; Ratmansky reconstruction (Zurich 2016); outside ballet, *Black Swan* (film, 2010) [probable from working knowledge and W3; dates to verify before teaching].
- **Bearing [argued].** Both works have a full remake ladder in all five modes. *Giselle*'s ladder is unusually *global* and *documented by its makers* (Gautier's account; Franklin, Khan and Masilo interviewed); *Swan Lake*'s is unusually *wide across media* and includes the design-led remake (Bourne) that design learners know. Neither has a court-period ancestor, so neither can braid Act I.

### L4. Decision 4: which part to build first

- **Where engagement is decided.** Evans, Baker and Dee (2016, *Journal of Higher Education* 87(2)), 44 MOOCs, 2.1 million student observations, 2,900 lectures: "participation declines rapidly in the first week but subsequently flattens out"; a lecture's position in a released batch and its *title wording* were associated with watching [documented: abstract, DOI 10.1080/00221546.2016.11777400]. Jordan (2015), already in the structure study: "the first and second weeks appear to be critical". Ye and Biswas (2014, *Journal of Learning Analytics*), Vanderbilt POSA MOOC: of week-one learners, those who only watched dropped out by week 4 at 60%, those who also took the quiz at 20% [documented: DOI 10.18608/jla.2014.13.14]. Taylor, Veeramachaneni and O'Reilly (2014, MITx 6.002x): one week of behaviour predicted persistence to the end with AUC about 0.7 [documented: arXiv 1408.3382].
- **Bearing [argued].** Two lessons for the first build: (1) the opening is where most learners are won or lost, so it must be built and tested before anything else is built at scale; (2) the opening must contain a *task the learner does* in the first sitting or two, because doing, not watching, separates the learners who stay.
- **How many testers.** Nielsen and Landauer (1993, INTERCHI): problem discovery follows a Poisson process; average single-user discovery rate 31% across 11 studies; maximum benefit-to-cost at about four evaluations in a "medium" example [documented: ACM abstract, DOI 10.1145/169059.169166]; Nielsen's rule of thumb: five users find about 85%, so run three rounds of five rather than one of fifteen [documented: NN/g, 2000]. **Contested:** Schmettow (2012, *CACM*) shows heterogeneity of users and problems makes the formula "grossly understate" the sample needed [documented: https://cacm.acm.org/research/sample-size-in-usability-studies/]. The program already plans "five testers" (Final Structure v3 §11) and has run a six-persona reader board on one ballet page ("The shoe") through three rounds (`../board/SUMMARY.md`) [documented in repository].
- **Bearing [argued].** Five real learners per round, in at least two rounds, is the right size for finding what breaks in an opening; it is not enough to measure completion. Completion needs a cohort (dozens, then hundreds) and a whole act.

### L5. Decision 5: the Gulf strand (extends scope 01 §3.5 and W3 §10, which hold the opera houses, Saudi licensing, Cairo and Beirut)

**The Gulf states.** In this pass, as in the earlier ones, **no resident professional ballet company was found in any Gulf state**; ballet there is (a) taught in private studios, mostly founded by foreign-trained women, (b) presented by opera houses and festivals as touring seasons, and (c) since 2020 in Saudi Arabia, an object of state licensing and training programmes. Scope the claim to "none found".

- **Algeria.** A "national ballet, Russian-style" from the 1960s (Skandrani's account) [probable]; the Ballet National was constituted as a public body by Executive Decree 92-290 of 7 July 1992, chiefly for national folk dance [documented: decree text, Arabic]; Soviet Azerbaijan staged *The Three Revolutions* in Algiers in 1973 [documented: Moine, HAL 2023, title and abstract only].

**Bearing [argued].** Three findings. (1) The Gulf's ballet story is real but is a story of *routes and institutions* (an émigré teacher, a presenting house, a licensing commission, a syllabus exam board), not of *made works*: no Gulf-made ballet with a documented concept, team and afterlife was found. (2) The routes match the module's existing export models exactly: émigré teachers (Al Neyadi in Abu Dhabi, as Eliana Pavlova in Kamakura and the Russian couple in Tunis), the examined syllabus (RAD in Muscat, Doha and Kuwait), the presenting opera house (Muscat, Dubai, Doha, Kuwait, Manama), Soviet state teachers (Baghdad, Damascus, Cairo), and now a state commission buying training from Beirut (TPAC with Caracalla). (3) Arabic-language press adds facts and contradictions not in English (the 1998 "first" French ballet in the Emirates; the Damascus school's dates; the Al Neyadi nationality discrepancy), so an Arabic reader is needed to make any of this teachable, and interviews would be needed for a Gulf case at work-level depth.
