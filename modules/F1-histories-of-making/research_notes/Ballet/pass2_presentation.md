# Ballet online, pass 2: how it is presented and taught

Read on 20 October 2026 for one question: what should a course page on "ballet as a history of making" (materials, techniques, makers, bodies, patronage, labour) look and read like for a first-time reader? Eighteen presentations were examined by fetching each page and reading what it does: its spine, the size of a reading unit, how it uses images and objects, how much apparatus it shows and where, what its interactives add, what a first-time reader takes away, and what it leaves out. Where a page could not be reached it says "not found" and names the nearest thing that could be. Word counts are estimates from the fetched text and exclude navigation and footers.

A caution that runs through everything below: almost every page is published by the institution it describes (a company, a shoemaker, an academy, a museum holding the object). None cites sources inline except the Met essay (two endnotes) and the London Museum blogs (a hedge, a linked conservation post). The design lesson is as much about what is absent as what is there.

## 2. The presentations, briefly

**1–4. V&A Theatre & Performance.** The two collection landings are shop windows: one paragraph of chronology, then cards. The cards are the best thing on them, because each carries a museum number, a date, a place and a display status ("Not on display", "View by appointment"), so a reader learns that the object is real and where it is. The introduction article is the house's one long read: six dated sections, seven captioned images with numbers, a declared origin ("written for the 2010–11 exhibition") and no sources for its numbers. Designers are everywhere, makers nowhere, except a single "Bonn & Mackenzie" and a "maker unknown". The conservation film shows a making-adjacent craft (humidifying tulle) with a named conservator, which is more than any other V&A unit gives, yet the tutu itself has no maker, date or number on the page. A first-time reader leaves with Diaghilev, Bakst, Picasso and the idea that costumes survive in drawers.

**5. Royal Ballet and Opera.** The news site could not be fetched (server error), so the "Ballet essentials" and "Behind the scenes" articles are not found. The 2012 pointe-shoe film survives on YouTube: two dancers visit Freed, meet the makers, and the factory is shown as a room of men at benches building a shoe from the inside out. Its companion straps a camera to a shoe. What the film teaches that no page does is scale and touch: paste, hessian, a hammer, a last, a maker's hands. What it withholds is names and numbers; the one figure (£250,000 a year on footwear) comes from the description, not the film.

**6–12. Google Arts & Culture.** The format is a vertical scroll of panels, 30–150 words each, one image per panel, caption under it with a bold institution line, a "Tap to explore" prompt that is mostly a disclosure toggle. It rewards institutions with a workshop to show. The Bolshoi costume-department story is the richest making unit in this whole survey: a sewing shop on the sixth floor, staff of 30–40 years' tenure, "until 2007 all tutus were made by one seamstress", "one day and 15–28 metres of fabric" per tutu, a dye house that paints imitation embroidery, pointe shoes built on individual lasts with "large needles and miniature nails". It names the chief designer and never a seamstress. The Australian Ballet's tutu story is a good object unit (lead image, two details, 60 words, designer and year) with mismatched dates left unexplained. The Bastille story shows a fabric library and a wig shop in 300 words and names an architect but no artisan. The Bolshoi building story is a dated building chronology with elites named and gilders not. The Chemin des Étoiles is the one place a dancer's career is treated as a labour structure (appointment, rank, retirement at 42). The ROH partner page was not reachable; the nearest footwear story is Warsaw's, which is a themed shoe gallery with a credited photographer and no construction.

**13. Library of Congress.** An exhibition that is a finding aid: a 240-word overview and ~58 items each with a caption, a collection name, a call number and a digital ID. It is the most honest apparatus in the survey (you can order the item) and the least readable (no section headings, duplicated numbers, metadata that says "Cologne"). Makers absent; designers named only at item level.

**14. NGA Australia.** The current page is a stub pointing to an archived 2010 site; the archive is a thumbnail grid of 318 works, 12 to a page, organised by 35 ballets, captioned ARTIST + role + ballet. The intro is the only prose, and it contains the survey's single most useful sentence about labour: the costumes "bear the evidence of hard and continuous use" and were made by "the experienced hands of their makers". The site never follows that sentence.

**15. Homans, *Apollo's Angels*.** The spine is nation-states in sequence, each chapter a court or a regime: kings, Enlightenment, Revolution, the Romantic ballerina, Denmark, Italy, the Tsars, Diaghilev, Communism, Britain, America twice, then an epilogue arguing the art is dying. It is a history of ideas and technique carried by masters. Shoes, tutus, workshops and non-European ballet do not have chapters. Its value for the course is the ordering (court → state → émigré → America) and the lesson that a strong argumentative close is itself a dispute worth showing.

**16. ENB, Akram Khan's Giselle.** The production page is a sales page with complete creative credits, a running time, and three sentences of story that avoid the words "migrant" and "garment factory" (the DVD page uses them). The timeline is a tour log with photographer credits. The 8-minute film (Maxime Dos, 2018) is where the making lives: Khan, Rojo, Lamagna, Little and three dancers in the studio from R&D to Manchester. Tim Yip's wall and costumes are credited, never shown being built.

**17–18. Mariinsky and Vaganova.** Both are chains of masters: foreign ballet masters arriving, Petipa, a Soviet middle, a present. Both are unsourced, undated in the footer sense (© 2014–2015), and image-poor (6 captioned gallery images; 2 uncredited portraits). The Mariinsky page does not mention Vaganova; the Vaganova page skips 1917. The one body fact in either is the funnel: thousands apply, 70 accepted, 30 finish. A first-time reader takes away a lineage and a boast.

**19. Freed of London.** No "In the making" page exists under that name. The catalogue gives the firm's own numbers (250,000 pairs, two-thirds bespoke, 3 mm), the History page is a dated timeline of shop addresses with uncaptioned photographs, and one 2019 post explains two machine steps (channelling a groove in the sole; welting a chain stitch through it) with four captioned photographs and the detail that the machine-maker closed in 2000 so parts are made by an engineer in-house. The maker's-mark symbols (anchor, butterfly, crown, fish…) appear on the homepage as images and are never explained, which is the survey's clearest missed opportunity: the mark is the one place a maker is visible to the dancer.

**20. The Met, Speelberg on Bakst.** The strongest object pages: seven drawings shown full width, each caption a full tombstone (artist and nationality, title, date, medium, dimensions in two units, credit line, accession number), workshop attributions marked "or Workshop", two endnotes, the collection's own provenance told in the body. It is one continuous 1,600-word essay with no subheads, timed at 6 minutes. It names assistants as copyists by transfer paper and then drops them; it uses "exotic" without quotation marks.

**21. London Museum, the Pavlova Swan.** Two blog posts, a costume-maker's and a conservator's, that together are the best model for a page about making. Kincel names the maker (Madame Manya), the material (tarlatan, starched cotton, later nylon tulle), the technology that changed the look (gaslight; the Industrial Revolution's net), the hours (30–40 for a skirt, "all hand-sewn") and her own hands' strain reconstructing it. Austin names the parts (boned bodice, gelatine sequins that dissolve in water, a metal hoop, failed elastic), the tools (low-suction vacuum, cold steam, Japanese tissue) and leaves her hedges visible ("believed to be goose", a date she does not resolve). Both lack an accession number in the text and any economics.

**22. NYCB.** "Ballet 101" was not found on nycballet.com. What exists is a repertory page per ballet with a credits block (premiere date, original cast, music opus, "Costumes: Karinska", sets and lighting by period) and three "Anatomy of a Dance" films in which dancers talk through a passage. The Costumes page says the company has its own shop by the Hudson with seamstresses and dressers, names a director of costumes and two jewellers, and embeds three films (one on remaking Theme and Variations tutus). Nothing on method, hours or Karinska.

**23. The two films.** *Ballets Russes* is a chronological survivor's history: a 2000 reunion of octogenarians, 90 hours of interview cut against unseen rehearsal reels, a narrator, 118 minutes; the "ballet wars", touring 110 cities in five months without a union, dancers who stayed in Australia. It is the survey's only unit where labour is the subject, because the labourers are the witnesses. *A Ballerina's Tale* is one body through an injury and a surgery at ABT; reviewers found it thin, and it omits shoes, costume and institution almost entirely.

## 3. The three clearest presentations, and why

**First: London Museum, the Pavlova Swan pair (Kincel 2025 + Austin 2021).** Clear because the spine is the object taken apart, in an order a maker would use: who wore it, what it is made of, why that material (gaslight, starch, the net factories), how it is built (yoke, knickers, pleated layers, bodice), then how it is kept (vacuum, steam, tissue). Pacing: 1,300–1,800 words in four or five headed sections, each with its own gallery, so the reader can stop after any section. Image-to-text: roughly one image per 70 words in the conservation post, each a process stage, each captioned under the image with a © line. Caveats sit in the sentence, not a footer: "allegedly", "believed to be goose", a date range the author does not resolve. A dispute is shown by linking the other post rather than settling it. What a designer can act on: headed sections in making order; a gallery per section; captions that name material and step; hedges inline; the maker named in the first section.

**Second: Google Arts & Culture / Bolshoi, the costume department.** Clear because the spine is the building walked floor by floor (sewing → dye and print → headwear → shoe), so a first-time reader learns there are four crafts, not one. Pacing: nine panels of 100–150 words, one image each, a number in nearly every panel (sixth floor; 30–40 years; one seamstress until 2007; one day and 15–28 metres; needles and miniature nails). Image-to-text: 1:1, image above, caption under with year and holder in bold. Caveats: none, which is the weakness; the dates in captions (2017 for the workrooms) do the honest work. A designer can act on: a workshop-by-workshop ordering; one concrete number per panel; a photograph of the room rather than the finished costume; a bold holder line under every image. It should be corrected by naming the seamstress where the Bolshoi does not.

**Third: the Met, Speelberg on Bakst.** Clear because every image is treated as evidence: full width, a complete tombstone, an accession number you can follow, "or Workshop" where attribution is unsure, and two endnotes pointing to a 1913 source. Pacing: one 6-minute read with no subheads, which works only because the images themselves paragraph the essay (seven figures across ~1,600 words, one every ~230 words). Caveats sit in the caption ("or Workshop") and in the endnotes. A dispute (who drew what) is shown as an attribution label, not a paragraph. A designer can act on: the caption as tombstone (maker, title, date, medium, dimensions, holder, number); figure pairs for comparison; a declared read-time; endnotes rather than nothing.

Runner-up for a different reason: the Library of Congress items list, because it is the only unit where every object has a number you could order by; its failure is pure presentation (no headings, 58 entries in one column, errors uncorrected).

## 4. What a page about making should borrow and what it should avoid

**Borrow**

- The tombstone caption from the Met: maker (or "maker unknown", as the V&A dares to write once), title, date, material, dimensions, holder, number. Put it under the image, never on it. The V&A cards show the same discipline at thumbnail size, with display status added; keep the status.
- The making order from the London Museum: wearer → material → why that material then → construction → care. Head each step; give each its own gallery; let the reader stop after any.
- One concrete number per panel, from the Bolshoi: metres of fabric, hours per skirt, years of tenure, pairs per year, the retirement age. Numbers are what a first-time reader keeps.
- Workshops as rooms, from the Bolshoi and Bastille: photograph the sixth-floor sewing room, the dye chamber, the fabric library, the welting machine, not only the finished tutu.
- The maker's mark, from Freed, explained: a symbol on the sole is the one place a maker's identity reaches the dancer and the reader. The course should show the mark and the hand it stands for.
- Hedges in the sentence, from Austin and Kincel: "believed to be goose, pending analysis"; "allegedly"; a date range left as a range. A dispute is two linked pages or an "or Workshop" label, not a settled paragraph.
- Witnesses for labour, from *Ballets Russes* and the Chemin des Étoiles: the people who did the work, their schedules (110 cities in five months; three to four hours before curtain), the structures (rank, appointment, retirement at 42). Short quotes, named.
- A declared origin and read-time, from the V&A ("written for the 2010–11 exhibition") and the Met ("6 minutes").
- Homans's ordering (court → state → émigré → America) as a backbone to hang the makers on, with its epilogue shown as a disputed claim.

**Avoid**

- Designer-only credit lines ("Costumes: Karinska"; "sets and costumes by Tim Yip") with no cutter, dyer, milliner or shoemaker. Every V&A, NYCB, ENB and NGA unit does this. If the maker is unknown, write "maker unknown".
- The chain-of-masters history (Mariinsky, Vaganova, Homans): a list of names and dates with no object, no room, no body. It reads as lineage, not making.
- Institutional self-description without a source: the Bolshoi's "most skilled costume department", Freed's "world leading", the Mariinsky's "closely linked with the history of European choreographic art". Keep the number, drop the adjective, name who said it.
- "Tap to explore" and carousels that only reveal text a paragraph could hold. None of the interactives surveyed explained anything a captioned photograph could not; the films did (paste, needles, steam), so use film for touch and time, and still images for evidence.
- Caption inconsistencies left unexplained (the Australian Ballet's costume 1970 / photo 2013; the étoiles' two spellings). If the costume and the photograph have different dates, say which is which.
- The finding-aid without a spine (LoC): numbers without headings exhaust a first-time reader. Keep the call number; add the section.
- The promotional close: shop, membership, newsletter. Every page surveyed ends there; a course page should end with the next maker.
