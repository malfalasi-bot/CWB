1. The short answers

Should every ballet have its own card? Yes. The register now holds 148 works from 1581 to 2022: 35 have full cards already, 48 need full cards, and 65 get short cards of about 150 words. It includes 34 works by women choreographers and 29 made outside western Europe, North America and Russia. Beyond the register, an index of 500 to 1,500 works can be harvested from open data. A well-read person knows 100 to 150 ballets; a one-volume reference covers 500 to 1,500; tens of thousands exist.

Can the shoe, the fashion and the products stay? Yes, as their own teaching strand, not as background: ten object studies of about two hours each (the pointe shoe, the tutu, stage to street, the ballet flat and others; section 3.3), each taught on its own and also placed in the act where its object first mattered. There are now 29 object and product cards (the pointe shoe as a product line, the tutu as a family, the leotard, the ballet flat, the Train Bleu bathing suit, the Ballets Russes posters, the Nutcracker as a licensing economy, balletcore) and five "collection logics" taken from ballet itself (the divertissement, the season, the mixed bill, costume families, the repertory with an anchor work). Each is read as a collection: its anchor, its members, its rules, what may vary, what is refused, how it is carried and sold. That is the program's threshold "the collection precedes the object", taught on real cases.

How can concepts be discussed beyond Giselle and Swan Lake? Through 24 concept threads that cut across the whole register, each taught on a comparison wall of six to ten ballets from at least three centuries and three regions. "The bird-woman" runs from La Sylphide to The Firebird, a Tatar bird-maiden (Shurale, Kazan 1950), a Chinese fish spirit (The Fish Beauty, Beijing 1959) and Bourne's male swans. "A woman against family and patriarch" runs from Baku 1940 to Beijing 1964, 1981 and 2001 and Seoul 1986. The strongest finding in the learning research supports this: comparing two cases roughly doubled transfer over studying the same cases one at a time, and across 57 experiments case comparison beat single-case study (Gentner, Loewenstein and Thompson 2003; Alfieri and others 2013, effect size 0.50).

F1 only, or wider? Wider. F1 is 34 hours in total; the ballet module alone was 44. And about half of what it asks learners to make (a premise, rules and refusals, obligations, a remake, a credit line) belongs to Concept DNA fields that other modules own. Recommendation: a shared ballet case world in the program's world set, which F1 and about a dozen other modules draw on; Giselle written as the program's historical exemplar Concept DNA; an F1 history core of about 6 hours; and the six acts kept as an optional deep path of about 35 hours, built from the same cards. Section 4 gives the evidence.

Four acts or six? Six, but evened to 5 or 6 hours each, about 35 hours in all. Section 5.

The other questions: Giselle stays as the returning work, with Swan Lake at three fixed points; the first build is the Prologue plus the full Giselle 1841 case, tested with five learners per lane in two rounds; the Gulf gets a route case now (how ballet reached the Gulf) plus the Cairo work El-Leila El-Kebira, and the missing Gulf-made ballet becomes a commissioning brief for learners. Sections 6 to 8.

Two cards in the new form (from works/W2_modernism.md):

The Firebird · 1910 · Paris, Palais Garnier The idea: a Russian fairy tale made for export: a prince frees captive princesses from a sorcerer with the feather of a magic bird. Built from: Afanasyev's folk tales; Polonsky's poem "A Winter's Journey"; Rimsky-Korsakov's fairy-opera music; the neo-Russian craft revival of Abramtsevo and Talashkino. The world: classical dance on pointe for magic, folk round-dance for humans, grotesque angles for monsters; magic harmony against folk tunes; no imported story, no inserted divertissement. The look: a sorcerer's overgrown garden with a golden apple tree; Slavic festival dress stencilled to imitate brocade; the bird in red-orange with a feathered headdress. Anchor image: the bird. The family: princesses as a corps, monsters as a chorus, the bird as the single exception. Off the stage: concert suites (1911, 1919, 1945) carried the music without the dance; the Firebird became a sign of Russianness. Remade as: Goncharova's new designs (1926); Balanchine with Chagall's designs (1949); staged in Russia only from 1921. Threads: two worlds · the bird-woman · folk as national idiom · the fairy tale. Credit: an idea worked out by a committee in Diaghilev's circle; music Stravinsky; choreography Fokine; designs Golovin and Bakst.

Le Train Bleu · 1924 · Paris, Théâtre des Champs-Élysées The idea: modern leisure as spectacle: sport and flirting on a Riviera beach at the end of the luxury train line. Built from: the Train Bleu sleeper to the Côte d'Azur; the 1924 Paris Olympics; Suzanne Lenglen and the Prince of Wales; Offenbach operetta; a young dancer's acrobatics. The world: real clothes, real sports, real places; serves, swings and poses like snapshots; refuses fantasy costume, Russianness and the past. The look: angular bathing cabins; knitted wool bathing suits, tennis whites, plus-fours, in brown, grey, pink, blue and black. Anchor image: the knitted bathing suit. The family: hand-knitted suits for the leads, machine-knitted for the chorus. Off the stage: the sportswear look went back to the street; a dancer's suede skull cap "set a fashion"; Picasso's curtain, enlarged from a small gouache, was a season asset later attached to this ballet. Remade as: the Paris Opéra (1992); English National Ballet (2025), in Lycra versions of Chanel's designs made with Chanel's permission. Threads: sport, leisure and the everyday · the machine age. Credit: idea and libretto Cocteau; music Milhaud; choreography Nijinska; set Laurens; costumes Chanel; curtain Picasso, painted at full size by Shervashidze.

The full card keeps all ten headings, reordered the same way: idea and roots first, the world and its look next, then the family, the translations and the versions, with the team and the record last.

3.3 The objects and collections strand: ten object studies

The shoe, the tutu and the fashion are not lost, and they are not a column in a table: each is an object study, a short block of five or six sittings (about two hours) that can be taught on its own. Every study follows the same five moves, which are the moves of building a product family from a world:

  1. The idea the object carries (weightlessness; rank; modernity; Russianness).
  2. How it is made: materials, construction, the workshop (with a schematic where no open photograph exists).
  3. The family: the anchor piece, the variants, the rules that keep them related, what may vary, what is refused, how variants are named.
  4. Off the stage: what it became as products, fashion, brands and media.
  5. Make: the learner designs a family for one object from their own world: anchor, variants, naming grammar, refusals (Maker); or briefs and judges one (Briefer).

3.6 Collection logics from ballet itself

  • The divertissement: a set of short numbers held by one world (Act III of The Sleeping Beauty), each detachable as a gala "single".
  • The season: Diaghilev's new "drop" of three or four one-act works a year, balanced by register, each with newly commissioned artists, carried by posters and a programme magazine.
  • The mixed bill: three works under one evening's name and image.
  • Costume families: corps identical, soloists varied, principals as the anchor, characters as exceptions; rank decides colour and decoration.
  • The repertory with an anchor work: The Nutcracker brings in about 45% of NYCB's ticket revenue by the company's own figure, and pays for new work.

4. Where it lives: the evidence and the recommendation

The options: (a) inside F1 as an extension; (b) a standalone elective of 44 hours; (c) a program-wide case world in the world set; (d) the program's historical exemplar beside the planned contemporary exemplar Concept DNA; (e) a combination.

What the studies and precedents show: - Comparison is what makes reuse work. Comparing two cases roughly doubled transfer over studying them separately, and separate study of the same two cases was no better than no training (Gentner, Loewenstein and Thompson 2003). Across 57 experiments, comparison beat single-case study (Alfieri and others 2013, effect size 0.50). A rich case met five times alone is five lots of separate study; met beside a second case each time, it teaches the principle. - Recurring cases across a curriculum are established practice, but the evidence is mostly satisfaction. Medicine and nursing use "virtual families" met in every module (Eastern Virginia Medical School's CareForward has more than 40 characters; at Edith Cowan, 82% of students said the shared family helped them link units). Strathclyde's law school ran six modules inside one fictional town, Ardcalloch, and reported better coursework and exams but a "high work rate for staff". The Open University built one study pack for three modules because a core idea was being taught differently in each. Business schools that reuse one case across courses cap it at two or three classes per course so students are not bored, and name a case champion. - No precedent found puts a case of this size inside one module. - The program already plans the pieces: a world set of about 1,200 cases tagged by unit, "the exemplar Concept DNA" before M2, and "cognitive apprenticeship (worked exemplar, then the learner's own)" as a principle.

Recommendation: (c) + (d), with a small (a), and (b) as an optional route. 1. The case world. Publish the library as a "Ballet" collection in the world set. 2. The historical exemplar. Write Giselle (1841) field by field as a full Concept DNA, marked as a reading, and pair it with the contemporary exemplar when M2 is built, so every Method unit that uses it compares the two. 3. F1's core, about 7 hours: the Prologue, the full Giselle 1841 case, one non-European case (The White-Haired Girl, Tokyo 1955, or El-Leila El-Kebira, Cairo 2001 or 2002), one comparison wall, and one object study (the pointe shoe, study 1). This keeps ballet at the scale of one F1 strand among many, which F1's own argument (a web, not a line) requires. 4. The deep path, about 35 hours, as an optional route for learners who want the whole history, assembled from the same cards once the first slice has been tested. 5. The borrowing rule: one to three sittings per module; each ballet card set beside a non-ballet case from that module's own set, with a "what do these share?" task and the principle given after; the Historian voice keeps the record of which module used which card.

5. Length and number of parts

What the studies show: - Length costs starts more than finishes. Each added week cut open-course completion by about 10%, but among learners who started, completion was unrelated to length (Jordan 2014). - Smaller parts pass more often. At the Open University, 30-credit modules had 42% higher odds of being passed than 60-credit ones, and each planned break week raised the odds by 28% (Nguyen, Thorne and Rienties 2018). - Boundaries are where learners leave. In MIT's Supply Chain MicroMasters, most dropout happened between courses, not within them; dropout per course fell from 48% in the first to 16% in the last; putting the hardest course first drove learners away (Borrella and others 2019). - Uneven parts discourage. FutureLearn found weekly workloads varying up to tenfold within one course (Ballesteros Mesa and Wilde 2016). - A task early keeps learners. Learners who only watched in week one dropped out three times as often as those who did the task (Ye and Biswas 2014). - Benchmarks: a FutureLearn course runs 2–6 weeks at 2–6 hours a week; a Coursera course 4–12 weeks; F1 is 34 hours.

Recommendation: six acts, evened to 5–6 hours each, about 35 hours in all. Four acts of ten hours would each be longer than a typical online course; the history's own turning points (1661, 1832, 1890, 1909, 1945, 1980) argue for six. The epilogue's premise work moves to M1, where premise belongs, and the systems lists become library cards reached on demand.

Protect the first two weeks. The court is the least concrete material, so the default route opens at the Prologue and Act II (Giselle), then goes back to Act I as "what Paris inherited". Every act can also be entered on its own. Each act's last screen shows the next act's first case, and a break week is planned between acts.

6. The returning work

What the studies show. To notice what varies, a learner needs something held constant (variation theory, Marton and Pang 2006), so a returning work is a sound design: story and score stay, while maker, city, body and politics change. A rotating braid removes the constant. But learning from one instance generalises poorly (Raviv, Lupyan and Green 2022), so the remake modes must be seen in more than one work.

The data. On Bachtrack's annual counts of performances, Swan Lake is the second most performed ballet in every year seen, after The Nutcracker; Giselle sits fourth to seventh. Giselle has the more global and better-documented ladder of remakes (Havana, Louisiana, Stockholm, London, Johannesburg; its originator recorded how the idea came). Swan Lake has the design-led remake design learners know (Bourne) and a film (Black Swan). Ek and Masilo each remade both.

Recommendation. Giselle returns in every act from Act II; Swan Lake comes in at three fixed points: Act III (its Moscow failure beside Giselle's Petipa revision), Act VI (Ek's and Masilo's two remakes side by side, maker constant, work varied) and one comparison wall of both works' versions. Each mode of remaking is taught in at least two works, and one sitting says plainly what Giselle is not typical of.

7. What to build first

What the studies show. The first one or two weeks decide engagement (Jordan 2015; Evans, Baker and Dee 2016); a task in week one separates stayers from leavers; series need two to four episodes to hook viewers (Netflix 2015, company data); five testers find most usability problems, and three rounds of five beat one round of fifteen (Nielsen and Landauer 1993), though this is contested for varied users.

Recommendation. 1. A vertical slice, 4–5 hours: the Prologue and the full Giselle 1841 case, with its first task in the second or third sitting, the Maker and Briefer products, and the Giselle exemplar Concept DNA. Test with five learners per lane in two rounds; measure minutes per sitting, whether the first task is done, whether learners start the seventh sitting unprompted, and a transfer task (fill a card for La Sylphide from given sources). 2. Short cards for all 148 works, the 24 walls and the 29 product cards (about 4–5 person-weeks of human checking), so the library exists at once. 3. The pointe-shoe object study (study 1), whose research, register and schematic already exist from the earlier page, rebuilt on the five moves; it tests the objects strand's template. 4. A second slice outside Paris (Tokyo 1955 or Cairo 2001), so the second test is not also canonical. 5. Full cards for the 48 remaining works (about 10–14 person-weeks of checking), starting with the works that complete one-sided walls. 6. The deep path, act by act, and the other nine object studies. 7. The harvest to 500–1,500 index rows once Wikidata can be reached.

8. The Gulf

What exists. No resident professional ballet company and no Gulf-made ballet with a documented concept was found in six states. What can be documented: émigré-founded schools (Kuwait School of Dance, 1974; Bahrain Ballet Centre, 1982; Fantasia Ballet, Abu Dhabi, 1998); examined syllabi (the RAD in Muscat, Doha and Kuwait); opera houses presenting touring seasons (Doha 2010, Muscat 2011, Manama 2012, Dubai and Kuwait 2016); and Saudi Arabia's Theater and Performing Arts Commission, which licenses ballet and in 2024 bought a dance-training programme from Lebanon's Caracalla school. Nearby: the Soviet-built schools of Cairo (1958), Baghdad (1968 or 1969) and Damascus (1992). Scholarship on Gulf ballet: none found beyond opera-house studies.

Recommendation, in line with the program's rule that every module ends with a regional slot: - Now: a route case, "How ballet reached the Gulf", built on the five export models with a Gulf or Arab instance for each; El-Leila El-Kebira (Cairo) as the Arab made work; every Gulf fact labelled by its source type, and self-described "firsts" written as "described as the first". - The missing work as a brief: Maker and Briefer write the commission a Gulf house could issue (premise, roots, mode of remaking, what must not change), labelled as a design exercise, not as history. - Before the regional vertical: an Arabic reader for Gulf and Levant press and ministry sources; consented interviews (a school founder, an opera-house programmer, a commission officer); a Russian reader for the Soviet side. Some people in this history have chosen anonymity, and the protocol for living people applies.
