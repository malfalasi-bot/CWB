1. What was wrong with "The shoe"

  • It was a story, not a unit. It took one object and told its story well enough, but it never said what idea the shoe serves, where it sits in ballet, or what the learner would carry away. It began from an object and worked outwards, instead of starting from the subject and choosing the object.
  • It rested on a handful of articles. Spitalfields Life, a few magazines, company pages. None of the scholarly base was read: Hammond on early pointe, Garafola on the business of ballet, Foster on the trained body, Kraut on ownership, Franko on the court. The board of personas checked the page against its own sources, so it could only find faults inside a narrow frame.
  • It opened with a quiz, which you had ruled out.
  • It ended on a paragraph written for the project (why there is no photograph, how the names were sourced), which tells a learner nothing.

What changed in the skills: hom-writing §1 now requires the subject to be scoped from every angle before any page is written; §2 bans an opening question or quiz and puts context before the thesis; §6 and the constitution's rule 9 move "what the sources leave out" into the argument where it matters, and the list itself to the editor view.

2. Why ballet belongs in this course

The course teaches making as a web of objects, makers, places, routes and institutions, with strands the record hides. Ballet tests that idea harder than most subjects, for four reasons:

  1. The made thing includes a person. A dancer's body is produced over seven to nine years in a state school, to a written specification, at a cost (injury, exclusion). No other case in F1 makes the body the product so plainly.
  2. The work leaves no object. A ballet survives as paper (notation, designs, contracts, programmes), as things (shoes, costumes, cloths), and in the bodies of the people who danced it. So the record decides what the work is.
  3. The credit system names one person. The programme says "choreography by", "designs by". Behind that name are scene painters, cutters, shoemakers, notators, rehearsal pianists and the dancers the steps were made on. Attribution and Authority, the course's strongest thread, runs through every part of ballet.
  4. It travelled by institutions, not by style. Ballet went out by court, academy, syllabus, Soviet teacher, émigré, touring star and opera house. Each route is a different institution. That is the web, and the usual line (Paris, St Petersburg, Paris, New York) is the obscured version of it.

How the threads land:

Thread Strength Where
Attribution and Authority Very strong Beauchamp v. Feuillet (1704); designer and scene painter; maker's marks; method named after one teacher, written by others; Graham "work for hire"; the répétiteur
Writing and Argument Strong At least eight ready-made two-reading disputes (§4)
Accessibility Strong Who could enrol, who could buy a shoe in their colour, who could see the record; many open-access sources
Planetary Weight Medium Polyester satin, nylon net, rosin, coal gas, touring freight, a tool designed to be used up; little is counted

3. What there is to know: seven strands

Each strand lists what a learner must know (tier 1). Tiers 2 and 3, sources and evidence are in the scope files named.

A. The court and the paper, 1581 to 1704 (scope 01, 02, 04)

  • Ballet began as a court spectacle made by a team (poet, composer, dance master, designer, machinist) to make power visible: the Ballet comique de la reine (Paris, 15 October 1581) and Louis XIV dancing in court ballets until 1669.
  • The stage was a machine: Torelli's scene changes (Paris from about 1645), perspective scenery, engravings as the record.
  • The Académie Royale de Danse (1661): thirteen dancing masters, licensed teaching, a standard.
  • Codification as a design system: five positions, turnout, a French vocabulary still used from Havana to Tokyo; Feuillet's Chorégraphie (1700) made dance printable.
  • The 1704 ruling: the king's council named Beauchamp as originator of the notation and let Feuillet keep publishing. The earliest known dispute over who invented a dance technology.

B. The made body (scope 02, 01)

  • The school as a machine: St Petersburg 1738 (twelve girls, twelve boys, by decree); Moscow 1773 (foundlings, the teacher probably paid per finished dancer); Vaganova's syllabus printed in 1934; Cairo 1958.
  • Technique makes the body: years of daily class reshape a child; Foster's "two bodies", the one you see and the ideal one.
  • Syllabi as exportable technology: Cecchetti (1922), RAD (1920), Vaganova, Bournonville; each method carries one name and was written down by others.
  • Pointe came before the pointe shoe: Hammond argues the earliest practitioners were not 19th-century ballerinas and not only women; Taglioni's surviving shoe (V&A, about 1842) has no block, only darning.
  • The specification and its costs: a narrow body type; eating-disorder risk about three times higher in dancers (Arcelus 2014, studies 1966 to 2013); injury rates (Royal Ballet, Mattiussi 2021); the Paris Opéra's 2021 report on whitening skin.

C. The stage as machine and picture (scope 04)

  • The Romantic white act was a lighting and scenery effect as much as a dance: gas at the Opéra from Aladin (1822); Ciceri's workshop signed every Opéra set from 1822 to 1831.
  • Before Diaghilev, design was a state department: the 1890 Sleeping Beauty sets were split by act among workshop painters.
  • The Ballets Russes (1909 to 1929) hired easel painters (Bakst, Benois, Goncharova, Picasso, Matisse), and scene painters made the cloths: Sapunov painted Golovin's Firebird (1910) his own way; Shervashidze painted Picasso's Train Bleu cloth (1924); Polunin, chief scene painter from 1918, wrote his own book (1927).
  • Stage to street: Bakst's ensembles for Paquin (1912), Poiret's Orientalist vogue, Chanel's Train Bleu (1924); who influenced whom is contested.
  • Light and absence as design: Jean Rosenthal and lighting as a credited profession; Balanchine's practice-clothes ballets from 1951.

D. Things made for dancing (scope 03, earlier pass 2)

  • The pointe shoe: paper, hessian and paste in a satin upper (the satin now mostly polyester, Gonzalez 2015), made inside out and turned; makers known by a stamped mark and paid by the pair; finished by the dancer's own needle.
  • The tutu: Romantic bell to classical plate to Karinska's hoopless "powder puff"; 30 to 120 hours of hand work; makers almost never named.
  • The designer named, the maker not: museum records put the designer in the "maker" field; the Paris Opéra's 180 artisans in eleven trades are photographed but unnamed; some "Bakst" drawings are workshop copies (Met).
  • Ballets Russes costumes survive as altered composites; the Australian National Gallery rebuilt about 100 costumes from 400 fragments.
  • Skin tone: pink as the default "flesh"; dancers pancaking shoes for decades; brown and bronze shoes from Freed with Ballet Black (2018), after Gaynor Minden's shades (2017).
  • Materials and their weight: nylon net from an 1808 Nottingham machine patent; rosin from pine resin; a tool designed to be used up in hours.

E. Who pays, who owns, what survives (scope 05)

  • Three funding models, and the model shapes the art: court and state; market and subscribers (the Paris Opéra as a private concession from 1831); today's mix. Garafola argues that when theatres went to market, pensions were cut, stars were paid for drawing power, and women in travesty replaced male dancers.
  • The cost disease (Baumol and Bowen 1966): Swan Lake needs as many dancers now as in 1895, so costs outrun ticket income and someone else must pay.
  • Dancers as a workforce: ranks as pay grades, contracts of 33 to 37 weeks in the US, unions, careers ending in the thirties.
  • Who owns a ballet: US choreography copyrightable only from the 1976 Act; Hanya Holm's 1952 registration in Labanotation; the Graham ruling that dances made on salary belong to the institution; the Balanchine Trust licensing about 75 works; Ashton leaving ballets to friends.
  • The record: Stepanov notation paid for by the Imperial Directorate from 1893; Sergeyev carried it out of Russia; Harvard bought it in 1969; reconstructions from it are new works.

F. Routes out (scope 01)

  • Five export models, each an institution: Soviet teachers under cultural agreements (Beijing 1954, Cairo 1958, Havana); a British syllabus examined by touring examiners; Russian émigrés teaching where they landed (Harbin, Shanghai, Kamakura, then Tokyo's Swan Lake of August 1946); the touring star (Pavlova in a Mexico City bullring, 1919); the presenting opera house buying tours (Muscat 2011, Doha, Manama, Dubai, Kuwait).
  • The same Soviet model produced different results in Beijing, Havana and Cairo.
  • The Gulf: opera houses without resident ballet companies; Saudi Arabia's Theater and Performing Arts Commission licensing dance; private studios since about 2017. Almost no scholarship.

G. Who was kept out, and how that changed (scope 01, 02, 03)

  • Ballet blanc as a colour that became a rule about skin; Gottschild on the Africanist presence "invisibilized" in American ballet.
  • Dance Theatre of Harlem (1969); skin-tone tights; the brown shoe; the Paris Opéra report (2021), recommendation 7: stop whitening skin.
  • Orientalism in costume and make-up (Schéhérazade, La Bayadère, Nutcracker), and the institutional responses (Final Bow for Yellowface, 2017).
  • Disability: almost no scholarship on classical companies (one thesis found).

4. The concepts and the debates

These are the ideas the unit teaches; facts serve them.

Concept The debate, and who argues it
Codification as a design system Franko: the court body as a text of absolute power. Hammond and Hammond (1979): technique develops by its own logic. Foster: Feuillet's lexicon as cultural dominance.
Technique makes the body Mauss (1934): techniques of the body are learned. Foster (1992): training makes two bodies. Wainwright and Turner (2006): the body as capital that injury and age spend.
Pointe before the shoe Hammond (1987–88): gradual, not only female; the "revolution" is hindsight. Lawry (2022): the shoe as an invented tradition that links pain to value.
Authorship of a ballet The 1704 ruling split it. Kraut (2015): copyright as a tool of race and gender power. The Graham courts: the institution as author.
The designer and the executant Polunin's own account; the NGA calling the scene painter a "set designer"; Aronson's "Remember the designers!"
The ephemeral and the record Homans: ballet has "no texts". Nicifero: Ebreo, Feuillet, Stepanov, Laban. Reconstruction as a new making.
The total work of art Bellow: the Ballets Russes stage as Wagner's total artwork. Järvinen: dance becomes secondary to music and design.
Patronage shapes form Garafola (1985, 1989); Wilbur on NEA grant rules; Klapper on the Ford grants of 1963.
The cost disease Baumol and Bowen (1966); Throsby (2001) on cultural value.
Ballet as ethnic dance Kealiinohomoku (1969/70): ballet is one ethnic form among many. Järvinen: not a unified form; its place in education is a colonial legacy.
Orientalism and self-exoticism Said; Wollen; Sharp; Fisher's "intercultural sharing" against her critics.
The canon map as an artefact Homans's two epicentres against the Harbin, Shanghai and Cairo routes. This is the course's threshold concept in one case.
Decline and revival Homans's "ballet is dying" against Rogoff and Horwell.

Ready-made two-reading disputes: who was earliest on pointe; Beauchamp or Feuillet; Sleeping Beauty as political allegory (Scholl against Mahiet); Ezrahi's "slaves" (Tomoff); Bakst or Poiret first; design primacy or dance primacy in the Ballets Russes; the authenticity of reconstructions; Fisher on Nutcracker's "Arabian Coffee".

5. Size

Each of the six angles came back at four to eight learner hours on its own; together they are a module of 30 to 40 hours, not a unit. For F1, ballet gets one unit of about twelve hours: six two-hour sessions with about an hour of free reading each. The rest stays in the scope files as the module extension.

6. The unit: six sessions

Each session has one idea, its cases, one free reading, and a making task. No quizzes, no points. Each session would become two or three learner pages.

Session 1. The court and the paper

  • Idea: ballet began as a design project of a court, and the court turned dance into a standard that could be printed, taught and owned.
  • You will know: the court ballet as a team-made spectacle; the 1661 academy; the five positions and the French vocabulary as a standard set of parts; Feuillet's notation; the 1704 ruling.
  • Cases and objects: Feuillet, Chorégraphie (1700; Library of Congress, open); the 1582 printed livret of the Ballet comique (Gallica, non-commercial); Torelli's stage engravings.
  • Reading: Library of Congress, "Baroque dance" (open).
  • Making: write a notation for a 16-count phrase; swap with a partner and perform theirs; compare with a Feuillet track drawing. Then answer: who invented the step, the dancer or the person who wrote it down?

Session 2. The made body

  • Idea: a ballet dancer is a made thing, produced by a school to a specification, at a cost.
  • You will know: the school as an institution (1738, 1773, 1934, 1958); technique as a specification; syllabi as exports; pointe before the shoe; the ideal body, who it leaves out, what it costs.
  • Cases and objects: a Feuillet page of the five positions (open); Taglioni's unblocked shoe (V&A, link only); a Degas barre picture (Met, open), read as the painter's view and not the record.
  • Reading: an extract of Foster, "Dancing Bodies"; Kealiinohomoku, "An Anthropologist Looks at Ballet as a Form of Ethnic Dance".
  • Making: write the ballet body as a product brief (inputs, process, tolerances, rejects); then annotate who the brief excludes and what it uses up.

Session 3. The stage: machine, picture and painters

  • Idea: a ballet's look is credited to one designer but made by a chain of trades, and the record keeps the drawing and loses the hands.
  • You will know: the baroque machine stage; gas and the white act; the Imperial scenic studios; the Ballets Russes painters and their scene painters; stage to street (Paquin, Poiret, Chanel); light and the bare stage as design.
  • Cases and objects: a Ciceri sketch (Gallica); a Bakst costume design (Met, open) beside a BnF record naming the costumier Marie Muelle; Picasso's Parade curtain (link only), with the Train Bleu cloth credit "designed by Picasso, painted by Shervashidze".
  • Reading: Met, "Performance as Escape" on Bakst's workshop copies (free); one chapter of Garafola, Diaghilev's Ballets Russes (library or archive.org loan).
  • Making: take a small design drawing and plan its scale-up to a 16-metre cloth; write the credit line naming every hand the work would need.

Session 4. Things made for dancing

  • Idea: ballet's objects are tools used up by bodies, finished by their users, and made by people the record names by a symbol or not at all.
  • You will know: the pointe shoe (materials, construction, mark, piecework, the dancer's labour, skin tone); the tutu from bell to plate; company workrooms as factories; costumes as composites; materials and their weight.
  • Cases and objects: a used pointe shoe and a piece of tutu net in the room; the V&A record of Cira Robinson's brown shoes; the Australian Ballet's "How to make a tutu" (open); Spitalfields Life's Freed visits (open).
  • Reading: Collins and Jarvis (2016) on costume and choreography (open manuscript).
  • Making: an object biography label: every hand and material you can name, and "not recorded" where you cannot. (The existing shoe page is rewritten as part of this session.)

Session 5. Who pays, who owns, what survives

  • Idea: the way ballet is paid for shapes what is danced and by whom, and a ballet has to become a document before anyone can own it.
  • You will know: three funding models; Garafola's argument on the market and the travesty dancer; the cost disease; dancers as a workforce; copyright, estates and licences; notation and reconstruction.
  • Cases and objects: Petipa's salaried contract (1862); Sergeyev's notations (Harvard); a Balanchine Trust licence page; an AGMA corps minimum; the Royal Ballet and Opera accounts.
  • Reading: Garafola, "The Travesty Dancer in Nineteenth-Century Ballet" (1985, six pages, open).
  • Making: a credits page for one ballet that names every role its programme leaves out, with who paid for each.

Session 6. Routes out

  • Idea: ballet spread by institutions (state teachers, syllabi, émigrés, stars, opera houses), and the usual line from Paris to New York hides most of the routes.
  • You will know: the five export models; Beijing, Havana and Cairo from one Soviet model; Harbin to Tokyo; Pavlova in Mexico; the Gulf opera houses; who was kept out and how that changed (Dance Theatre of Harlem, the brown shoe, the Paris report).
  • Cases and objects: a Cairo programme; a 1946 Tokyo programme; an RAD syllabus; a Royal Opera House Muscat season.
  • Reading: Wilcox, Revolutionary Bodies (open, Luminos), one chapter; Tomé, "Swans in Sugarcane Fields" (open).
  • Making (the unit's assessed piece): map one ballet object's route as a web: every place, institution and hand it passed through, with the strands the record hides marked as such.

7. How the pages will be written

  • Each page opens with context (period, place, institution) and the idea, then the evidence. No opening question.
  • Each page says where it sits: the session's idea, what came before, what comes next.
  • Facts serve the concept. A detail that does not carry the session's idea stays in the scope files.
  • Gaps in the record are said where the reader meets them, inside the argument. The editor view holds the full list and the sourcing notes.
  • Sources are the scholarly base first, primary documents second, press third. Every claim keeps its confidence word in the sentence where it is probable or contested.

8. Still to research or verify before writing

  • Read at source: Hammond (1987–88), Foster's "Phallic Pointe", Daly (1987), Aalten (2005), the Mauss quotation, Wiley (1976), Chazin-Bennahum: each was summarised partly from memory and is marked probable.
  • Contested dates: the Paris Opéra pension (1698 or 1713); Ciceri's appointment (1815 or 1818); Brugnoli (1822 or 1823); about ten institutional founding dates (scope 01 §6).
  • Current: the NYCB–AGMA agreement after November 2025.
  • Readers needed: a Russian reader for the Moscow, Cairo and Soviet sources; Arabic, Chinese and Japanese sources were not searched in depth; a dancer and a shoemaker or costume maker to read sessions 2 and 4.
  • Thin everywhere: the Gulf (no ballet scholarship found); disability in classical companies; wages of shoemakers and costume makers; production outside Europe and the US (Chacott, Grishko's Czech and North Macedonian plants).
  • Images: the reliably open holders are the Met, the NGA, NYPL (public-domain items), the Library of Congress and Harvard; Gallica and the V&A are non-commercial; the Royal Ballet and Opera license through ArenaPAL. A unit built only from free images will look like Degas and Bakst. The image list per session is the next job.
