1. The short answers

Is the six-session plan the best way to organise it? No. It split ballet by angle (court, body, stage, things, money, routes), so every session rebuilt its context from scratch, the works themselves were scattered, and the concept, which you rightly call the main product, had no home. No study shows that ordering by theme or by date is better in itself; the evidence that does exist favours worked examples, stories, and a framework of dates the learner can hang things on (section 3).

Should the sessions be mixed or joined? Joined. The angles stop being sessions and become the chapters of each work's making. Giselle is a concept (Gautier's), a team (seven makers, one uncredited), a body technique (pointe without a block), a set of things (Lormier's costumes, Ciceri's reused set), a business (Pillet needed a vehicle for a star), and a record (Stepanov notation, Justamant). Taking one work apart touches every angle at once, in the order a maker meets them.

Is the concept itself a product? Yes, and this is now the spine. Every ballet is a premise someone had, rooted in named sources, specified in documents (a letter, a livret, a composer's plan, designs), built by a team into a world with rules and refusals, delivered as an experience on one night, then handed on and remade. The module treats that chain as the thing being made, and the bodies, objects and stages as how it was made.

What changes when all the research goes in? Three things. The unit of the module becomes the work, not the angle. The angle research moves to the period where each idea first decided something (the blocked shoe to Imperial Russia, copyright to the 1950s), so it is met in context instead of in a block. And the routes out of Europe stop being a final session: each act from Act III on carries at least one case made outside the Paris, St Petersburg and New York line.

2. Why ballet is the right case for a course on building worlds

The course teaches learners to turn ideas, objects and influences into a finished concept and an experience. Ballet has done that, on record, for 445 years:

  • The idea rarely comes from the choreographer. Across 33 works the originator was a queen's commission, a first minister, a tenor, a critic, an archivist, a theatre director, a composer, a painter, a poet, a patron, a state contest or a dancer remaking her own role. Ideas come from the edges of an institution, and the institution assigns a team.
  • Every concept has nameable roots. Heine's Wilis and a Hugo poem behind Giselle; Perrault and the Louis XIV revival behind The Sleeping Beauty; Afanasyev's tales and Golovin's painting behind The Firebird; Roerich's archaeology and Princess Tenisheva's workshops at Talashkino behind The Rite of Spring; a 1961 film behind The Red Detachment of Women; the Rana Plaza collapse behind Khan's Giselle.
  • The concept leaves documents, in a sequence a designer recognises. A letter of proposal (Vsevolozhsky to Tchaikovsky, 1888); a scenario or livret; a composer's specification down to the bar (Petipa's plan for The Sleeping Beauty); design drawings and maquettes; a rehearsal score; a production book; notation as the handover (the Sergeyev collection, digitised by Harvard in 2024); a revival package (Cunningham's Dance Capsules).
  • Worlds are built from rules and refusals. Nearly every work in the sample runs on two worlds and two movement languages (village and forest; court and lake; classical for magic, folk for humans). Most reform theories are defined by what they refuse: Noverre against masks, Fokine against ready-made steps, Balanchine's "eliminate".
  • Remaking is part of the life of a concept. The research found five modes of remaking, each a question a designer can ask of any concept: re-setting (where else does this hierarchy exist?), re-psychologising (what if the supernatural is inside the hero?), re-politicising (whose story should this be?), re-motivating (why does each character act?), re-excavating (what was there before habit?).
  • The credit line is a design decision. "Choreography by Coralli" drops Perrot; "after Petipa" drops Ivanov; "China Ballet Troupe" drops everyone; "Matthew Bourne's Swan Lake" puts the remaker in the title. The course's Attribution and Authority thread runs through every case.

These map onto the Concept DNA the learner builds across the program (section 6).

3. How the structure was chosen

Seven structures were assessed against these learners (adult, self-paced, phone-first, twenty-minute sittings, Maker and Briefer lanes, one Concept DNA across the program). Full study: works/S_structure_study.md.

Chronological survey Strong Moderate Weak Reads as the line F1 argues against Too passive
Thematic by angle (the old plan) Weak Weak Fragmented Context rebuilt every session Replaced

Works in acts (hybrid) Strong Strong Strong Needs a light template and a rule for which angles lead Chosen

What the evidence supports, and how firmly:

  • Worked examples beat problem-solving for beginners, including learning to recognise designers' styles (Rourke and Sweller 2009; documented). So take works apart fully before asking learners to build their own.
  • Stories are understood and remembered better than essays (Mar and others 2021, effect about 0.55; documented). A work's life is a ready-made story.
  • Mixing periods helps learners tell visual styles apart (paintings showed the largest effect) but not arguments (Brunmair and Richter 2019; documented). So show the looks of different periods side by side, and keep each sitting's argument in one block.
  • Guidance should fade as knowledge grows (probable). Early acts are fully worked; later acts hand more to the learner.
  • Learners need a framework of dates to hang depth on (probable, from history-teaching research). So a short overview first, and anchor dates that come back.
  • Longer online courses lose learners, and the first two weeks decide engagement (Jordan 2015; documented). So the module is built in acts that can each be finished on their own, and it opens on a work and a story, not a court treatise.
  • A twenty-minute sitting holds about 1,000 to 1,500 words, four to six images and one task (argued from reading-speed data). One sitting carries one claim and one work-moment or object.

4. The module: a prologue, six acts and an epilogue

About 44 hours, or 130 sittings of twenty minutes. Each act is a period named by an idea, takes two to five works apart as full cases, gives shorter treatment to satellite works, places the angle research where it first mattered ("systems"), returns to Giselle once, and ends with one product the learner keeps. Each act can be taken on its own after the prologue.

The braid: Giselle. It returns in every act from Act II: Paris 1841; Petipa's revisions and the Stepanov notation in St Petersburg; Sergeyev's 1924 Paris staging from that notation; Alicia Alonso's Havana version; then Creole Giselle (1984), Ek (1982), Khan (2016) and Masilo (2017). By the end, the learner holds a 180-year version history of one concept. Swan Lake was the alternative; Giselle won because its originator wrote down how the idea came, and because it has the widest range of remakes.

The lens rule. Each case foregrounds two or three angles, chosen for where that work is strongest, so that across the module each angle leads at least twice and none is repeated case after case.

Prologue. What a ballet is, as a made thing (2 hours, 6 sittings)

  • Giselle in one sitting: the idea in a critic's letter, the team, the premiere, the versions.
  • How to take a work apart: the case card (section 5).
  • The anchor dates (1581, 1661, 1841, 1890, 1913, 1964, 1995) on a map of routes, not a line.
  • Ballet's record is paper, and the most common open image of ballet is a painter's (the Degas problem).
  • Ballet is one dance among many (Kealiinohomoku; Järvinen).
  • The working vocabulary: positions, turnout, pointe, corps, livret, divertissement, répétiteur.

Act I. The court designs a world, 1581 to 1763 (5 hours, 15 sittings)

  • Idea: ballet began as a court's concept for showing power, and the court turned dance into a standard that could be written, taught and owned.
  • Full cases: Balet comique de la Royne (1581): a concept that survives only as a printed book, a team of named specialists, and a plot later claimed by a poet. Ballet royal de la Nuit (1653): Mazarin's brief, the young king as the rising sun. Noverre's Lettres sur la danse (1760) and Médée et Jason (1763): the earliest known written grammar published before the work, and its refusals (masks, symmetry, irrelevant virtuosity).
  • Systems: the stage as a machine (Torelli); the Académie Royale de Danse (1661); five positions and turnout as a standard set of parts; Feuillet's notation (1700) and the 1704 ruling between Beauchamp and Feuillet; the Opéra school; eighteenth-century costume and the step.
  • The concept lesson: premise as a theme; Ménestrier's "unity of design" as a perimeter; a grammar written down first.
  • Product. Maker: a one-page "dessein" for your own world (one subject, its parts, what does not belong), and a 16-count notation another learner can perform. Briefer: a commission for a court occasion (purpose, message, who performs), and a judgement of two responses.

Act II. The theatre sells a dream: Paris, 1820 to 1870 (6 hours, 18 sittings)

  • Idea: the Romantic ballet was a business product as much as an art: a director needed a star vehicle, a writer supplied a story, a workshop built a world of light.
  • Full cases: La Sylphide (1832): a tenor's idea from Nodier's tale, with the sprite changed from male to female. Giselle (1841), braid: Gautier's account of the idea, the rejected first draft, Saint-Georges's plot, Perrot uncredited, Adam's motifs, Ciceri's reused set. Coppélia (1870): Hoffmann's dark tale turned comic, the hero danced by a woman.
  • Satellites: the nuns of Robert le diable (1831) as the model white act; Bournonville's Danish Sylphide (1836), the version that survived.
  • Systems: the Opéra as a private concession from 1831; gas lighting from 1822 and the white act as a lighting effect; the Romantic tutu; pointe before the pointe shoe (Hammond; Taglioni's shoe has no block); the market and the travesty dancer (Garafola: pensions cut, women in men's roles); the 1805 rule that designers deposit their drawings; the risk of gas (Emma Livry, 1862); Blasis's manual; the dancers Degas painted, as workers in an institution.
  • The concept lesson: premise as a story; the livret as the concept document; the two-world grammar; a star as an obligation.
  • Product. Maker: a livret for your world: two worlds, a crossing, the scene sequence, three rules. Briefer: a director's brief for a star vehicle, and a judgement of three livrets against it.

Act III. The empire specifies: St Petersburg and Moscow, 1847 to 1903 (6 hours, 18 sittings)

  • Idea: in the Imperial theatres a concept was specified like a commission: a director proposed it, a ballet master wrote the plan, a composer delivered to the bar.
  • Full cases: The Sleeping Beauty (1890): Vsevolozhsky's letter, Petipa's numbered plan, Perrault cut in half, the Louis XIV revival, the workshop painters splitting the acts. La Bayadère (1877): authorship contested between Khudekov and Petipa; Orientalism and its sources. Swan Lake (1877, 1895): a failure in Moscow, remade after the composer's death with a new libretto by his brother.
  • Satellites: The Nutcracker (1892): Hoffmann via Dumas, and the racial types in its character dances that remakes now change. Giselle in St Petersburg (braid): Petipa's revisions, Justamant's Paris notation, Stepanov's.
  • Systems: the school as a machine (St Petersburg 1738; Moscow's foundlings, 1773); the Imperial Theatres as patron and bureaucracy; pay (a visiting Italian ballerina earned about fourteen times her Russian colleague); the scenic studios; the classical tutu; the blocked shoe and the Italian school; Stepanov's notation (1892) and the scores Sergeyev would carry west; "character" dance and classical dance as two registers.
  • The concept lesson: obligations as a specification; the commission; the divertissement as a collection of numbers held by one world.
  • Product. Maker: a Petipa-style plan for one act of your world: each number's action, character, length and tempo. Briefer: a letter of proposal (Vsevolozhsky's) and a list of fixed constraints.

Act IV. The committee and the avant-garde, 1909 to 1960 (8 hours, 24 sittings)

  • Idea: the Ballets Russes made ballet a collaboration between painters, composers and choreographers, run by a producer who commissioned people rather than stories; the state and the neoclassical stage answered with their own models.
  • Full cases: The Firebird (1910): a committee in Benois's flat, Afanasyev's tales, classical dance for magic and folk dance for humans. The Rite of Spring (1913): Stravinsky's vision against Roerich's world, Talashkino, the riot, and the 1987 reconstruction copyrighted as a new work. Le Train Bleu (1924): Cocteau's idea, Chanel's real sportswear, Picasso's curtain painted by Shervashidze. The Flames of Paris (1932): a Soviet concept made by a team for an anniversary. Agon (1957): rules taken from a 1623 dance manual, practice clothes, timings agreed in advance.
  • Satellites: Schéhérazade (colour as story; the Poiret vogue; credit disputed), Petrushka, L'Après-midi d'un faune (the Louvre's vases), Parade, Les Noces, Apollon musagète, The Red Poppy, Schlemmer's Triadic Ballet at the Bauhaus (ballet as a design curriculum). Giselle (braid): Sergeyev's 1924 staging from notation.
  • Systems: Diaghilev's method (the committee, the veto, the season as a collection); the Ballets Russes as a business; designer and scene painter (Sapunov, Shervashidze, Polunin; the costumier Marie Muelle); Karinska and her workshop; costumes that survive as altered composites; stage to street (Bakst for Paquin, Poiret, Chanel, Art Deco); Fokine's five principles (1914); Soviet drambalet; the émigré diaspora (Harbin, Shanghai, Rio, Australia); syllabi as exports (RAD 1920, Cecchetti 1922, Vaganova 1934); lighting as a credited profession (Rosenthal); the bare stage.
  • The concept lesson: the team and the curator; refusals that generate rules; a grammar invented for each work against a shared one; the anchor expression; obligations imposed by a state.
  • Product. Maker: assemble a committee for your world: the brief, the team, a credit line naming every hand, and one translation of the world off the stage (a garment, an interior, a poster). Briefer: a Diaghilev brief ("astonish me", with its constraints), and a judgement of proposals.

Act V. States and routes, 1945 to 1990 (6 hours, 18 sittings)

  • Idea: after 1945 ballet travelled by institutions, and states used it to build nations; the same Soviet model produced different worlds in Beijing, Havana, Tokyo and Cairo.
  • Full cases: The Red Detachment of Women (Beijing, 1964): a film turned into a ballet by a committee under the state. Alicia Alonso's Giselle (Havana, 1945 to 1963; braid): a performer's role, first danced in 1943, hardened into a national concept, given back to Paris in 1972. Matsuyama Ballet's The White-Haired Girl (Tokyo, 1955): the earliest known ballet of that Chinese story, which China then borrowed back. El-Leila El-Kebira (Cairo Opera Ballet, 2001 or 2002): a 1961 puppet operetta made into a ballet.
  • Satellites: Spartacus (Bolshoi, 1968).
  • Systems: five export models (state teachers, examined syllabi, émigrés, the touring star, the presenting opera house); Cold War tours and competitions; public patronage and philanthropy (the Arts Council, the Ford grants of 1963, the NEA); South Africa; the Gulf opera houses (Muscat from 2011) and Saudi Arabia's licensing of dance; Dance Theatre of Harlem (1969).
  • The concept lesson: who may change a work, and who sets the brief; a source in another medium (film, puppet theatre).
  • Product. Maker: a route map for one ballet: every place, institution and hand it passed through, with the hidden strands marked. Briefer: a cultural-diplomacy brief for a touring production, and what it must not say.

Act VI. Remaking, and the industry now, 1980 to today (8 hours, 24 sittings)

  • Idea: remaking is how a concept stays alive; every remake keeps a fixed layer and frees another, and the line between them is the concept.
  • Full cases: Creole Giselle (1984; braid): re-setting in 1840s Louisiana, keeping the steps. Bourne's Swan Lake (1995): re-psychologising, male swans in feathered breeches. Khan's Giselle (2016; braid): migrant garment workers and a wall. Masilo's Swan Lake (2010) and Giselle (2017; braid): re-politicising. Ratmansky's The Sleeping Beauty (2015): re-excavating from notation.
  • Satellites: Ek's Giselle (1982) and Swan Lake (1987).
  • Systems: the pointe shoe today (makers' marks, piecework, two businesses on Britain's endangered-crafts list, Gaynor Minden's moulded shank, polyester satin, the dancer's own hours on the shoe); skin tone (the brown shoe of 2018, after dancers had coloured their own for decades); the Paris Opéra's 2021 report and Final Bow for Yellowface; the body ideal, injury and its costs; dancers as a workforce (contracts, unions, the Paris strikes and pensions); the cost disease and the Nutcracker economy; who owns a ballet (copyright from 1978, the Graham ruling, the Balanchine Trust, Ashton's bequests); the record today (film, Benesh, the répétiteur, revival packages); workrooms as factories (the Royal Ballet and Opera at Thurrock; the Paris Opéra's 180 artisans in eleven trades); materials and their weight (nylon net, rosin, waste).
  • The concept lesson: the five modes of remaking; the keep-and-change line; handover, lifecycle, rights and money.
  • Product. Maker: a remake proposal for one work in this module: the mode, a keep-and-change table, the anchor image, the credit chain. Briefer: a commissioning brief that states what must not change and what may, and a judged set of three proposals.

Epilogue. Your world, with ballet as precedent (3 hours, 9 sittings)

  • Choose the premise of your own world and write it three ways: as a theme (court), as a story (livret), as a system (Agon).
  • Fill your own case card, using the six act products.
  • Write the handover: what a stranger would need to remake your world.

5. The case card: the concept as the product

Every full case is taken apart on the same card, in the order a concept is made. In early acts the module fills the card; by Act VI the learner fills most of it from the sources given. Each heading names the Concept DNA field it feeds.

Heading What it records Concept DNA field
Roots The texts, images, objects, music and events the idea came from, and how each was used Sources (SRC)
Premise The idea in one sentence, as its makers framed it and as scholars read it Premise (PRM)
Who had it The originator, the brief, and the documents of development Brief (BRF), Obligations (OBL)
The world Its rules, its grammar of movement and look, and what it refuses Rules (RUL), Grammars (GRM), Anti-DNA (ANT), Perimeter (PER)
How it was made The bodies, things, stage and workshops, through two or three angles Anchor expression (ANC)
Team and credit Every role, and who the credit line names and drops Rights and money (RGT)
The night The premiere: place, audience, what they saw and felt (the experience)
The record What survives, who holds it, on what terms Handover (HND)
Afterlife Versions, remakes and translations into other media Lifecycle (LCY), Adjacent translation (ADJ), Version history (VER)

A filled card, in brief: Giselle (Paris Opéra, 28 June 1841)

  • Roots: Heine's passage on the Wilis in De l'Allemagne; Hugo's poem "Fantômes", about a girl who died after a ball; the white acts of La Sylphide and Robert le diable.
  • Premise: love outlasts betrayal and death (a peasant girl, deceived by a disguised nobleman, dies and protects him among the ghosts of jilted brides).
  • Who had it: Théophile Gautier, poet and critic, by his own account on reading Heine. His first draft, an empty ballroom of Wilis, was rejected as a string of dances. Saint-Georges built the plot. The director, Léon Pillet, needed a vehicle to launch Carlotta Grisi.
  • The world: two worlds (a sunlit vine-harvest village; a moonlit forest grave) and two movement languages; the rule that the Wilis dance any man to death before dawn and that love can only delay it; no happy marriage.
  • How it was made: Adam's recurring motifs; Ciceri's Act I set reused from an earlier ballet; Lormier's costumes; Grisi's dances made by her partner, Jules Perrot.
  • Team and credit: credited to Coralli for choreography; Perrot, who made Grisi's dances, appears on no poster or programme.
  • The night: the Opéra's subscribers; an immediate success.
  • The record: Lormier's designs (BnF, non-commercial); Justamant's notation of the 1860s Paris version; Stepanov's notation from St Petersburg (Harvard).
  • Afterlife: dropped in Paris in 1868; survived in St Petersburg; returned west as "Russian" in 1911; reconstructed by Ratmansky in 2019; remade in Havana, Louisiana, an asylum, a garment factory and South Africa.

6. What the learner holds at the end

  • A case library of about twenty ballets, each on the case card, entered in the Concept DNA's Sources field and tagged to the fields it informs later (premise, rules, anti-DNA, grammar, rights, translation, lifecycle). The Method modules can reuse the library as worked examples.
  • The version history of Giselle, 1841 to 2017: who changed what, why, and who paid.
  • Six act products, which follow the documents ballet itself invented, in the order it invented them: a dessein and a notation (the court), a livret (the Romantic theatre), a composer's plan (the Imperial theatre), a committee, a credit line and a translation (the Ballets Russes), a route map (the state era), a remake proposal (today).
  • In the Briefer lane: six briefs (a court commission, a star vehicle, a letter of proposal, an "astonish me" brief, a touring brief, a commissioning brief with fixed and free layers) and six judged sets.
  • From the epilogue: a premise written three ways and a handover for the learner's own world.

The learner leaves knowing the history, and having practised, in historical order, every document a concept passes through on its way to becoming an experience.

8. Before writing

  • Read at source. About twenty items rest on summaries and are marked probable: Hammond (1987–88), Foster's "Phallic Pointe", Daly, Aalten, the Mauss quotation, Wiley's Tchaikovsky book (Petipa's plan reached us through two secondary sites), Garafola and Taruskin where cited via Wikipedia, the Benois–Bakst dispute over Schéhérazade, the Ballets Russes committee in Benois and Grigoriev, Gautier's letter to Heine in the original.
  • Contested dates are listed in each file with both readings (for example the Paris Opéra pension, 1698 or 1713; Red Detachment's premiere; El-Leila El-Kebira's).
  • Readers needed: Russian (Imperial and Soviet sources, Cairo), Chinese (Red Detachment), Japanese (Matsuyama), Arabic (El-Leila El-Kebira, the Gulf); a dancer, a shoemaker or costume maker, and a scenic artist.
  • Thin everywhere: ballet made in the Gulf (none found; the opera houses commission tours, opera and dance theatre); disability in classical companies; makers' wages; production outside Europe and the US.
  • Images: no open images were found for any post-1945 work; the reliably open holders are the Met, the National Gallery of Art, NYPL, the Library of Congress and Harvard; Gallica and the V&A are non-commercial; designs by Goncharova, Benois and Picasso stay in copyright until 2031 to 2044. An image list per act is the next research job.

9. Decisions for you

  1. Where the module lives. A ballet module inside F1 at Practice and Mastery depth, with the twelve-hour plan as its Orientation path; or a case module shared by F1 and the Method modules (M1 premise, M2 Concept DNA, M3 world architecture), which would reuse its case cards as worked examples. F1's own Concept DNA role is Sources only, so the epilogue's premise work would count toward M1.
  2. Six acts of 5 to 8 hours, or four longer ones (merging II with III and V with VI). Shorter acts finish better; six follow the history more closely.
  3. The braid: Giselle (chosen) or Swan Lake.
  4. Which act to write first. Proposed: the Prologue and Act II, because Giselle opens both and Act II has the best-documented origin story.
  5. The Gulf strand: a research pass in Arabic sources and Gulf press, and whether a Gulf-made case exists or must be commissioned as a design brief instead.
