# Ballet as a source of products and collections

Research note for F1 (Histories of Making), ballet case. Purpose: teach how a creative world becomes a family of products, using the program's Concept DNA vocabulary (anchor, family, roles, drops, carriers, distribution, grammars, obligations, permitted variation, adjacent translation) and the threshold concept "the collection precedes the object".

Status: complete first pass, 21 Oct 2026. Layout: research log (raw findings with sources) first, then SYNTHESIS (A teaching claims; B collection-DNA readings; C collection logics inside ballet; D gaps and traps; E proposed object and product cards).

Confidence marks: **[documented]** (primary source, museum/archive record, or multiple reliable secondary sources agree); **[probable]** (reliable secondary source, not independently confirmed); **[contested]** (sources disagree, or a popular story with weak support); **[argued]** (our interpretation for teaching, not a historical claim). Company figures and claims are labelled "(company's claim)". "Earliest known", never "first".

Not repeated here: the material history of pointe shoe, tutu and costume (see scope/03_objects_makers_materials.md); stage-to-street and designers' stage work (scope/04_stage_design.md); the 33 works as made concepts (works/). This note reads the same ground as products and collections.

### Pointe shoe and dance-shoe firms
- [documented, Wikipedia "Freed of London", seen 21 Oct 2026] Freed founded 1929 by cobbler Frederick Freed (b. East End 1899, son of a sample shoemaker), his wife (a milliner) and an assistant, both ex-Gamba; basement/St Martin's Lane workshop, Endell Street factory (1934), Mercer Street (1947); founders retired 1968, firm acquired by D H Sam Thomson and Magdalan Shoes of Norwich; dance apparel only from 1985; wholly owned by Onward Kashiyama (now Onward Holdings) from 1993; 2012 bridal line and New York store with Chacott (Onward's Japanese dancewear firm); **2013 ballet flats, "the first instance" of Freed making shoes not meant for dance**; ISO accreditation 2016; Ballet Brown and Ballet Bronze Oct 2018. Maker's letter or symbol stamped on sole; 26 makers, c. 50 shoes a day (Wikipedia, citing Business Insider 2021: "only 24 artisans"). Headquarters listed as Leicester in infobox, London in text [contested: check]. Wikipedia says Pavlova and Fonteyn were "among the first customers" [probable; Fonteyn was 10 in 1929, so "early" not "first"]. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freed_of_London
- [documented as the company's claim] Capezio "Established 1887" timeline: Salvatore Capezio (b. Muro Lucano 1871); "1892: Official Pointe Shoe Maker ... Anna Pavlova" and "a unique pointe shoe named 'The Pavlova'" (company dates the Pavlova shoe both 1892 and after 1910: internal inconsistency; Pavlova was 11 in 1892) [contested]; 1910 Pavlova bought Capezio pointe shoes for herself and her company on her first US tour [probable: company and Wikipedia agree]; **1941: "Designer Claire McCardell featured the Capezio long sole ballet shoe with her fashion collection"**; Lord & Taylor and Neiman Marcus took it up; "Capezio made the cover of Vogue in 1949" (company's claim). https://www.capezio.com/about-us/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capezio . Tory Burch's brand blog (2022) repeats the 1941 McCardell commission and Vogue cover [company blog; secondary]. https://blog.toryburch.com/2022/09/en-pointe-the-history-of-ballet-flats/
- [documented as company's timeline] Repetto: 1947 Rose Repetto makes ballet shoes for her son Roland Petit near the Opéra; 1952 earliest advertising campaign, "signed by Madame Repetto"; **1956 "Cendrillon" ballet flats "dedicated to Brigitte Bardot"** for *Et Dieu... créa la femme*; 1959 boutique 22 rue de la Paix; **1964 logo stylised by the artist Leonor Fini**; 1967 factory at Saint-Médard-d'Excideuil, home of the *cousu-retourné* (stitch-and-return: sole sewn inside out, then turned); c. 1970 Serge Gainsbourg adopts the "Zizi" Oxford made for Zizi Jeanmaire (Petit's wife, i.e. Rose's daughter-in-law; Repetto's English page wrongly says "stepdaughter" of Gainsbourg) [documented with correction]; 1999 revival under Jean-Marc Gaucher; collaborations Issey Miyake 2000, Yohji Yamamoto 2002, Comme des Garçons, Karl Lagerfeld 2009; 2005 millionth pair of ballet flats (company's claim); 2010 Atelier Repetto customisation in **252 colours**; 2012 training school at Coulaures; 2024 Jacquemus reinterprets the Zizi. Revenue €60 m and 340 staff in 2013 (Wikipedia, company figure). Repetto owned by Esmark/Danskin's parent from Sept 1987 [probable: FundingUniverse]. https://repetto.com/en/pages/univers-repetto-histoire ; https://fashionunited.uk/press/fashion/everything-you-need-to-know-about-repetto/2022030761835 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Repetto ; Journal du Luxe 2017 says Bardot asked for the shoe [probable].
- Bloch founded Sydney 1932 by Jacob Bloch (already in scope 03, pass2).

### Practice clothes into everyday wear
- [documented, Wikipedia "Leotard"] Jules Léotard (1838–1870), acrobat, called his garment a *maillot*; "leotard" earliest recorded 1886. Late 1970s leotards common as exercise and streetwear (disco, aerobics); Danskin "flourished"; Gilda Marx another maker. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leotard
- [probable: FundingUniverse company history, based on press] Danskin founded 1882 NYC by Joel and Benson Goodman, dry-goods store importing tights and hosiery; dancers' trade led to manufacture; added snaps to a leotard to make the bodysuit for general wear (late 1960s per Apparel Search); 1976 swimwear in a shiny nylon-spandex maillot; early 1980s aerobics lines; Sept 1987 parent Esmark bought **Repetto**; a $3 m "all the world's a stage" campaign raised adult streetwear sales by over a third in 1987. https://www.fundinguniverse.com/company-histories/danskin-inc-history/ ; https://www.company-histories.com/Danskin-Inc-Company-History.html
- [company's claim: Danskin Europe / Iconix licensee page] Danskin introduced "the first knit tights and leotards" and popularised "ballet pink" and "theatrical pink" tights; name from "dance" + "skin"; "the first nylon bodywear" for the general population (1950s); "80% share of the USA bodywear market" by end of 1970s. https://www.danskin.eu.com/ourhistory . Treat every "first" and the 80% as company claims [contested]. Brand sold to Iconix 2007 and licensed (Apparel Search) [probable].
- [probable, LA Times 23 May 2006] designer **Bonnie August** at Danskin "brought the leotard into mainstream fashion during the disco era"; unitard or leotard with wrap skirt as "nocturnal uniform"; early use of Lycra spandex. https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2006-may-23-et-leotard23-story.html
- [probable, popular sources] legwarmers: studio warm-up garment; aerobics boom; *Flashdance* (1983) the usual turning point. https://rarehistoricalphotos.com/legwarmers-fashion-1980s ; Click Americana reprints a 1983 article with a 1982 Danskin ad. Low-authority; find a museum or press source before teaching.
- [documented, encyclopedia.com entry on McCardell (Gale, *Women in World History*)] Claire McCardell (1905–1958) made "a wool leotard to be worn under sweaters and skirts" for cold wartime college dormitories (fuel rationing): practice garment re-specified as an everyday layer. Year usually given as 1943 [probable, not seen at source]. McCardell's work held at FIT, the Met (e.g. 2009.300.6973, cocktail dress 1943, image not downloadable) and LACMA. https://www.encyclopedia.com/women/dictionaries-thesauruses-pictures-and-press-releases/mccardell-claire-1905-1958

### The *Nutcracker* economy
- [documented as survey figures] Dance/USA, "Nutcracker Again?" (Dec 2018), from its Annual Financial Survey 2017: Nutcracker/holiday revenue = average 48% (median 55%) of surveyed companies' season revenues (i.e. performance income), and 15% of total revenues; ticket sales $51 m (2017) vs $30 m (2008) across 16–24 member companies; on average two more performances a season; marketing 10–12% of ticket sales. In 2008 it was 22–25% of season revenue. https://www.danceusa.org/ejournal/2018/12/03/nutcracker-again . NPR (22 Dec 2025): NYCB's executive director Kathy Brown says *Nutcracker* is "about 45% of our total ticket revenue" from c. 50 performances; Dance/USA large companies' *Nutcracker* ticket revenue rose from just over $57 m (2022) to well over $84 m (2024). https://www.npr.org/2025/12/22/nx-s1-5628350/the-nutcracker-helps-keep-the-lights-on-for-american-dance-companies
- [documented] **The nutcracker figure precedes the ballet.** Leavenworth Nutcracker Museum: standing soldier and king nutcrackers in Sonneberg and the Erzgebirge by 1800; "Nussknacker" in the Grimms' dictionary (1830 per museum); Wilhelm Füchtner of Seiffen made a commercial lathe-turned series in 1872 (museum: "first commercial production") ("father of the nutcracker"); the museum says collector demand "is renewed each year by the many productions of the Nutcracker Ballet". https://www.nutcrackermuseum.com/history.htm . DW (Grenier, 5 Dec 2018) gives c. 1870 and says Füchtner drew on Heinrich Hoffmann's *König Nussknacker* (1851), not E.T.A. Hoffmann; Steinbach, refugee carvers relocated near Hanover after WWII, sold to US soldiers; the figures became popular in the US in the 1950s; Steinbach offers c. 350 figures and new collectible models yearly (incl. Darth Vader). https://www.dw.com/en/how-the-nutcracker-achieved-worldwide-fame/a-46529001 ; https://www.dw.com/en/a-brief-history-of-the-nutcracker-doll/g-45911564 . Atlas Obscura (2023): Füchtner jointed several turned pieces like house carpentry; the family workshop is in its eighth generation. [Date 1870 vs 1872: contested; say "early 1870s".]
- [documented, Variety 12 Feb 2001; Wikipedia; NYT 28 Sep 2003] ***Barbie in the Nutcracker*** (VHS 2 Oct 2001): Mattel's earliest full-length Barbie feature and earliest CGI Barbie film, by Mainframe Entertainment, budget $15 m (Variety); choreography Peter Martins; five NYCB principals motion-captured; London Symphony Orchestra. Planned tie-ins: "a book, eight dolls, a Hallmark Christmas ornament and related licenses"; Mattel expected *Nutcracker* products to top $100 m in 2001 (company's forecast). Artisan sold 3.4 m units = $65 m consumer spending (Variety 17 Mar 2002); 3.5 m by 2003 (NYT); "$150 million in total sales" incl. merchandise (Wikipedia, citing others) [probable]. Followed by *Barbie of Swan Lake* (2003, Martins again) and further ballet titles: a ballet-licensed film series. https://variety.com/2001/digital/features/mattel-s-barbie-dolls-it-up-for-nutcracker-pic-1117793579/ ; https://www.nytimes.com/2003/09/28/arts/dance-barbie-dances-with-help-from-city-ballet.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbie_in_the_Nutcracker

### Names as products: Pavlova, Taglioni, Sylphide
- [documented] **Pavlova (dessert).** Helen Leach (Otago), *The Pavlova Story: A Slice of New Zealand's Culinary History* (Otago UP, 2008): "three different pavlovas" (a layered jelly, small meringue kisses, a meringue cake) emerged in New Zealand cookbooks before 1930; contributions of "a gelatine manufacturer, a Dunedin spinster" and many housewives. https://www.otago.ac.nz/press/books/the-pavlova-story . Per ABC News (2024) summarising Leach, the earliest known recipe named "pavlova" was a promotional layered jelly in *Davis Dainty Dishes* (1927, Davis Gelatine company) [probable]. https://www.abc.net.au/news/2024-06-23/settling-the-debate-over-who-invented-the-pavlova/103541682 . National Geographic (2025): earliest meringue "pavlova" so labelled in the *NZ Dairy Exporter Annual* (1929); then the Rangiora Mothers' Union book (1933); Australia's claim is Bert Sachse, Esplanade Hotel, Perth (1935), whose own 1973 interview says he adapted a recipe sent by a New Zealander to *Australian Woman's Mirror*; the name probably honours Pavlova's 1926 Australasian tour; contemporaries named sponge cakes, jellies and an ice-cream after her too; Annabelle Utrecht and Andrew Paul Wood trace large meringue-cream-fruit constructions to 18th-c. German-speaking kitchens. https://www.nationalgeographic.com/travel/article/the-story-behind-pavlova-recipe-where-to-eat . **Key scholarly frame**: Leach, "The Pavlova Wars: How a Creationist Model of Recipe Origins Led to an International Dispute", *Gastronomica* 10:2 (2010) 24–30: the belief in a single inventor ("creationist model") vs cookbook evidence for "progressive parallel evolution". https://online.ucpress.edu/gastronomica/article-abstract/10/2/24/44114/ . [argued] This is the course's "earliest known, never first" rule as a published argument, and the first product to carry a ballerina's name was a manufacturer's promotion.
- [documented: Paris Musées / Palais Galliera record 2025.21.1318] fashion plate from *La Mode* (1830–40): "Robe à la Taglioni en tulle brodé et lamé des magasins de Mme Gagelin, rue Richelieu, 93", coiffure by M. Sergent: a ballerina's name used to sell a named shop's dress. https://www.parismuseescollections.paris.fr/en/node/621907 (Paris Musées released many works CC0; check this item's licence icon) .
- [probable: essay "Sylfever" hosted on vintagepointe.org (PDF, author and original venue not stated; reads like a company programme essay)] 1830s London retail around *La Sylphide*: "gauze sylphide", "tissu sylphide", "satin sylphide", "mousseline sylphide", a "La Sylphide" parasol, a "turban Sylphide", and by 1835 Madame Saint Anton's "Corset Perfectionne Sylphide"; racehorses named "Sylphide" and "Taglioni"; hair "à la Taglioni"; Princess Victoria owned a Sylphide doll and a horse named Taglioni (from her journal); an 1860 New York "La Sylphide Bourbon" label (A.M. Bininger & Co.). http://vintagepointe.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Sylfever.pdf . Verify the period quotations (e.g. *La Belle Assemblée*, *The World of Fashion*) before teaching.
- [probable, blog drawing on Russian sources] "à la Taglioni" wreaths of roses in St Petersburg ballrooms during her 1837–42 seasons. https://rosescrimeasite.wordpress.com/2024/02/09/the-russian-empress-maria-alexandrovna-upbringing-by-roses-2/
- [probable: Fokine's memoirs as quoted on a French blog] *Les Sylphides* (1909): every dancer in an identical Romantic tutu "à la Taglioni"; Fokine: "J'étais environné de 23 Taglioni" and checked every centre parting; later dancers tried to distinguish themselves with differently coloured wreaths, which he deplored. https://leschaussonsverts.eklablog.com/les-sylphides-1909-un-ballet-trois-histoires-a1875572 . [argued] A costume family whose rule is identity, and whose refused variation is the soloist's coloured wreath. Check in Fokine, *Memoirs of a Ballet Master* (1961).

### Museum exhibitions of ballet and fashion
- [documented] **Museum at FIT, *Ballerina: Fashion's Modern Muse*** (11 Feb–18 Apr 2020; curator Patricia Mears): more than 90 objects; tutu-inspired couture (e.g. Balenciaga for Hattie Carnegie, pink silk tulle, 1950), "American ready-to-wear separates based on leotards and tights and leg warmers", footwear from flat "ballerina" slippers to "pointe"-style high heels; ballet colours ("ballet pink"; "Sleeping" blue and lilac from *The Sleeping Beauty*) entering couture; dated span: haute couture first looked to the classics in the 1930s–70s; "Ballet's sway on fashion began to slowly diminish by the onset of the 1980s" (museum text). Ballerinas as "women of style" in Dior, YSL, Halston (Fonteyn, Markova, Tallchief, Virginia Johnson). Lent: Marc Happel's 2012 *Symphony in C* tutu (NYCB, Swarovski crystals); Messel's Aurora for Fonteyn (V&A). Panel on African American ballerinas (27 Feb 2020). Book: Mears et al., Vendome 2020. https://www.fitnyc.edu/museum/exhibitions/ballerina.php
- [documented] **Museum at FIT, *Dance & Fashion*** (13 Sep 2014–3 Jan 2015; Valerie Steele; architect Kim Ackert): nearly 100 costumes and dance-inspired fashions; Dior's "Black Swan" ball gown "epitomized ballet's influence on fashion"; Happel says his *Symphony in C* costumes drew on Dior and Balenciaga (the loop runs both ways); Pavlova's and Fonteyn's pointe shoes beside Louboutin and Noritaka Tatehana; a Bakst *Schéhérazade* costume (1910) acquired by MFIT beside YSL's 1976 "Ballets Russes" collection; NYCB costumes by Stella McCartney (*Ocean's Kingdom*, 2011) and Rodarte (*Two Hearts*, 2012); the museum lists Valentino Garavani, Prabal Gurung, Gilles Mendel, Olivier Theyskens, Rodarte and Iris van Herpen as NYCB designers, and YSL, Riccardo Tisci (Givenchy) for the Paris Opéra Ballet. https://www.fitnyc.edu/museum/exhibitions/dance-and-fashion.php

# SYNTHESIS

## A. What ballet teaches about worlds becoming product families [argued throughout, built on the log above]

1. **The family usually comes before the hero product.** The nutcracker figure (Erzgebirge, early 1870s) predates Tchaikovsky's ballet (1892); the *Nutcracker Suite* (March 1892) predates the ballet (Dec 1892); "pavlova" was a name applied to existing meringue cakes and jellies, and three pavlovas coexisted before one won; the leotard was an acrobat's *maillot* before it was a dancer's or a shopper's. Ballet often does not invent the product; it names, frames and re-distributes a family that already exists. This is the threshold concept "the collection precedes the object" with dates attached.
2. **Three ways of naming variants** in one object class: by **maker** (Freed's stamped symbols), by **dancer** (Grishko's Maya and Ulanova; Capezio's "Pavlova"; Repetto's "Zizi" and "Cendrillon" for Bardot), by **variables** (Gaynor Minden's eight-field code). Each is a different theory of where value lives: in the hand, in the star, or in the system.
3. **Adjacent translation keeps a small set of signs and drops the rest.** Fashion took from ballet: the bell or plate silhouette (tulle skirt), the colour "ballet pink", the flat soft slipper, the knitted second skin (leotard, tights, legwarmers), the wrap and the bun. It refused: the pointe box, the hard shank, the darned platform, the pain. The ballet flat is the pointe shoe with its technology removed.
4. **Names are carriers.** Taglioni (1830s dresses, wreaths, horses), the Sylphide (fabrics, a parasol, a corset), Pavlova (a dessert), Diaghilev (a perfume), Bardot (a shoe). The name travels farther and lasts longer than the dance.
5. **Drops are seasonal in ballet itself.** Diaghilev's season, the Paris Opéra's "territoire visuel" per season, NYCB's annual Fashion Gala premieres, Lyst's quarterly "hottest product", Steinbach's yearly new nutcracker models: the calendar structures the collection.
6. **The anchor pays for the range.** *Nutcracker* earns about 45% of NYCB's ticket revenue (company figure) and on average 48% of surveyed US companies' season revenue (Dance/USA 2017): one work funds the repertory, as one icon (Cendrillon, the Chanel two-tone, the Miu Miu flat) funds a house's variations.
7. **Refusals define families.** Fokine refused coloured wreaths in *Les Sylphides*; pink satin was the unspoken rule of the pointe shoe until dancers, a small company (Ballet Black) and a petition forced brown and bronze into the core range; Under Armour sold its brand by reading ballet's own refusals aloud and overturning them.

## B. Collection-DNA readings [argued: our interpretation; facts as marked in the log]

### B1. The pointe shoe as a product line
- **Anchor:** the satin "turn-shoe" with a paste-and-fabric box and a shank (Freed's hand-made shoe and its makers' marks; Freed's model names such as Classic and Studios are [probable], not checked this pass; Capezio's Pavlova as a named early model [contested dating]).
- **Family members:** per-maker variants (Freed: Crown, Anchor, Butterfly, Triangle, F); per-dancer models (Grishko Maya I/II, Ulanova I/II, Vaganova; four lasts, 26 models); per-variable configurations (Gaynor Minden: line × model × length × width × box × shank × vamp × heel; 5 shanks, 5 boxes, 2 vamps, 2 heels); colourways from 2017 (Gaynor Minden), 2018 (Freed Ballet Brown and Bronze), 2020 (Bloch after a petition) (scope 03).
- **Rules that keep them related:** a platform to stand on; a box that encloses the toes; a shank that supports the arch; satin (now mostly polyester) upper; ribbons and elastics sewn by the dancer; a look from the audience of a seamless line of leg.
- **Permitted variation:** last shape, hardness, vamp height, maker, and (since 2017) colour.
- **Refused (for most of its history):** visible technology; any colour but pink; a shoe that lasts (Gaynor Minden's durable elastomer was argued over precisely because it broke this rule).
- **Carriers and distribution:** company shoe rooms and bulk orders by maker; specialist fitters; dance shops (Freed's 50+ countries; Grishko's 70+); the fitting appointment as a ritual; the dancer's own finishing labour.
- **Course use:** a single object that shows three naming logics and a product code (Gaynor Minden's catalogue is a primary source for "grammar").

### B2. The tutu and costume as families
- **Anchor:** the white Romantic bell (the Sylphide "uniform", Taglioni/Lami) and the classical plate.
- **Members:** Romantic, classical plate, Karinska's "powder puff" (scope 03); corps, soloist, principal, and character costumes inside one production; fashion's tulle ball gown (Balenciaga for Hattie Carnegie, 1950; Dior's "Black Swan" gown).
- **Rules:** a fitted bodice on a basque; layered net; legs and line visible; identity across the corps.
- **Permitted variation:** colour and decoration by rank and role; length by period; shape by designer (Gaultier's square tutus, Viktor & Rolf's "nibbled" tutu).
- **Refused:** individual distinction in the corps (Fokine's "23 Taglioni"; no coloured wreaths).
- **Carriers:** company workrooms; couture houses at galas; exhibitions (FIT 2014, 2020; CNCS 2019–20); dolls and film (Barbie's *Nutcracker* and *Swan Lake*).

### B3. Practice clothes into everyday wear
- **Anchor:** the leotard and tights (the dancer's second skin).
- **Members:** McCardell's wool leotard as an under-layer (wartime); Danskin's bodysuit (snaps added), nylon bodywear, swimwear maillot (1976), aerobics lines (1980s), wrap skirt plus leotard as disco uniform (Bonnie August); legwarmers; "ballet pink" and "theatrical pink" tights as named colours; Freed's apparel from 1985; Repetto ready-to-wear.
- **Rules:** knit, stretch, close to the body, single colour, made for movement.
- **Permitted variation:** fastening, fibre (wool, cotton, nylon, Lycra), colour, cut at the leg and back, context (studio, street, beach, gym).
- **Refused:** structure (no boning, no shank); ornament that interrupts the line.
- **Carriers:** department stores (Lord & Taylor, Neiman Marcus), film (*Fame*, *Flashdance*), exercise videos, the gym, and in the 2020s TikTok.

### B4. The ballet flat
- **Anchor:** the soft ballet slipper made for the street (Capezio for McCardell 1941; Repetto's Cendrillon 1956).
- **Members:** Ferragamo for Hepburn (1954); Repetto Zizi (Oxford form, c. 1970) and 252-colour Atelier; Chanel's two-tone ballerina (SS 1984, after the 1957 slingback); Tory Burch Reva (2000s); Freed's flats (2013); Miu Miu satin flats (AW 2022, "a simpler, Gen-Z friendly version of its 2016 counterpart" per Lyst); Jacquemus × Repetto Zizi (2024).
- **Rules:** flat or near-flat; low vamp; soft sole; a drawstring or bow as the sign of the dance shoe.
- **Permitted variation:** toe-cap colour (Chanel), logo hardware (Tory Burch T; Chanel camellia), strap (Ferragamo; Miu Miu's elastic), material (satin, suede, patent), sole for the street.
- **Refused:** the box, the shank, the ribbons, the dancer's labour.
- **Carriers:** film stars (Bardot, Hepburn), designer collections, Lyst indices, TikTok; brand heritage pages that tell the story as lineage.

### B5. Balletcore (2022 onward)
- **Anchor:** the satin ballet flat (Miu Miu) and the wrap cardigan over a leotard-like top.
- **Members:** legwarmers, bodysuits, tulle skirts, ribbon bows, "ballet pink", hair bun.
- **Rules:** soft, pale, layered, bodily; a dancer's off-duty dress rather than the stage costume.
- **Permitted variation:** everything that is not the stage: it borrows the rehearsal room, not the performance.
- **Refused:** the hardness, the injury, the institution.
- **Carriers and data:** TikTok views (300 m+ reported, Jan 2023), Lyst indices and searches (+1,100% reported for the Miu Miu flat), retailer search data (Boohoo). All platform metrics, not sales.

### B6. The *Nutcracker* as a licensing economy
- **Anchor:** Balanchine's 1954 NYCB production in the US (scope 01/05) and the Tchaikovsky score.
- **Members:** the Suite (1892); the wooden figure (Füchtner, Steinbach, many makers; collector editions yearly); Disney *Fantasia* (1940); Barbie in the Nutcracker (2001: film, book, eight dolls, a Hallmark ornament, licences); Disney's *The Nutcracker and the Four Realms* (2018) [probable; box office not checked]; ornaments, advent calendars, sugar-plum confectionery.
- **Rules:** Christmas; a girl, a toy soldier, a journey to a land of sweets; Tchaikovsky's tunes.
- **Permitted variation:** setting (Bourne's orphanage, Morris's *Hard Nut*), cast, the national dances (redesigned after the Final Bow for Yellowface pledge).
- **Refused:** the dark Hoffmann source; and since 2017 in many companies, yellowface in "Tea".
- **Carriers and distribution:** the annual run (about 50 NYCB performances), school casts (children as performers and buyers), TV and video, the toy shop. The anchor funds the repertory.

### B7. The ballerina's name as a brand (Taglioni, Pavlova, Bardot, Copeland)
- **Anchor:** a star in a role (Taglioni as Sylph; Pavlova's tours and the Swan).
- **Members:** fabrics and dresses "à la Taglioni"; the Sylphide corset; Capezio's Pavlova shoe; the pavlova cakes; Grishko's Maya; Repetto's Zizi; Barbie's Misty Copeland doll (2016) [probable, not checked here].
- **Rules:** the name signals lightness and grace; the product promises some of it.
- **Permitted variation:** any category (food, clothing, horses).
- **Refused:** the dancer's control of the name: almost none of these were licensed by the dancer.
- **Carriers:** fashion plates, cookbooks, advertising, shop windows.

### B8. Company identity
- **Anchor:** the institutional mark (NYCB lyre from Noguchi's *Orpheus*; RBO crest kept from 2010, new "Granby" wordmark; Paris Opéra's fixed mark with a new seasonal territory each year).
- **Members:** season brochures, posters, programmes, cinema and streaming idents, shop goods.
- **Rules:** archive-derived (RBO's palette and wordmark from its archive); one red; one crest.
- **Permitted variation:** per season (Paris Opéra), per production, per channel (RBO's brightened red for screens).
- **Refused:** RBO's old house-first name: the rename privileges the companies over the building.
- **Carriers:** print, cinemas (RBO: 1,500 cinemas a season, company figure), social media.

## C. Collection logics inside ballet itself
- **Petipa's divertissement** [argued from works/W1]: a set of numbers held by one world (Act III of *Sleeping Beauty*: fairy-tale guests each with a variation). Anchor: the grand pas de deux. Members: national and character dances. Rule: each number is short, closed and showcases one dancer or type. Permitted variation: which tales, which nations. Refused: plot advance. This is a collection format, and it is detachable: numbers circulate as gala "singles" (Bluebird; Black Swan pas de deux).
- **Excerpt before work** [documented]: the *Nutcracker Suite* (March 1892) before the ballet (Dec 1892). The concert suite was the earliest distribution channel.
- **Diaghilev's season** [argued from works/W2 and scope 04]: a new "drop" each year of three or four one-act works per evening, balanced by register (exotic, Romantic, modern); new artists commissioned per drop; posters (Cocteau's matched pair, 1911) and *Comœdia illustré* programmes as the catalogue; a season curtain (Picasso's *Train Bleu* cloth) as a season asset later reassigned.
- **The mixed bill** [argued]: a capsule collection: three works under one evening's name and image.
- **Costume families in a production** [argued]: corps (identical), soloists (variant), principals (anchor), characters (exceptions); the rule of rank governs colour and decoration.
- **Repertory with an anchor** [documented for revenue]: *Nutcracker* at NYCB (about 45% of ticket revenue, company figure) funds commissions (Dance Magazine 2020 quotes Dance/USA's director that *Nutcracker* money pays for new work).

## D. Gaps and traps
- **"First" claims to neutralise**: Chanel "first couturier to create costumes for the stage" (curator's claim; Chanel dressed Cocteau's *Antigone* in 1922); Freed "first UK company" to make brown pointe shoes (true in the UK only; Gaynor Minden 2017); Danskin "first knit tights", "first nylon bodywear" (company); Capezio "Official Pointe Shoe Maker" to Pavlova "1892" (impossible date); VCA "extremely rare" motif before 1941 (house claim); Füchtner "first commercial" nutcrackers (museum's phrase; say "earliest known serial production").
- **Contested dates**: Grishko 1988/1989; Füchtner 1870/1872; Claude Arpels–Balanchine meeting late 1940s/1961/early 1960s; *Ballet Précieux* 2006/2007; McCardell–Capezio 1941 vs WWII leather shortage; Repetto's own page calls Zizi Jeanmaire Gainsbourg's "stepdaughter" (she was Roland Petit's wife).
- **Figures to label as someone's metric**: all balletcore numbers (Lyst, Boohoo, TikTok); Repetto revenue; Danskin's 80% share; Gaynor Minden's "half our shoe sales"; Barbie unit sales (Variety/Artisan vs Wikipedia $150 m); RBO's 1,500 cinemas; Under Armour views (Effie case).
- **Not found this pass**: *Black Swan* (2010) merchandise beyond the Rodarte costumes (no documented capsule or licensed line located); *The Nutcracker and the Four Realms* figures; the Misty Copeland Barbie (2016) not checked; Bloch and Capezio colour ranges (see scope 03); a scholarly history of balletcore; ballet-named perfumes before 1950 (Mitsouko is linked to Diaghilev only by anecdote); Disney's *Fantasia* merchandise; a full list of NYCB gala designers by year (FIT 2014 lists Valentino, Gurung, Mendel, Theyskens, Rodarte, van Herpen).
- **Rights**: the most teachable images (Cocteau posters, Chanel, Picasso, VCA, Ferragamo) are in copyright outside the US or held by companies; the open set is Romantic prints (PD), nutcracker photos (CC0/CC BY-SA), Met Bakst (CC0), NYPL and LoC items (check each).
