# R2 — Knowledge gaps review (dance history + design history)

Reviewer stance: two readers in one — a dance historian (what the field expects a well-taught learner to know) and a design historian (what a world-building course needs from ballet). Reviewed: scope/ (6 angle files + brief), works/ (W1–W4, S), repertory/ (CENSUS, register.yaml, threads), products/, decisions/, pass1_dossier, pass2_body/verify/presentation, CASE_WORLD_PLAN, UNIT_PLAN, board/. Method: grep counts and heading checks across ~220k words, then targeted reading where coverage looked thin. Severity: **MUST** add / **SHOULD** add / **COULD** add. Sources: scholarly first; [OA] marks open access; no URLs composed.

Status: complete, 25 October 2026.

## 0. Verdict in one paragraph

The research is unusually strong on **how a ballet is commissioned, specified, built, credited, paid for, recorded and remade**: the concept documents, the workshops, the shoe and the tutu, labour and law, notation, Orientalism and skin tone, the export routes, the collection logics. It is weak, and in places silent, on **the three things that make a ballet a world a person actually enters**: (1) **the work's internal grammar** (music as the time-frame, mime as the language, the pas de deux and the corps as a modular choreographic system, the dancer as the one who makes it live); (2) **the night** (the building, the auditorium, the interval, the applause, who could get in); and (3) **the media world that most people meet instead of the stage** (photographs, film, television, cinema relays, YouTube and Instagram, toys, children's books, films such as *The Red Shoes* and *Black Swan*). The six angle files were cut by *making*, not by *experience*, so whole areas fell between them: there is no music angle, no audience angle, no media angle and no childhood angle. For a course about turning a concept into "a finished creative world **and experience**", the second half of that phrase is under-researched. The AI Collaboration thread has three sentences of evidence; Handover Grammar is served by notation but not by the technical handover documents a designer actually produces.

## 2. MUST-add gaps

### 2.1 Music and the composer as maker (no music angle exists)

**What is missing.** Music appears only as a credit and as one superb document (Petipa's bar-count plan to Tchaikovsky). There is no account of: the **specialist ballet composer** (Pugni, Minkus, Drigo, writing to order and paid like staff: scope 05 has Pugni's 1860 contract but never draws the lesson) versus the **symphonic composer** whose score changes what ballet can be (Delibes, Tchaikovsky, then Stravinsky); the **conductor and arranger as editor** (Drigo's 1895 re-ordering and re-orchestration of *Swan Lake* after Tchaikovsky's death, the score most companies actually play; Lanchbery's Hérold arrangement for Ashton's *Fille mal gardée*, 1960; tempo as an interpretive decision taken with the dancers); **interpolation** (numbers moved between ballets for a star: the Black Swan pas de deux's own history); the **leitmotif** in *Giselle* (Adam) as a world-building device; the **rehearsal pianist and class accompanist**, whose improvisation times every exercise (scope 02 lists them as missing); the **concert suite** as the work's first product (the *Nutcracker Suite* played before the ballet, already in products); and **music-first** composition (Balanchine) versus **independence** (Cage) as two theories of how sound and movement share a world (W4 has the theory, not the practice).

**Why it matters here.** Music is the time-frame of a ballet world: it fixes duration, pace, entrances and the number structure, and it is the layer most likely to survive (W4 itself says choreography and music outlived décor). For the Concept DNA it is the clearest real case of **obligations** (bar counts), **anchor expression** (the score as anchor) and **adjacent translation** (suite, recording, film soundtrack). For Briefers, the commission letter to a composer is a model brief.

**Severity: MUST.** A dance historian would mark a ballet course with no account of the score as incomplete.

**Best sources.**
- Marian Smith, *Ballet and Opera in the Age of "Giselle"* (Princeton UP, 2000): already cited, but only for the livret; use its chapters on *airs parlants* and the rehearsal score.
- Roland John Wiley, *Tchaikovsky's Ballets* (Clarendon, 1985): already cited; use it for the 1877 failure, the 1895 Drigo revision and the plans as documents.
- Stephanie Jordan, *Moving Music: Dialogues with Music in Twentieth-Century Ballet* (Dance Books, 2000): the standard method for reading music and choreography together.
- Nadine Meisner, *Marius Petipa: The Emperor's Ballet Master* (OUP, 2019): Petipa's working relations with Minkus, Drigo and Tchaikovsky.

**Where.** A music strand of one sitting per act, plus one object study ("The score as a specification": Petipa's plan → Tchaikovsky's draft → Drigo's edit → the conductor's tempo on the night). F1 core: one card on the Petipa–Tchaikovsky plan as a brief. Borrowing: F3 (sound), M2 (obligations).

### 2.2 Mime: how ballet tells a story without words

**What is missing.** Mime appears only as the thing Noverre, Fokine and *drambalet* refused, and as what Ratmansky restored. The research never shows the **system**: a codified gesture vocabulary (marry, love, dance, die, swear, beautiful, queen, "I", "you"), the great mime scenes as narrative engines (Odette's Act II story in *Swan Lake*; Giselle's mother telling of the Wilis; the Lilac Fairy against Carabosse in the *Sleeping Beauty* Prologue), the music written to carry mime (Smith's *airs parlants*: well-known tunes quoted so the Paris audience could follow), **character dance** as a second legibility system (national dances as costume-plus-rhythm signs), the **programme synopsis** as a legibility device, and the Royal Danish Ballet's Bournonville tradition as a living mime archive.

**Why it matters here.** This is the single best case in the course of a **designed sign language that lets a world be read by an audience**, which is the core problem of any world without words: retail, wayfinding, games, installations, brand systems. It also teaches that legibility is negotiated with an audience over time (the codes stopped being read, so reformers cut them; reconstructions now restore them with programme notes). It belongs in the Concept DNA's **grammars** field.

**Severity: MUST.**

**Best sources.**
- Marian Smith (2000), as above, chapters on pantomime and *airs parlants*.
- Edward Nye, *Mime, Music and Drama on the Eighteenth-Century Stage: The Ballet d'Action* (CUP, 2011): already in W4's list; under-used.
- Doug Fullington, "Finding the Balance: Pantomime and Dance in Ratmansky's New/Old *Sleeping Beauty*", in *The Oxford Handbook of Dance and Reenactment* (online 2017): already cited in W3; use it as the case study.
- Knud Arne Jürgensen, *The Bournonville Heritage* (Dance Books, 1990), for Danish mime as a preserved system. Also the companies' own mime explainer films (Royal Ballet and Opera, Royal Danish Ballet): institutional, label as such.

**Where.** Act II (the Romantic livret and *airs parlants*), Act III (*Swan Lake* Act II and the *Sleeping Beauty* Prologue), and Act VI (Ratmansky's restoration). A five-minute "learn six gestures" activity is the best possible phone-first opener for the F1 core.

### 2.3 Choreography as craft: the internal grammar of a ballet

**What is missing.** The files read choreography through theory (Ménestrier, Noverre, Fokine, Forsythe, McGregor) and through authorship disputes. Nowhere does a learner meet the **working grammar** a ballet is built from:
- the **step vocabulary** as material, and the **enchaînement** (a phrase of steps) as the unit of composition;
- the **grand pas de deux formula** (entrée, adagio, two variations, coda) as a reusable template that every Petipa ballet, and every gala, runs on;
- the **corps de ballet as geometry**: lines, diagonals, wedges, canon, unison against counterpoint; the Shades entrance in *La Bayadère* (one phrase repeated by a corps of two dozen or more, entering one by one down a ramp; the number varies by production and should be checked per staging) as the purest case of a rule generating a world;
- the **number structure** (scene, pas d'action, variation, divertissement, apotheosis) as the architecture of an act;
- the choreographer's **tools** in the studio (demonstration, dancers' improvisation, counts, video, the répétiteur, "setting" on a cast);
- the **rehearsal process** from first call to dress rehearsal, and what changes in the last week.

**Why it matters here.** This is ballet's **component library**: a modular system of named parts with strict rules of combination, which is exactly what the program's threshold concepts "a concept is a generative system" and "the collection precedes the object" need to see working. CASE_WORLD_PLAN §3.6 has the *collection logics* (divertissement, season, mixed bill), but not the grammar that sits under them. Without it, learners are told ballet is a system but never see the system.

**Severity: MUST.**

**Best sources.**
- Janet Adshead (ed.), *Dance Analysis: Theory and Practice* (Dance Books, 1988): the standard method (components, relations, interpretation, evaluation), the best fit for a design course's "how to read a made thing".
- Jacqueline M. Smith-Autard, *Dance Composition: A Practical Guide to Creative Success in Dance Making* (Bloomsbury, many editions since 1976): motif, development, structure; the UK school-and-university standard.
- Twyla Tharp, *The Creative Habit: Learn It and Use It for Life* (Simon & Schuster, 2003): a ballet-and-modern choreographer's account of process written for non-dancers; ideal for the Maker lane.
- Meisner (2019) and Jennifer Homans, *Mr. B: George Balanchine's 20th Century* (Random House, 2022), for how Petipa and Balanchine actually worked in the studio.

**Where.** Act III (the pas de deux formula and the Shades as a rule-generated world), Act IV (Balanchine's plotless grammar), Act VI (Forsythe's operations). A "build a pas de deux from its five parts" activity in the F1 core. Borrowing: M2 grammars field; M3 World architecture.

### 2.4 The night: audience, ritual and who could attend

**What is missing.** Each full card has a heading "the night", but in practice it gives the date, theatre and budget. The experience of attending is absent: buying the ticket; the **subscription** and the box as social property (the Paris *abonnés*, the Imperial court's seating); the **claque** (paid applause in Paris, active into the 20th century); the **foyer** and the grand staircase as stages for the audience; **lights down** (gaslit auditoria stayed lit; the darkened house is a late-19th-century invention associated with Bayreuth, 1876); the overture; the **interval** as a designed social and commercial event (bars, champagne, the programme sale); **applause conventions** (applauding entrances and variations mid-act, unlike opera and concerts; the ballet's built-in pauses for applause); the **curtain call, révérence and bouquets** as a scripted coda; the **stage door**; and **who could get in** (court, subscriber, the 19th-century bourgeois public, Soviet factory allocations, 20th-century cheap seats and student standby, today's price ladders). The research has the Foyer de la danse only as a site of sexual exploitation; that is important and stays, but the foyer was also the hinge between the world on stage and the world in the house.

**Why it matters here.** The course says it teaches how a concept becomes "a finished creative world and experience". The night *is* the experience, and ballet has a 400-year record of designing it: a time-structured journey with thresholds, rituals, rest stops, souvenirs and exits. It is the best historical case the program has for **experience design**, **service blueprints** and **journey maps**, and it shows that an audience is trained into a world (they learn when to clap). It also carries the **access** question (who could enter, at what price, by what dress code).

**Severity: MUST.**

**Best sources.**
- James H. Johnson, *Listening in Paris: A Cultural History* (University of California Press, 1995): how Paris audiences moved from talk to silence, the claque, the boxes; check whether it is in the UC Press E-Books Collection (possibly [OA]).
- Lawrence W. Levine, *Highbrow/Lowbrow: The Emergence of Cultural Hierarchy in America* (Harvard UP, 1988): the "sacralisation" of the audience.
- Matthew Reason and Dee Reynolds, "Kinesthesia, Empathy, and Related Pleasures: An Inquiry into Audience Experiences of Watching Dance", *Dance Research Journal* 42.2 (2010), from the AHRC "Watching Dance" project: what audiences actually report feeling. Pair with Susan Leigh Foster, *Choreographing Empathy: Kinesthesia in Performance* (Routledge, 2011).
- Lynn Garafola, "The Travesty Dancer in Nineteenth-Century Ballet" (already cited, [OA] at Columbia Academic Commons) for the male gaze of the subscribers.

**Where.** Act II (the Paris Opéra night: subscribers, claque, gaslight), Act III (the Imperial court in the house), Act VI (today's price ladder, relays, dress codes). A **journey-map activity** of one ballet evening, then and now, in the F1 core. Borrowing: S1 Space and experience takes it whole; R1 (pricing and access).

### 2.5 Theatre buildings as designed worlds

**What is missing.** The Garnier appears as a 5% rake and the steps of a strike; the Gulf houses as opening dates. There is no account of the **building as the first expression of the world and its social order**: Charles Garnier's Opéra (1875) designed so the audience would perform to itself on the Grand Escalier (his own book *Le Théâtre*, 1871, says so), the Chagall ceiling (1964) as a later layer; the **Mariinsky** (Alberto Cavos, 1860) and the **Mariinsky II** (Diamond Schmitt, 2013, after Dominique Perrault's competition-winning design was dropped); the **Bolshoi** (rebuilt after the 1853 fire; restoration 2005–11 and its cost); the **Royal Opera House** (E. M. Barry, 1858; Dixon Jones and BDP redevelopment, 1999; Stanton Williams "Open Up", 2018, which redesigned the threshold for a public that did not feel invited); the **Sydney Opera House** (Utzon, 1973; the hall swap after his resignation left the ballet in a theatre with a cramped stage and pit, a famous case of a symbol beating a brief); the **David H. Koch (New York State) Theater** (Philip Johnson, 1964, built with Kirstein and Balanchine for dance); the **Oslo Opera House** (Snøhetta, 2008, a roof the public walks on); **Dubai Opera** (Atkins, principal architect Janus Rostock, opened 31 August 2016: a dhow-shaped building whose hall converts by hydraulics from theatre to concert hall to flat-floor event space, which makes it a building designed for a market with no resident company); and the **Royal Opera House Muscat** (2011). Backstage as architecture (the fly tower, the rake, the sprung floor, the pit) is partly in pass2_body and scope 03.

**Why it matters here.** For world-builders the house is the **perimeter made physical**: it decides who sits where, how you enter, what you see on the way, and which kinds of world can be staged at all (a raked stage, a small pit, a flat floor). The Gulf houses are the program's local, visitable cases, and Dubai Opera's transformable hall is a design answer to exactly the absence the research documents (no resident company).

**Severity: MUST** (the Gulf learner can walk into two of these buildings).

**Best sources.**
- Iain Mackintosh, *Architecture, Actor and Audience* (Routledge, 1993): the standard design-side book on auditoria and the relation of stage to house.
- Christopher Curtis Mead, *Charles Garnier's Paris Opéra: Architectural Empathy and the Renaissance of French Classicism* (MIT Press, 1991); with Garnier's own *Le Théâtre* (1871), which should be on Gallica (check; probable [OA] as public domain).
- UNESCO World Heritage nomination dossier for the Sydney Opera House (2007) [OA]; Françoise Fromonot, *Jørn Utzon: The Sydney Opera House* (Gingko/Electa, 1998).
- For the Gulf: Paolo Petrocelli (2019) and Katherine Hennessey on ROHM, already cited; Dubai Opera's own "Our Story" and architecture pages (institutional; label as self-description); the published **technical specification packs** of Dubai Opera and ROHM (venue documents; find by search), which are also handover documents (see 3.2).

**Where.** Prologue (the house as the first thing a world shows), Act II (Le Peletier and gaslight), Act III (Mariinsky), Act VI (Sydney, Oslo, ROH "Open Up", Dubai, Muscat). A new object study, "The house as a world", in the objects strand. Borrowing: S1 takes it whole.

### 2.6 The media world: photography, film, television, relays, social media

**What is missing.** Apart from Shiryaev's 1906–09 films (a fine find) and licence notes for Bert and Hoppé, there is no history of how ballet became an **image** and a **screen product**:
- **Photography**: the carte-de-visite ballerina; Auguste Bert and E. O. Hoppé on the Ballets Russes (Hoppé's *Studies from the Russian Ballet*, 1911); the studio posed photograph versus the action shot; Gordon Anthony and Baron (*Baron at the Ballet*, 1950) building the image of British ballet; Martha Swope at NYCB; the photo-call as a production stage; today's dancer-photographer genre (the "Ballerina Project" street shots).
- **Film**: Soviet ballet films (*Romeo and Juliet*, Mosfilm 1955, Ulanova; the Kirov *Sleeping Beauty*, Lenfilm 1964) as export products; *The Red Shoes* (Powell and Pressburger, 1948), which recruited a generation; Hollywood's ballet narratives.
- **Television**: Balanchine's *Nutcracker* on CBS (1958), BBC studio ballet, *Live from Lincoln Center* (from 1976); "dance for camera" as a form.
- **Cinema relays and streaming**: Royal Opera House cinema relays (from 2008), the Bolshoi in cinema via Pathé Live, Marquee TV (2018), the company streaming platforms after 2020; **World Ballet Day** (from 2014), which turned the daily class, the back room, into a global broadcast.
- **Social media**: YouTube variations as a de facto archive; Instagram dancers as their own brands; competition and class clips on TikTok; the effect on technique (the extreme extension as a "shareable" image).

**Why it matters here.** Most people meet ballet as images, not performances. For a world-building course this is the **transmedia** layer (Wolf's "windows", Jenkins's "world-making"): the world is carried and sold by media long after the night. It is also where the **Degas problem** (scope 06) continues: the image of the ballerina that the public holds is a media construction. And it is a rights case (Hoppé in UK copyright to 2042; the RBO's own films).

**Severity: MUST** for this course (SHOULD for a pure dance-history course).

**Best sources.**
- William A. Ewing, *The Fugitive Gesture: Masterpieces of Dance Photography* (Thames & Hudson, 1987).
- Adrienne L. McLean, *Dying Swans and Madmen: Ballet, the Body, and Narrative Cinema* (Rutgers UP, 2008): ballet in Hollywood and British film from the 1930s.
- Sherril Dodds, *Dance on Screen: Genres and Media from Hollywood to Experimental Art* (Palgrave, 2001); Erin Brannigan, *Dancefilm: Choreography and the Moving Image* (OUP, 2011); *The International Journal of Screendance* (Ohio State University Libraries) [OA].
- Martin Barker, *"Live to Your Local Cinema": The Remarkable Rise of Livecasting* (Palgrave Pivot, 2013).
- Harmony Bench, *Perpetual Motion: Dance, Digital Cultures, and the Common* (University of Minnesota Press, 2020) [OA via DOAB and Manifold].

**Where.** A new act-spanning strand, "How the world travels": Act IV (photography and the Ballets Russes), Act V (Soviet film, *The Red Shoes*, television), Act VI (relays, streaming, Instagram, World Ballet Day). Object study 10 ("Ballet now as fashion and identity") should absorb the social-media part. Borrowing: S5 Identity and communication; S6 Digital.

### 2.7 AI, motion capture and digital ballet (the AI Collaboration thread)

**What is missing.** The research has McGregor's *Becoming* and *AISOMA* (W4 §1.11), Forsythe's *Synchronous Objects*, and one line on the NYCB 2025 AI and likeness clause. That is not enough for a program-wide thread whose Level 1 checkpoint is "parametric first rule" and whose owner is the Stranger Lab. Missing:
- **The lineage of the machine as co-choreographer**: Merce Cunningham composing with the LifeForms software (from *Trackers*, 1991), developed at Simon Fraser University; *BIPED* (1999, motion capture by Paul Kaiser and Shelley Eshkar) and OpenEndedGroup's *Loops*; Forsythe's *Improvisation Technologies* (1994/1999) and **Motion Bank** (The Forsythe Company, 2010–13), which made digital "scores" of other choreographers' work; McGregor's **Living Archive** with Google Arts & Culture Lab (2019), a tool trained on 25 years of his video archive that proposes movement to dancers.
- **Generative dance models** trained on video of dancers: the AIST++ dataset and "AI Choreographer" (Li and others, ICCV 2021) and EDGE (Tseng, Castellon and Liu, CVPR 2023), both [OA] on arXiv. These raise the questions the thread needs: whose movement trained the model, who is credited, what the dancer consented to.
- **Likeness and digital doubles**: the NYCB clause (already found), and the *Black Swan* (2010) dispute over how much of the dancing on screen was the star's and how much her uncredited dance double's (Sarah Lane, 2011), which is the clearest public case of a digitally composited dancer and a disappeared co-author.
- **Digital scenography and capture on stage** (projection and tracking in contemporary ballet), which scope 04 lists only as a heading.

**Why it matters here.** Ballet is a rare field with a 35-year record of artists using software to generate, notate and hand over movement, and a present-day labour fight over likeness. It lets the AI Collaboration thread start from history, not hype: the tool proposes, the choreographer selects, the dancer embodies, and the credit line decides who was the author. It also connects to Attribution and Authority (who is the author of a machine-proposed phrase?).

**Severity: MUST** (a named program thread is barely served).

**Best sources.**
- Thomas Calvert and others, "The Evolution of an Interface for Choreographers", *Proceedings of CHI '93* (ACM, 1993), on LifeForms; the Merce Cunningham Trust's own pages on LifeForms and *BIPED* (institutional).
- Scott deLahunta (already cited) on Motion Bank and choreographic objects; Motion Bank's own published scores (online; check licence).
- McGregor, *Living Archive* (2019), on waynemcgregor.com and Google Arts & Culture (institutional; describe as such); W4 already holds *AISOMA*.
- Ruilong Li and others, "AI Choreographer: Music Conditioned 3D Dance Generation with AIST++" (ICCV 2021) [OA]; Jonathan Tseng, Rodrigo Castellon and C. Karen Liu, "EDGE: Editable Dance Generation from Music" (CVPR 2023) [OA].
- Harmony Bench (2020) [OA] for the digital circulation of dance.

**Where.** Act IV (Cunningham's counter-model, then LifeForms as its continuation), Act VI (Living Archive, generative models, the likeness clause). A thread checkpoint activity: give the Stranger a rule set ("parametric first rule") and judge its phrases the way McGregor's dancers judged Living Archive's. Borrowing: S6; the Stranger Lab.

### 2.8 Handover beyond notation (the Handover Grammar thread)

**What is missing.** Handover is served by notation (Stepanov to Sergeyev to Ratmansky), the Cunningham Dance Capsules and McGregor's *Mind and Movement*, which is good. But the thread is defined in the program as the designer's technical handover (orthographic language, construction logic, "the audited package"). Ballet produces exactly such packages and the research does not name them:
- the **production bible** (the record of a staging: blocking, casting, cues, design references);
- the **stage manager's calling script** or prompt book, with lighting and fly cues;
- the **lighting plot and cue sheet** (lighting design as a profession appears in MODULE_PLAN's topic list but has no research behind it: Jean Rosenthal, Jennifer Tipton);
- the **costume bible** (fabric swatches, dye recipes, construction drawings, measurement sheets for every dancer in a role);
- the **technical rider** a touring company sends ahead, and the **venue technical specification** the house sends back (stage dimensions, rake, floor, pit, flying, power): Dubai Opera and ROHM publish these;

## 6. Lessons a learner should leave with that no file states plainly

The plans state six lessons well (the idea rarely comes from the choreographer; roots are nameable; the concept leaves documents; worlds are built from rules and refusals; remaking is part of a concept's life; the credit line is a design decision). These are missing and should be stated in the same plain form:

1. **A world is experienced in time, and the time is designed.** A ballet is an evening with a structure (overture, acts, interval, curtain calls), and the structure is as designed as the décor. The score is that structure's skeleton.
2. **A world must teach its audience to read it.** Mime, character dance, quoted tunes and the printed synopsis were legibility systems; when audiences stopped reading them, the world had to change or explain itself. Every world needs an onboarding grammar.
3. **Strong worlds run on a small, strict, reusable grammar.** The pas de deux formula and the corps rules let hundreds of ballets be built from the same parts; what varies is the dialect. That is a component library, not a style.
4. **The building is the first expression of the world, and it decides who belongs.** Staircase, boxes, price ladder and dress code are design; so is the threshold a company redesigns to let new people in.
5. **Most people meet a world through its media, not its premiere.** Photographs, films, toys, books and feeds made "the ballerina" for millions who never saw a ballet. Design the windows as carefully as the stage.
6. **A world survives only if its paper and its people are handed over together.** Notation without a répétiteur, or a rider without a stage manager, does not carry the world.
7. **A world recruits its next makers from childhood, and that pipeline is also its biggest product.** The ballet class, the pink tutu and the competition are how the world reproduces itself, at a cost to bodies and families.
8. **The performer co-authors the world every night.** The work is not only what the choreographer fixed; it is what each cast makes of it, and the credit line rarely says so.
9. **A world is defined against its neighbours.** Ballet knew itself by refusing modern dance and folk forms, and later by borrowing from them; the boundary is part of the identity.
10. **The same world is read differently by different audiences.** "Arabian Coffee" is a fantasy in New York and a caricature in Dubai; a world-builder must ask who is watching.
