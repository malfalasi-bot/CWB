# R3 — Writing and formats review: the ballet cards and the pointe-shoe page

Reviewer brief: read the proposed ballet "short cards", the case card, and the built pointe-shoe page as a learner (a designer or briefer, on a phone, twenty minutes), then say what the learning and museum-interpretation research says, propose better forms, and rewrite.

Status: complete, 25 October 2026. Sections: 1 read as a learner; 2 research; 3 forms; 4 rewrites; 5 recommendations.

**Material read in full:** `CASE_WORLD_PLAN.md` §3.2 (the *Firebird* and *Train Bleu* short cards); `MODULE_PLAN.md` §5 (the case card and the filled *Giselle* card); `build/units/ballet/the-shoe.md` with `the-shoe-v1.md` and `the-shoe-v2.md`; `works/W2_modernism.md` §0.1, §2, §8; `works/W1_court_to_imperial.md` §5; `pass2_presentation.md`; `board/SUMMARY.md` and `board/round3/learner.md`; the voice guide; the structure's presentation forms and lecture rules; the personas; F1.4 and F1.25 as the house standard.

**One-line verdict.** The research is strong and the facts are mostly there; the cards throw away the verbs. A short card that holds about 25 names and three finite verbs can be filed but not understood, and the built shoe page, which is real prose, buries its one big idea halfway down and tells a procedure as a paragraph. The fix is not fewer facts. It is a through-question, causal sentences, and a form chosen for each kind of content.

### 1.2 *The Firebird* short card (CASE_WORLD_PLAN §3.2)

**What it tells.** A ballet of 1910, premiered in Paris, based on a Russian fairy tale, made from four named sources, with a rule about who dances how, a look, a cast structure, some concert suites, three later versions, and a credit line.

**What I could say back after one reading.** "A Russian fairy-tale ballet with a magic bird, Paris 1910, Stravinsky's music, folk sources. Classical dance for magic, folk dance for humans." That is two sentences out of 193 words, and the second is the only idea a designer could use.

**What is understood.** The plot ("a prince frees captive princesses from a sorcerer with the feather of a magic bird"); the anchor image (the bird); the split rule, if the reader already knows what "on pointe" and "round-dance" mean.

**What is lost.** Everything that makes this a case of *making*:
- **Why "made for export"?** The card says "a Russian fairy tale made for export" and stops. Export from where, by whom, to whom, and why would a Russian company make Russianness for Paris? The research has the answer: Diaghilev's company sold Russia to Paris ("Diaghilev invented Russia for foreigners", Chanel, probable, W2 §0.1); the ballet was not staged in Russia until 1921 and entered the Mariinsky only in 1994 (W2 §2.6). On the card that stake survives as the last clause of "Remade as", where it is not a remake at all: "staged in Russia only from 1921".
- **How it was made.** The single most teachable fact in the research, that a committee of friends devised the scenario over a winter and that two composers dropped out before a 27-year-old Stravinsky got the commission, is in italics at the foot: "*Credit: an idea worked out by a committee in Diaghilev's circle*". Fokine dictating the music to the composer passage by passage, with "the piano substituting for the wall" (W2 §2.3), is gone. So is Diaghilev's veto (Golovin's costumes for the bird replaced by Bakst's). These are the moments a designer recognises from their own work: a brief by committee, a supplier who did not deliver, a client who overrules the designer.
- **Why the rule matters.** "classical dance on pointe for magic, folk round-dance for humans, grotesque angles for monsters" is the card's best idea, but nothing says what the rule *does*: it lets an audience tell, from movement alone, which world each character belongs to. Without the "so that", it reads as a fact about steps.

**What is noise for this reader.** "Polonsky's poem 'A Winter's Journey'", "Abramtsevo and Talashkino", "concert suites (1911, 1919, 1945)", "Threads: two worlds · the bird-woman · folk as national idiom · the fairy tale". Each is a name or a tag with no verb attached. Abramtsevo and Talashkino are not explained in the card, and the research itself only says "the neo-Russian craft revival at Abramtsevo and Talashkino, through Golovin" (W2 §2.2).

**Why it reads as chopped data.**
- **No through-question.** The card answers ten fields, not one question. A reader cannot tell which field matters.
- **Lists instead of sentences.** "Built from" is four semicolon-separated sources with no verb saying how each was used, although §3.2's own field definition asks for "how each was used".
- **Unexplained names.** Diaghilev appears only in the credit line and is never introduced; Afanasyev, Polonsky, Rimsky-Korsakov, Golovin, Bakst, Goncharova, Chagall, Balanchine are each one word.
- **Insider terms.** "divertissement", "corps", "anchor image", "threads", "re-set" (the remake modes). "No inserted divertissement" means nothing to Omar; it is the Anti-DNA field showing through.
- **The schema shows.** The field labels are Concept DNA fields renamed. That is right for the reference form (the structure's "revisiting as reference, 2–5 min") and wrong for the first meeting with a work.
- **No confidence.** §3.2 itself asks for "flags on anything probable or contested". The card states "the Firebird became a sign of Russianness" flat; W2 marks it probable. "The world" and "the family" are W2's [argued] readings, presented as facts (voice guide W5).
- **Voice-guide checks it fails.** W1 (it opens on a category, "The idea:", not on something you can see); N1 (it has almost no sentences to measure); the Label tier's "ends with the main gap or question" (it ends on a credit).

### 1.3 *Le Train Bleu* short card

**Say-back after one reading.** "A 1920s beach ballet in real sportswear by Chanel; a Picasso curtain." The curtain is what I remembered, and the card files it under "Off the stage", where it does not belong (it hung on the stage).

**What is lost.**
- **The trigger.** W2 §8.3 says Cocteau had the idea after seeing the young English dancer Anton Dolin do acrobatics. The card reduces this to the fifth item of a list: "a young dancer's acrobatics". The one causal sentence the work has (a person saw something and had an idea) is turned into an ingredient.
- **The curtain story.** A gouache 34 × 42.5 cm, seen in Picasso's studio, enlarged in 24 hours by Prince Alexander Schervashidze to 6.78 × 8 m, first used as a season front cloth and attached to this ballet only months later (W2 §8.3). That is a worked example of scaling a small design to a huge one and of a collection asset being reassigned. On the card it is "Picasso's curtain, enlarged from a small gouache, was a season asset later attached to this ballet", and "season asset" is jargon from the plan, not a word a learner has met.
- **The contrast that gives it meaning.** "refuses fantasy costume, Russianness and the past" is a superb rule, but the card does not say *whose* Russianness. Fourteen years after *The Firebird*, the same company refused the very look that had made its name. Placed beside the *Firebird* card, that is the lesson; on its own, it is a phrase.
- **The so-what of the knitwear.** "hand-knitted suits for the leads, machine-knitted for the chorus" is a lovely concrete fact about a costume family: rank shown by the method of making. The card does not say so.
- **The stake in the remake.** "in Lycra versions of Chanel's designs made with Chanel's permission" hides the point W2 makes plainly: a living brand controls costumes a century later.

**Unexplained names:** Suzanne Lenglen and the Prince of Wales (who they are and why they are in a ballet: the tennis champion and the golfer the characters were based on); Offenbach; Laurens; Nijinska; Shervashidze. Thirty-five capitalised words in 198.

### 1.4 The *Giselle* case card (MODULE_PLAN §5)

This is the best of the three, because some fields contain real sentences with causes: "His first draft, an empty ballroom of Wilis, was rejected as a string of dances. Saint-Georges built the plot. The director, Léon Pillet, needed a vehicle to launch Carlotta Grisi." A reader can follow that.

**Say-back after one reading.** "A critic, Gautier, got the idea from Heine; his first draft was rejected; a librettist made the plot; a director wanted a star vehicle; one choreographer did the work and another got the credit; it died in Paris and survived in Russia."

**Where it still fails the learner.**
- **Order.** "Roots" comes before "Premise", so the reader meets "Heine's passage on the Wilis" before being told what a Wili is or what the ballet is about. The story arrives in a parenthesis inside "Premise". The heading order follows how a researcher fills the record, not how a reader builds a picture.
- **Causes left unjoined.** "Ciceri's Act I set reused from an earlier ballet" sits under "How it was made", and "Pillet ... pushed for speed" sits elsewhere in the research (W1 §5.3). Joined, they explain each other: a director in a hurry, so the set was reused. Apart, they are two facts.
- **The best story told as fragments.** "dropped in Paris in 1868; survived in St Petersburg; returned west as 'Russian' in 1911" is a French ballet that was kept alive in Russia and sold back to the West as Russian. It is the most memorable thing about *Giselle*, written as five semicolon clauses.
- **Apparatus in the learner's line.** "Lormier's designs (BnF, non-commercial)" puts a licence note into the record field; learners do not need "non-commercial" here.
- **Insider terms.** "white acts", "Stepanov's notation", "Justamant" without a word of explanation.
- **"The night: the Opéra's subscribers; an immediate success."** The field promises the experience and delivers seven words. A reader learns nothing about what the audience saw.
- **Nine headings is nine slots.** The nine-heading template is a good analyst's tool and a heavy first read; nine equal headings give no signal of which one matters for this work.

### 1.5 "The shoe" page (current, with v1 and v2)

This page is prose, it names people, it is careful about doubt, and three rounds of review raised it to 4 of 5 with every reader. It is far better than the cards. The problems are structure and purpose, not sentences.

**Say-back after one reading** (mine, matching the board's round-3 learner): "A pointe shoe is paper, hessian and flour paste, hand-built inside out; makers stamp a mark on the sole; Ray is paid by the pair; the companies' numbers are their own." What I could not say back: why any of this matters to a designer building a world, and how the shoe came to exist.

**What works.**
- Ray: "so they know who to blame". One person, one quote, one fact about pay. The board's learner wrote, "he is the page".
- "The dancer is on the poster. The designer is in the programme. The maker is a symbol on a sole." This is a Serrell-style big idea: three short sentences, concrete, checkable.
- "The idea came before the technology." The best causal sentence on the page.

**What is lost or misplaced.**
- **The big idea arrives late.** "The dancer is on the poster..." sits in the seventh paragraph, about 450 words in. The opening paragraph instead promises three themes at once: "the body, the craft and the money meet in it".
- **The cause comes last.** "The idea came before the technology" opens the *final* section. For a course about turning ideas into worlds, the shoe's story starts there: Taglioni made standing on the toe *mean* weightlessness in 1832, in a slipper with no block, and the block came later to serve that meaning. Told last, after the factory, it reads as a footnote; told first, it is the thesis.
- **A procedure told as a paragraph.** "How it is built" holds about eight steps in one paragraph (last, satin upper, block of hessian and paper, paste, pleats, sole pasted and hammered, turned, oven). The figure beside it is a cross-section, which shows *parts*, not *steps*. Text and picture describe different things, and the board's learner found the key and the text repeating each other ("The key and the next paragraph say the same thing twice").
- **Numbers without a question.** "What a company says about itself" gives Freed's 160,000 and "nearly 250,000", visitors' 150,000 to 333,000, six thousand pairs at fifty pounds, 780,000 dollars, forty-two hours against six. The board's learner kept none of the factory totals and none of the 780,000. The objective ("spot which numbers on this page are a company's own") is shown, not practised; the board itself said so ("A second activity... would make objective 3 practised rather than shown").
- **Names with nothing attached.** Ray, Taksim, Daniel, Sheila, Varsha Bahen, Rashimi Patel, Sergei Murza, Olga Monakhova. A name sticks when something is attached to it (Ray's quote, Daniel's four months, Sheila's forty years); the rest are a roll call.
- **Causal leaps.** "pointe is danced almost entirely by women, so this hour on her own time is women's work" is true and important, but the "so" carries an argument the page does not make.
- **Forward reference into nowhere.** "The foot pays too; the injury counts are on page 4."
- **No world-building frame.** CASE_WORLD_PLAN §3.3 says the pointe-shoe study follows five moves (the idea the object carries; how it is made; the family; off the stage; make). The built page does move 2, attribution, and a late fragment of move 1. The family (Freed's marks as one way of naming variants, Gaynor Minden's code, the brown and bronze range) and the life off the stage are not on it, so a designer gets a labour page, not a lesson in how a world becomes a product line. (That is a scope point for R1; here it matters because it leaves the page without a through-question a designer would ask.)

**What the three versions show.** Each review round made the page more accurate and slightly harder to read. v1: "His name is not in the record. His mark is on thousands of feet." (wrong, as it turned out: the source names Ray, but it is the kind of sentence a reader keeps). v3: "Ray, whose mark is the crown, told a writer from Spitalfields Life, a local blog that visited his bench in 2013, that he had been there twenty-six years and makes forty pairs a day." v1: "That is a union's figure in a dispute. It is also the only count of this labour anyone has made." v3: "The figure is a union's in a dispute, and the nearest count of this labour this page found." Every correction added a clause of attribution to the sentence. The lesson is not to hedge less; it is to move attribution into the structure (a source line per step, a tombstone under each image, as the Met and the London Museum do in `pass2_presentation.md`), so the sentence can stay short and the claim stays honest.

**One conflict to flag.** The page opens with "Before you read on, choose one" (a three-option prediction). If the owner's ruling against opening quizzes covers prediction prompts, this must move: either after the build has been explained, or replaced by a looking prompt ("Turn the shoe over") that asks for no answer.

## 2. What the learning research says

**How the citations are marked.** *[checked]*: opened or its abstract read in this pass (numbers as reported there). *[lit.]*: a standard reference I cite from the literature without re-opening it here; check the number at source before it goes into learner-facing copy or a decision paper. No URLs are given: references are by author, year, title and venue. Consensus and scite could not be used in this pass (quota and subscription); web search and fetch were.

### 2.1 Why fragmented fact cards are hard to keep

1. **Memory keeps meaning and connections, not items.** In Bransford and Johnson's classic study (1972, *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*), a passage of clear sentences was hard to understand and recall until readers were told beforehand what it was about [lit.]. A card of fields gives no "what it is about" before the facts, so each fact has nothing to attach to.
2. **Causal links decide what is remembered.** In narrative comprehension, events with more causal connections to the rest of a story are recalled more often and rated more important (Trabasso and van den Broek 1985, *Journal of Memory and Language*; van den Broek 1988) [checked: existence and title; lit. for detail]. A card that lists the committee, the composers and the veto without "because" or "so" removes the connections that would make them memorable.
3. **A list is the weakest structure.** Meyer and Freedle (1984, *American Educational Research Journal*) gave listeners the same content organised as a "collection" (a list) or as comparison, causation or problem and solution; the list version was recalled least [checked: existence; lit. for detail]. Readers who use a text's top-level structure recall more (Meyer, Brandt and Bluth 1980, *Reading Research Quarterly*) [lit.]. The short card is a collection structure by design.
4. **Gaps a novice cannot fill.** When texts leave inferences to the reader, low-knowledge readers suffer most; rewriting a text to supply the missing links improved recall (Britton and Gülgöz 1991, *Journal of Educational Psychology*) [checked: existence; lit. for detail], and revising history textbook passages for causal coherence improved comprehension (Beck, McKeown, Sinatra and Loxterman 1991, *Reading Research Quarterly*) [lit.]. McNamara, Kintsch, Songer and Kintsch (1996, *Cognition and Instruction*) found low-knowledge readers gained from a more coherent text while high-knowledge readers sometimes did better with a less explicit one [lit.]. This is the key to the cards: **a terse card serves an expert who already holds the links (the program's reference form) and fails a learner meeting the work for the first time.** The "expertise reversal effect" names the same pattern in instructional design (Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler and Sweller 2003, *Educational Psychologist*) [lit.].
5. **Every unknown name is a new element in working memory.** Working memory holds only a few new items at once (Cowan 2001, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*: about four chunks) [lit.]; cognitive load theory treats each unfamiliar, interacting element as load (Sweller 1988, *Cognitive Science*) [lit.]. Twenty-five unexplained names in 193 words is far beyond what a reader can hold while also building the plot.
6. **Phone reading makes it worse.** A meta-analysis found comprehension lower on screens than on paper, with the gap larger for informational text and under time pressure (Delgado, Vargas, Ackerman and Salmerón 2018, *Educational Research Review*) [lit.]. Our readers are on phones, reading informational text, in twenty minutes.

### 2.3 What museum interpretation adds

- **Tilden (1957), *Interpreting Our Heritage*.** "Information, as such, is not Interpretation." Interpretation is revelation based upon information; its chief aim is provocation, not instruction; it relates the subject to the visitor's own experience; it presents a whole rather than a part [lit.; quotation from memory, check before use]. The short cards are information in Tilden's sense. The design-lesson reading of *The Firebird* (section 4.3) is the "relates to the visitor's experience" principle for a designer.
- **Ham (1992; 2013), *Environmental Interpretation* and *Interpretation: Making a Difference on Purpose*.** A **topic** is a subject ("*The Firebird*"); a **theme** is a whole sentence about it ("A committee designed Russia for a Paris audience, and gave it a rule anyone could read"). Ham's TORE model: thematic, organised, relevant, enjoyable [lit.]. Every card needs a theme sentence, not a topic.
- **Serrell (1996; 2nd ed. 2015), *Exhibit Labels*.** The **big idea**: one sentence with a subject, an action and a consequence, which also says what to leave out [lit.]. The voice guide already uses Serrell's competence test. The shoe page has its big idea ("The dancer is on the poster... the maker is a symbol on a sole") but places it seventh. Serrell's *Paying Attention* (1998) timed visitors across many exhibitions and found most spend under twenty minutes regardless of size, so the big idea must arrive early [lit.].
- **Bitgood's attention-value model** (Bitgood 2010; *Attention and Value*, 2013): visitors weigh the value they expect against the cost in effort; reading rises as word counts fall and when the text answers a question they already have [lit.]. A card that front-loads twenty-five names reads as high cost and low value.
- **The Getty's *Quick Guide to Adult Audience Interpretive Materials*:** "begin by identifying concrete visual details to assist viewers in guided looking" (quoted in the voice guide §2) [checked in the voice-guide pass]. **The Smithsonian's *Guide to Interpretive Writing for Exhibitions*:** "Refer directly to something the visitor can see"; sentences of 25 words or fewer (voice guide Table B) [checked in that pass]. **The V&A's gallery text guidelines:** explain what is puzzling in the object (voice guide Table A). McManus (1989, *Curator*) found visitors do read labels and repeat their words in conversation, which is an argument for one quotable sentence per label [lit.].

## 3. Better forms

**The general fix first.** Keep the short-card fields: they are a good schema and the right **reference form** (the structure's "revisiting as reference, 2–5 min"). Stop presenting them as teaching text. **Walk: "Designing a country for strangers"** (seven steps, 60 to 120 words each, one "you" prompt per step)

> **1. The brief.** In 1910 Serge Diaghilev, a Russian impresario, was presenting a season of ballet in Paris. He needed a new work that would read as Russian to a Paris audience, and he commissioned music to fit a slot in that season. The Mariinsky Theatre's own programme now calls the result "an export Russian fairy tale". The brief, in other words, named its audience before it named its story. *Before the next step, write the audience your own current project is really for, in five words.* [W2 §0.1 point 2; §2.1; §2.6]

> **2. The sources.** The friends drew on Alexander Afanasyev's collection of Russian folk tales, published 1855–63, chiefly "Koshchei the Deathless" and the tale of Ivan Tsarevich, the Firebird and the Gray Wolf. Benois recalled that the poet Pyotr Potemkin proposed the subject, citing a poem by Yakov Polonsky in which "Firebirds sing by night". The music looked back to Nikolai Rimsky-Korsakov, Stravinsky's teacher. Golovin brought the look of the Russian craft revival he had worked in. *Notice which source supplied the plot, and which only the mood.* [W2 §2.2; tales and poem probable; Golovin's craft background documented. **Not found:** what Abramtsevo and Talashkino were, beyond "the neo-Russian craft revival", so they are not named here.]

> **3. The idea.** No single person had it. Over the winter of 1908–09 a group met, informally led by Fokine, to turn the sources into a scenario: a prince, hunting, captures and frees a magic bird, who gives him a feather; with it he breaks a sorcerer's spell and frees thirteen princesses. Most accounts say the princesses, and the sorcerer's soul hidden in an egg, were the group's additions. Programmes still credit the story to Fokine alone. *Look at the credit line on the 1910 programme: whose names are missing?* [W2 §2.1; §2.3 and §2.5; committee probable; credit documented. The programme image is held by the Library of Congress, W2 §2.8; its licence is not checked.]

> **4. The world rules.** The scenario split the cast into two worlds, and Fokine gave each its own way of moving. The Firebird dances classical steps on pointe, the vocabulary of the supernatural. The princesses dance folk round-dances. The sorcerer's monsters move in angular character dance. The music probably follows: strange, shifting harmony for magic and plain folk tunes for people. We read this as the ballet's core rule, and later works copied it: the audience can tell who belongs to which world from movement alone. *Write one rule for your own world that a stranger could read without a caption.* [W2 §2.4, argued and probable as marked; §2.10]

> **5. Execution.** The team changed under the work. Nikolai Tcherepnin withdrew from the score; Anatoly Liadov did not deliver, and whether he declined or failed is not known. Stravinsky, 27, took the commission in December 1909 and finished by May 1910, writing to Fokine's dictation. Diaghilev also overruled his designer: Golovin's costumes for the Firebird, the Tsarevna and Ivan were replaced by Léon Bakst's. Golovin kept the garden and the festival dress. *Compare the bird in Bakst's design with Golovin's world around it: does it look like it belongs to another place?* [W2 §2.3, documented; §0.1 point 3. **Not found:** an open image of Golovin's rejected bird design, so the comparison uses Bakst's bird against Golovin's set.]

> **6. Launch.** *The Firebird* opened at the Paris Opéra on 25 June 1910, conducted by Gabriel Pierné, with Tamara Karsavina as the bird and Fokine as Ivan. It succeeded at once. The principal ballerina first asked to dance it had refused the role. It was made for Paris, not St Petersburg: Russia first saw it in 1921, in Fedor Lopukhov's version, and the Mariinsky staged Fokine's version only in 1994. *Where would your own world be launched first, and who would see it last?* [W2 §2.5–§2.7; Pavlova's refusal probable, so the step says only "the principal ballerina", as Britannica does]

> **7. Afterlife.** The music travelled without the dance, in concert suites of 1911, 1919 and 1945. In 1926 Natalia Goncharova re-designed the ballet, partly by altering the 1910 costumes with new trims. Marc Chagall designed Adolph Bolm's 1945 version, and George Balanchine reused those designs in 1949, saying of the tale, "You can't make a ballet out of it." Each version kept the bird and re-dressed the rest. *Say back, in one sentence, the rule that made this world readable. It is saved to your record.* [W2 §2.7, documented; "kept the bird" is our reading]

### 4.2 The pointe-shoe object study: the opening as a walk

Title: **"The idea came before the shoe."** Through-question: *Which came first, the wish to float or the shoe that lets a dancer stand on her toes?* Five opening steps; the build, the mark and the family follow as in §3.2.

> **1. A shoe with no hard toe.** A black satin slipper, about 1842, sits in the V&A. Marie Taglioni wore it and inscribed it to Lady Selina Bidwell. It has a leather sole and no block. The only stiffening is darning under the toes and about 1.5 cm up the sides. Whatever Taglioni did on her toes, she did on strength and thread. *Open the V&A's photograph and find the darning: that is all the support there is.* [scope 02 §1.3 and log, V&A S.2746-1986, documented; image non-commercial, so link out. **Not found:** who made the shoe.]

> **2. The idea came first.** On 12 March 1832, at the Paris Opéra, Taglioni danced *La Sylphide*, a ballet about an air spirit, in darned, leather-soled satin slippers. We read what she did as giving standing on the toes a meaning: lightness, a creature of air. She was not the earliest. Amalia Brugnoli is described rising onto her toes in Vienna in 1822 or 1823, and earlier accounts include men. Say "earliest known", never "first". *Notice that the meaning arrived before any equipment for it.* [pass2_body §1.1, premiere documented, slippers probable; scope 02 §1.3 and traps, Brugnoli contested, men per Hammond and Fairfax]

> **3. The technology followed.** About fifty years later, Italian shoemakers built the toe into a hard box of newspaper, flour paste and pasteboard, probably between the 1870s and the 1890s. In the early 1900s Anna Pavlova's shoes probably added a hardened box and an internal leather sole. The shoe was redesigned to serve an idea that already existed. *What in your own field is still being done on strength, before the tool for it exists?* [pass2_body §1.1, probable]

> **4. What it is made of now.** At Freed of London, which supplies the Royal Ballet, there is no wood in the block. It is layers of hessian, a coarse jute cloth, and paper, each brushed with a flour-and-water paste mixed once a week, inside a satin upper. The stiffener under the arch is the shank, of leather or layered card. The dancer balances on the platform, the flat front of the block. *Find each part on the drawing before you see how they go together.* [the-shoe.md "How it is built"; pass2_body §1.1 table; Freed's "no wood" via Spitalfields Life 2013]

> **5. How long it lasts.** Nothing in the shoe is permanent: sweat softens flour paste. A pair lasts about ten to twenty hours of dancing, by the usual estimate, and a principal can finish a pair in one performance. The Royal Ballet says it uses more than six thousand pairs a year at about fifty pounds a pair, a figure it publishes to raise money. *Keep that number in mind; the next steps ask who made all those pairs, and who wrote down their names.* [the-shoe.md; company figure as told]

### 4.3 *The Firebird* as a design lesson

> Read *The Firebird* as a design job and every stage of one is there. **The brief.** Diaghilev, a Russian impresario, needed a new work for his 1910 Paris season. It had to read as Russian to a Paris audience. **The sources.** Two folk tales from Afanasyev's collection, a poem a friend proposed, and the music of Stravinsky's teacher, Rimsky-Korsakov. The designer, Golovin, added the look of a Russian craft revival. **The idea.** Over a winter, a committee of friends merged those sources into one story. A prince frees thirteen captive princesses with a magic bird's feather. **The world rules.** Magic dances on pointe, people dance folk rounds, and monsters move in sharp angles. The music probably splits the same way, so an audience can read the world without a programme. **The execution.** Two composers dropped out before Stravinsky, aged 27, took the job. The choreographer dictated the music to him, scene by scene. The producer also overruled his designer and replaced the lead costumes. **The launch.** It opened at the Paris Opéra on 25 June 1910. Russia did not see it until 1921. **The afterlife.** Concert suites carried the music without the dance. New designers re-dressed it in 1926 and 1945, and each kept the bird. Three moves transfer to any world you build. Name the audience in the brief. Give the world a rule a stranger can read. Write a credit line that records the committee, not only the name on the poster.

## 5. Recommendations, in order

1. **Re-label the short cards as records.** Keep §3.2's ten fields as the reference form and the database. Do not show them as the learner's first meeting with a work.
2. **Write tiers from the records.** A Line and a Label for every register work, written as sentences that open on something visible and end on the gap (voice guide §3). For each work in a teaching path, add a Story or a walk of five to seven steps in design-job order (brief, sources, idea, rules, execution, launch, afterlife). Section 4.1 is the template.
3. **One theme sentence and one through-question per card, walk and page**, visible on the first screen (Ham's theme; Serrell's big idea).
4. **A causal-link pass on every card:** each field gains a "because" or a "so that", and facts the research holds in different sections are joined (Pillet's hurry and the reused set; Dolin's acrobatics and Cocteau's idea; Diaghilev's export brief and the 1921 Russian premiere).
5. **A name budget:** no more than three new names per screen, each with a role on first mention.
6. **Rebuild the shoe page as the object study it was meant to become:** open on the idea (section 4.2), split the build into drawn steps with labels on the image, draw the marks, keep Ray, and move the company figures into a practised sort placed after the explanation (the board's own open item).
7. **Teach in pairs before walls:** *Firebird* with *Train Bleu*; *Giselle* with *The Firebird* for "two worlds".
8. **Retrieval without quizzes:** a say-back sentence at the end of each walk, saved and never scored; Apply tasks that fill a second record from given sources; spaced returns through the returning work and the threads.
9. **Move attribution into structure:** tombstone captions, a source line per step, and the claims sheet, so sentences stay short and still honest.
