# Ballet scope 01: history and institutions

Angle: ballet from the Italian and French courts to the present, with institutions (academies, state patronage, schools, syllabi, competitions, touring, opera houses) treated as makers of the art, and the global and colonial story of who exported ballet, who adapted it and who was kept out.

Written 21 October 2026 for F1 "Histories of making". The learner is a foundation-year design student, not a dancer. This file maps what can be known; it is not a page.

**Status key.** **[D]** documented: seen at source during this pass (the source and URL are in the working register at the end). **[P]** probable: from reliable secondary sources or institutional pages, not checked at the primary. **[C]** contested: sources disagree, and both readings are given. **[A]** argued: a scholar's interpretation, with the scholar named. **OA** = open access. The working register at the end holds the evidence, with URLs only where a result showed them.

**The thesis this angle offers the unit.** Ballet is made by institutions as much as by choreographers. A royal academy fixed its vocabulary, schools turned foundlings and peasants' children into a corps, and each state paid for it for its own reasons. Each route by which ballet left Europe (Soviet teachers, a British syllabus, Russian émigrés, a touring star, a Gulf opera house) made a different ballet at the other end. The institutions wrote the histories, and they kept the names of their founders and leading dancers while the pupils, teachers' wives, rehearsal pianists, tailors and the dancers they excluded mostly went unrecorded.

## 2. The concepts

1. **Codification as institutional making.** The 1661 academy and Beauchamp–Feuillet notation fixed a vocabulary. Anna Leon (*Corpus*) reads the Académie as "a project of control over dance" [A]. Foster, as summarised in an OA transcript-verlag chapter, calls Feuillet's homogenising lexicon "a quasi-colonialist project of cultural dominance" (*Choreographing Empathy*, 2011, pp. 101–4) [A]. Against a pure power reading, Sandra Noll and Philip Hammond's Weberian article ("The Internal Logic of Dance", *Journal of Social History* 12.4, 1979) treats technique as developing by its own rationalising logic toward autonomy [A]. Sandra Noll Hammond applies the same idea to pointe: it was "a logical development of a dance technique that had long emphasized lightness and elevation" (Stanford page) [D].
2. **Authorship of a ballet.** Whose is a dance: the king's who ordered it, the master's who invented the signs, or the publisher's who printed them? The 1704 ruling split the answer (Glon) [D at secondary]. The same question applies to "Petipa's" ballets, which survive in Stepanov notation made by others and were restaged by Sergeyev, and to any reconstruction (Fullington, Ratmansky). Feuillet's privilege protected the signs themselves, so the notation "protects the authorship of choreography, and itself" (transcript-verlag OA chapter) [A].
3. **The ephemeral and the record.** Homans: "Ballet has no texts and no standardized notation, no scripts or scores" (Introduction, p. xix) [A]. Nicifero (Project MUSE review, 2012) answers with Ebreo's treatise, Feuillet, Stepanov and Laban [A]. The Sergeyev Collection and the Paris Opéra's 1805 rule requiring designers to deposit their maquettes [D] show that institutions do make records, but of a selective kind: designs and steps survive, while the making of costumes and the pay rarely do.
4. **State patronage against artistic autonomy.** Ezrahi's "artistic repossession": artists used Soviet "organizational structures and ideological frames" for their own ends; "ballet proved stronger than politics and ideology" (p. 232) [A]. Geraldine Morris (*Dance Research*, 2013) counters that isolation kept Soviet choreography conservative [A]. Tomé shows Cuban dancers made into model workers who cut sugarcane, embodying the New Man and New Woman [A]. Wilcox argues that Soviet-style revolutionary ballets are *not* the main socialist legacy in China; Chinese dance is [A].
5. **Reading politics into works.** Kirstein, Kennan, Janet Kennedy, Sally Banes and Helena Hammond read *The Sleeping Beauty* (1890) as Franco-Russian rapprochement and Louis XIV ceremonial. Scholl (2004) calls this "one of the more evergreen of myths", since the alliance was signed only in 1894. Mahiet (2016) reopens the question through Wortman's imperial "scenario of power" [C]. This is a ready-made two-reading dispute for a seminar.
6. **Transfer, adaptation, localisation.** There are five export models, each a different institution:
   - Soviet teachers sent under cultural agreements: Beijing 1954, Cairo 1958 [D at secondary].
   - A British syllabus examined by touring examiners [D], plus de Valois's teachers in Tehran [P].
   - Russian émigrés teaching where they landed: Harbin, Shanghai, Kamakura [D at secondary].
   - A touring star: Pavlova in Mexico, as Reynoso argues, helped Mexican elites perform a "mestizo modernity" that was at once cosmopolitan and Mexican [A].
   - A presenting house buying tours: the Gulf, which Hennessey calls "sites of cultural importation and consumption" [A].

   Tomé's "Music in the Blood" (2013) asks whether a "Cuban" style is national character or Alonso's training passed on [A].
7. **Orientalism on the ballet stage.** Fisher ("'Arabian Coffee' in the Land of the Sweets", *DRJ* 2003–04) grants the Orientalist heritage but sees the divertissement as possible "intercultural dance sharing" (the *DRJ* editors' summary of her argument) [A]. Critics (Gendron, SIA thesis, OA) say she ignores the direction of borrowing [A]. Final Bow for Yellowface (2017) and the Ndiaye–Rivière report (proscribe blackface, yellowface and brownface) are institutional responses [D]. One irony for the course: the earliest full-length ballet staged in Cairo was the Orientalist *Fountain of Bakhchisarai* (1966) [D at secondary].
8. **Whiteness and the *ballet blanc*.** Gottschild coined "invisibilized" for the Africanist presence in American ballet [D]. She is quoted as calling ballet "the last bastion of white supremacy in concert dance" (via Fisher 2004) [D at secondary]. Fisher (2014, via Lee) writes that ballet "is very white" from the seventeenth century on [D at secondary]. Casey reads DTH's *Creole Giselle* as an encounter with "ballet's iconic whiteness" through hybridity [A]. The Paris report's recommendation 7, "Cesser de blanchir les peaux" (stop whitening skins), turns an aesthetic into an institutional rule [D].
9. **Decline and revival.** Homans's epilogue says "ballet is dying" [A]. Rogoff and Horwell answer that ballet has always alternated rigidity and revival [A]. Teach this as a historiographical habit, the elegy written by a former insider.
10. **"Ballet is a woman."** Kant titles her Romantic section with this phrase. It names the feminisation of the art after 1830 and, with it, a labour force of women paid and pensioned by men's institutions [A/P].
11. **The canon map as an artefact.** Homans's two "epicentres" (France, Russia; Nicifero) and the usual line Paris → St Petersburg → Paris → New York are an editorial choice [A]. Japan's ballet came through Harbin and Shanghai, and Egypt's through Moiseyev's folk-dance plan and Bolshoi teachers [D at secondary]. That directly illustrates the course threshold concept of "a web with obscured strands".

## 5. How it is taught elsewhere

- **University of Victoria, "Cultural History of Ballet" (Kat Sark).** A history-department survey that reads Homans chapter by chapter, one or two chapters a week, from the courts to the Epilogue. It adds one national case, Neufeld's *Passion to Dance: The National Ballet of Canada* (2011), with film clips each week. The bibliography lists most of the scholars this brief assesses (Garafola, Scholl, Foster, Franko, Guest, Kant) plus Prevots, *Dance for Export* (1998), Stoneley, *A Queer History of the Ballet* (2006) and Chazin-Bennahum, *The Lure of Perfection* (2005) [D]. *Lesson:* Homans works as a spine for non-dancers, but she is the whole map. The rest of the world appears only as the teacher's own country.
- **"THEA 166, Ballet: A History"** (copy on docslib; institution not shown in the excerpt). Homans is the required text, plus "two additional readings on ballet in Cuba and China" [D]. *Lesson:* the global story is an add-on of two readings. The course should invert that ratio.
- **University of Nicosia, DANC-251 "Historical Perspectives in Dance I".** Organised by issues, not by nations:
  - cross-casting at Louis XIV's court;
  - *Giselle* and *Creole Giselle*;
  - gender in fairy-tale ballets;
  - *Swan Lake* revisited by Matthew Bourne and Dada Masilo;
  - *Rite of Spring* reconstructions;
  - Balanchine "the Neoclassical and the Jazz";
  - St Denis, "cultural imperialism and appropriation".

  Texts are Albright & Dils (eds), *Moving History/Dancing Cultures* (Wesleyan 2001) and Albright, *Engaging Bodies* (2013) [D]. *Lesson:* this is the closest model to a web of works and remakings, and the best fit for F1.
- **Accademia Teatro alla Scala, teacher diploma, "Comparative analysis of historical methods and styles".** Students read the syllabi themselves: Beaumont–Idzikowski (Cecchetti), Bournonville's *Études*, Kostrovitskaya & Pisarev and Vaganova, Schorer on Balanchine technique, and Pappacena on Blasis [D]. *Lesson:* syllabi are primary sources that can be read as design documents.
- **Textbooks in common use:** Homans (2010); Kant (ed.) (2007); Hammond, *Ballet Basics* (5th ed. 2004), which ends with a short history chapter [D]; Au, *Ballet and Modern Dance* (Thames & Hudson) [P].

## 6. Gaps and traps

1. **"First" is everywhere and is usually an institution's own claim.**
   - The *Ballet comique* is "the first ballet" (Britannica, Wikipedia).
   - Freed and Ballet Black's brown shoes are "the first skin tone pointe shoes".
   - Gebara's "first professional dance company" in Lebanon comes from her own ITI bio.
   - Varna is the "oldest" competition (the competitions' own sites).
   - The Bolshoi Academy fixes its founding on a contract date.

   Write "earliest known", and label self-accounts as such.
2. **Founding dates conflict; show both** [C]:
   - RAD Royal Charter: 1935 (granted) or 1936 (signed 17 Jan 1936). Its original name is also given two ways.
   - Borovansky Ballet: 1940 or 1943.
   - Pavlova sisters' studio: 1919 arrival vs "1920s".
   - Tokyo *Swan Lake*: 5 or 9 Aug 1946.
   - Cairo *Bakhchisarai* (1966): staged by Lavrovsky or by Pavlov.
   - Vaganova's lifetime editions: three or four.
   - Tomé's *Cuban Studies* article: 2018 or 2019.
   - The Bolshoi's "founding": 1773 (school), 1776 (Urusov–Maddox company) or the building.
   - *Red Detachment*: 26 Sep or 1 Oct 1964 (pass 1).
3. **Institutions write their own histories.** The sources for the Bolshoi and Vaganova academies, the RAD, the Australian Ballet, Les Ballets Persans, Freed and the Cairo Opera are all their own pages. They name founders and patrons and leave out pupils, wives, accompanists and workshops.
4. **The canon map misleads.**
   - The standard line Paris → St Petersburg → Paris → New York, with "spread" afterwards, hides Harbin, Shanghai and Kamakura, and hides the fact that Egypt's ballet school grew out of a Soviet folk-dance plan (Moiseyev 1957).
   - Wilcox shows that in China ballet was not the main socialist dance legacy.
   - Homans and the syllabi built on her treat the world beyond France and Russia as appendices.
5. **The Soviet export is under-studied in English.** The best evidence on Cairo is in Russian (Barsova) and press features. Arabic, Chinese and Japanese sources were not searched in depth this pass. Specialist readers are needed.
6. **The Gulf has almost no scholarly base for ballet.** What exists is about opera houses (Petrocelli, Hennessey) plus state statutes and journalism. In this pass no resident professional ballet company was found in the Gulf states; scope the claim to "none found". Petrocelli advises the Royal Opera House Muscat, so declare that interest when citing him.
7. **Race: avoid repeating the myth when quoting it.** The 1963 *NYT* "anatomically" claim (John Martin) and the "corporealities" anecdote (Gottschild) must be framed as what was said and refuted. Fisher's reading of Orientalism is contested and should not be taught as settled.
8. **Disputed formulations.** Homans's "no texts" claim; Ezrahi's "slaves" (Tomoff calls it "absurd"); the political reading of *Sleeping Beauty*. Teach each as two readings.
9. **Missing from the record.**
   - Wages and contracts of the corps across institutions (one piece-rate contract is the only number found this pass).
   - Women teachers who carried syllabi abroad.
   - Local dancers in touring companies.
   - Who paid for Gulf tours and at what price.
   - Russian archives, which are now hard to reach.
   - Paris Opéra costumes, sold off.
10. **Licence traps.** Gallica is free for non-commercial use, not open. Internet Archive lending is not open access. NYPL items need a per-item check. CC BY-NC-ND (Wilcox) forbids adapting the text. Most company and press photographs are ©.
11. **Planetary Weight is thin in this angle.** It shows in the touring economy (Pavlova's company sleeping on ship decks between ports, per Dallal; ocean liners and railways) and in opera houses paid for by oil and gas revenue (Winkelsesser contrasts Oman with its richer neighbours). No source in this pass counts the carbon or materials of touring. That is a gap to name, not fill.

## 7. Size

- **Hours.** This angle deserves about **6–8 learner hours** in a foundation design course: two 90-minute sessions, about 3 hours of reading (one Homans chapter, one OA article, one primary source), and a 1-hour written task. More would turn it into a dance-history course. Less would leave the institutions as background to the shoe and the tutu, which repeats the canon's own habit.
- **One page (about 20 minutes, inside the existing four-page ballet unit): "The school is a machine."** Four dated founding acts, each with who paid and who was enrolled:
  - St Petersburg, 1738: 12 girls and 12 boys by decree.
  - Moscow, 1773: 54 foundlings, the teacher paid per finished dancer.
  - Leningrad, 1934: a syllabus printed for every school in the Union.
  - Cairo, 1958: 35 children from a newspaper advertisement, one a chauffeur's son.

  End on the list of who is not named. This carries tier 1.3–1.4 and the Attribution thread in one short span list.
- **One session (90 min): "Who owns a dance?"** Use the Beauchamp v. Feuillet ruling of 1704 next to the Sergeyev notations and a 2018 brown shoe. Students argue authorship, then exclusion, with two sourced readings each. Seminar texts: Glon (or an English summary), Mahiet (2016) on the *Sleeping Beauty* dispute, and recommendation 7 of the Ndiaye–Rivière report.
- **One unit (four short pages plus a seminar):**
  1. *The court and the academy*: power and codification.
  2. *The school*: bodies made by institutions.
  3. *Five routes out*: Soviet teachers, the British syllabus, émigrés, the touring star, the presenting house, each with one city and one object (a Cairo programme, a Pavlova scrapbook, a 1946 Tokyo programme, an RAD syllabus, a Muscat season).
  4. *Who was kept out*: from *ballet blanc* to brown shoes.

  Total about 8 hours.
- **Thread fit.**
  - **Attribution and Authority:** very strong (the lawsuit, notation, deposit rules, institutional self-histories).
  - **Writing and Argument:** strong (four ready disputes).
  - **Accessibility:** strong (who could enrol, who could buy the shoe; many OA sources).
  - **Planetary Weight:** weak (touring and oil-funded venues only, unquantified).

- Japan (Japanese names in Japanese order). Eliana Pavlova (1897–1941) and sister Nadezhda: Kamakura City Kawakita Film Museum says she "arrived in Japan in 1919" and set up the country's earliest ballet studio in Kamakura (Shichirigahama); "mother of Japanese ballet" [D museum page; studio date still unfixed: pass 1 had "1920s"; **contested**]. Kawashima Kyōko, *Nihon baree no haha Eriana Pavurowa* (book) and KAKEN project 26370117 (Waseda, 2014–17) on the Tokyo Ballet Troupe (東京バレエ團, est. 1946): Kawashima Kyōko, "Nihon baree dai-ni no tanjō 'Tōkyō Baree-dan' to sono jōen sakuhin", *Engeki eizō* 56 (2015). First complete *Swan Lake* in Japan: Imperial Theatre, opened 9 Aug 1946 (Tōsei Gakuen Shōwa Music Univ. ballet archive programme record) — Kimura Misaki (2025 conference abstract, Japan Society for Dance Research) gives 5 Aug 1946, run extended from 25 to 30 days → **date contested (5 vs 9 Aug)**. Organiser Shimada Hiroshi; choreography Komaki Masahide (1911–2006), trained in Harbin with Elizaveta Vasilevna Kvyatkovskaya (Japanese article, J-STAGE *Buyōgaku* 41, 2018, from Bureau of Russian Émigrés archives in Manchukuo), danced with the Shanghai Ballets Russes and brought "score to wigs" from Shanghai; Kawashima argues the staging was closer to the Shanghai Ballets Russes (Petipa–Ivanov) than to Gorsky. Olga Sapphire (1907–1981), Leningrad-trained, taught at the Nichigeki revue theatre from 1936 (Inada Naomi's assessment). Ono Yukie, *Yakeato no "Hakuchō no mizu"* (Bungei Shunjū, 2013). [D] → The Japanese route ran through Russian émigrés, Harbin and Shanghai, not through Paris or London: a strong "obscured strand". Online: Shōwa Music University Ballet Archive (programmes database) https://ballet-archive.tosei-showa-music.ac.jp/ ; RAD exams in Japan from the 1970s.
- South Africa: UCT Ballet School founded 1934 by Dulcie Howes (ex-Pavlova company) at the invitation of Prof. W. H. Bell, Dean of the South African College of Music. Under apartheid, students of all races could study but "coloured dancers had to find work overseas"; some sought racial reclassification; "Ballet in South Africa benefited from an apartheid government" (B. Valentine, UCT thesis 2017, **OA OpenUCT**). Gerard Samuel (UCT School of Dance director; first-hand: Playhouse Dance Company, Durban, 1994, with Boyzie Cekwana) writes on "Dancing the Other in South Africa"; UCT dance archive project (2009). "The Voice of South African Ballet in History" (ResearchGate, 2023; author not captured). State companies CAPAB (Cape) and PACT (Transvaal), founded 1963 [P]. Eoan Group (coloured community arts, Cape Town) [P]. [D for UCT theses]

- Moscow: decree of Catherine II and Betskoi's Moscow Foundling Home (Vospitatelny Dom): dance classes for foundlings; Filippo Beccari engaged 23 Dec 1773 (the Bolshoi Academy's own founding date), 26 girls and 28 boys, four mornings a week 7–11; Beccari and his wife paid not a salary but per trained dancer: 250 roubles a soloist, 150 a figurant (Wikipedia citing Russian sources) [P]; first graduates 1779; from 1784 the Home's pupils (actors, dancers, musicians) taken onto Maddox's payroll at the Petrovsky Theatre (later Bolshoi) [P: Betskoy Foundation page citing Vsevolodsky 1913]. Leonova (CyberLeninka, OA) describes the Academy's archive of 18th–19th-c. documents being prepared as a facsimile edition [D]. Bolshoi Academy history page [D] https://en.balletacademy.ru/academy/history-and-mission/ → foundlings and piece-rate pay: the corps as a manufactured product, an unnamed-labour strand. St Petersburg: 4 May 1738 decree, Landé, 12 girls and 12 boys (pass 1, documented at vaganovaacademy.ru). Serf ballet troupes on noble estates, some bought into the Imperial theatres (e.g. Stolypin's troupe, 1806) [P, not checked this pass].
