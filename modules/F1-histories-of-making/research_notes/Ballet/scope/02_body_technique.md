# Scope 02: the body and technique as making

Angle: the dancer's body as a made thing; technique as a codified, transmitted system; pointe as a technology of the body; the record of technique (manuals, notation, film).

Evidence marks: **[D]** documented (seen at source in this pass), **[P]** probable (secondary, not checked at source), **[C]** contested, **[A]** argued (an interpretive position, attributed).

Compiled 21 October 2026 from web searches and fetched pages on that date. Builds on `../pass2_body.md` (pointe-shoe construction, Freed makers, injury and workload figures) and does not repeat its tables; where a fact is already there, this file points to it. The working log at the end records each source as it was seen.

**One-line thesis for this angle.** A ballet dancer is a made object: a child's body worked for ten years against a written standard (five positions, turnout, a French vocabulary), equipped with a shoe that only works because the body has been rebuilt to use it, and recorded, imperfectly, in manuals, notations and film. The technique has named authors; the bodies it was made from mostly do not.

## 1. The knowledge map

### Tier 1: must know (cannot be skipped)

**1.1 Technique makes the body.** What: ballet training as years of daily class (barre, centre, adagio, allegro) that reshape bones, muscles and habits of a child; the dancer's body as the product of a process, not a given. Why it matters for making: it is the clearest case in the course of a *person* as the made thing, with a workshop (the studio), tools (barre, mirror, shoe), a specification (the ideal body) and quality control (selection, examination). Sources:
- Foster, Susan Leigh. "Dancing Bodies", in Crary & Kwinter (eds), *Incorporations*, Zone 1992, pp. 480–495; reprinted in Desmond (ed.), *Meaning in Motion*, Duke 1997. Key line: "Training thus creates two bodies: one, perceived and tangible; the other, aesthetically ideal." [D] A course-hosted PDF exists on a UCLA site; it is a teaching copy, not an open licence.
- Mauss, Marcel. "Les techniques du corps", lecture 17 May 1934, *Journal de Psychologie* 32(3–4), 1936; trans. Ben Brewster, "Techniques of the body", *Economy and Society* 2(1), 1973, 70–88; reprinted in the same *Incorporations* volume, pp. 455–477. [D, via Monoskop bibliography] The founding text for "the body is man's first and most natural instrument" [P, quotation from memory, check before use].
- Wainwright, Steven & Bryan Turner. "'Just crumbling to bits'? An exploration of the body, ageing, injury and career in classical ballet dancers", *Sociology* 40(2), 2006, 237–255, doi 10.1177/0038038506062031. Royal Ballet ex-dancers; Bourdieu's habitus and "physical capital". [D] Not OA.
- Aalten, Anna. "In the Presence of the Body: Theorizing Training, Injuries and Pain in Ballet", *Dance Research Journal* 37(2), Winter 2005 (UvA repository dates it 2006). [D] UvA-DARE record exists; OA status not shown.

**1.2 Codification: the five positions, turnout and a written vocabulary.** What: the reduction of court dancing to named positions and steps under Louis XIV's Académie royale de danse (founded 1661 [P]); Pierre Beauchamp(s) commissioned to devise a notation; Feuillet's *Chorégraphie* (1700) "notated the five positions of the feet" [D, LoC essay]; Rameau's *Le Maître à danser* (1725) [P]; Blasis's *Traité élémentaire* (Milan 1820) as the earliest known manual of theatrical (not social) technique in this line [P for the "earliest" claim; the 1820 edition D]. French terms as a shared language that survives in every studio from Havana to Tokyo. Why: a codified vocabulary is a design system: a standard set of parts, a notation, and an institution that polices it. Students can compare it with type specimens or building orders. Sources:
- Feuillet, *Chorégraphie, ou L'art de décrire la dance* (Paris 1700; LoC holds the 1713 Dezais edition). LoC: "not aware of any U.S. copyright or any other restrictions". [D, OA]
- Blasis, Carlo. *Traité élémentaire, théorique et pratique de l'art de la danse*, Milan: Beati & Tenenti, 1820; digitised by the University of Utah Marriott Library, rights statement NoC-US. [D, OA]
- Franko, Mark. *Dance as Text: Ideologies of the Baroque Body*, Cambridge UP 1993 [P]; 2nd edn OUP 2015 [D]. French court ballet c. 1573–1670 and the politics of the codified body.
- LoC essay "Baroque dance" in *Dance Instruction Manuals ca. 1490–1920* (An American Ballroom Companion). [D, OA] A good first reading for students.

**1.3 Pointe as a technology of the body.** What: rising onto the tips of the toes as a technique that came before the hardware. The earliest known accounts include men; Taglioni's surviving shoe has no block. Why: the clearest case in the unit of a body technique and an object changing each other over time, and of a "first" that keeps moving. Sources:
- Hammond, Sandra Noll. "Searching for the Sylph: Documentation of Early Developments in Pointe Technique", *Dance Research Journal* 19(2), Winter 1987–88, 27–32, doi 10.2307/1478168. Uses late-18th- and early-19th-century treatises; argues early pointe work "was not exclusively female" and that its earliest practitioner was not a 19th-century ballerina (abstract, paraphrased). [D] Not OA.
- V&A S.2746-1986: shoe worn by Marie Taglioni, c. 1842, black satin, leather sole, "unblocked", stiffened only by overdarning under the toes and about 1.5 cm up the sides. [D] Image terms are non-commercial only.
- Weiss, Rist & Grossman, IADMS resource paper "Guidelines for Initiating Pointe Training" (© IADMS 2008; journal version *J Dance Med Sci* 2009 [P]): not before age 12; age alone should not decide. [D] Free to reproduce for education with credit.
- Fairfax, Edmund. *The Styles of Eighteenth-Century Ballet*, Scarecrow 2003 [P], with his blog post "Eighteenth-Century Pointe" (2 Jan 2019) gathering 1710–1790s evidence of men "sur l'orteil" (Pitrot in Magri 1779; Sandham's 1721–22 London bill). [P]
- For the shoe's construction, makers and costs: `../pass2_body.md` §1.

**1.4 The body ideal and its policing: weight, race, disability.** What: the selection and maintenance of a narrow body type (thin, long-limbed, light-skinned, non-disabled) through auditions, teaching, costume and kit. Why: this is the Attribution and Accessibility threads made physical: who is allowed to be the material. Sources:
- Arcelus, J., G. Witcomb & A. Mitchell. "Prevalence of eating disorders amongst dancers: a systemic review and meta-analysis", *European Eating Disorders Review* 22(2), 2014, 92–101, doi 10.1002/erv.2271. 33 studies 1966–2013; eating-disorder prevalence 12.0% across dancers, 16.4% in ballet; dancers at "a three times higher risk". [D] Preprint OA (CC BY-NC-ND) at Loughborough.
- Kealiinohomoku, Joann. "An Anthropologist Looks at Ballet as a Form of Ethnic Dance", *Impulse* 1969 or 1970 (sources disagree), reprinted in Copeland & Cohen (eds), *What Is Dance?*, OUP 1983, 533–549. Argues ballet is an ethnic form whose valued body (long, slender) is a cultural choice, not a universal. [D for reprint and argument, via Wikipedia and MIT OCW reading list; P for first-publication year]
- Gottschild, Brenda Dixon. *The Black Dancing Body: A Geography from Coon to Cool*, Palgrave Macmillan 2003 [P]; and "Stripping the Emperor: The Africanist Presence in American Concert Dance" (on Balanchine), set on the Bryn Mawr syllabus [D listing].
- Ndiaye, Pap & Constance Rivière, report on diversity at the Paris Opéra, released week of 8 February 2021: end blackface, brownface and yellowface; rethink the school's "stringent physical criteria". [D via Pointe Magazine; report PDF seen in search results, not opened]
- Freed of London with Ballet Black (Cassa Pancho, Cira Robinson): "Ballet Bronze" and "Ballet Brown" pointe shoes from 22 October 2018; dancers had spent up to two hours "pancaking" pink shoes with foundation. [D, balletblack.co.uk]
- Annandale, Alicia E. *A critical analysis of the representation of persons with disabilities and disfigurement in ballet*, MA (Visual Studies), University of Pretoria, 2023; cases Michaela DePrince, Joe Powell-Main, Freefall Dance Company. [D] OA thesis, licence not stated.

**1.5 What the making costs the body: injury and workload.** What: injury rates, workload and the sports-science turn in company medicine. Why: the human counterpart of the Planetary Weight thread: what is used up in making the product. Sources:
- Mattiussi, A. M. et al. "Injury epidemiology in professional ballet: a five-season prospective study of 1596 medical-attention injuries and 543 time-loss injuries", *BJSM* 2021 (Royal Ballet, 123 dancers, 417,693 hours). Figures and repository link in `../pass2_body.md` §4.3. [D] OA via St Mary's repository.
- Smith, T. O. et al. "Prevalence and profile of musculoskeletal injuries in ballet dancers: a systematic review and meta-analysis", *Physical Therapy in Sport* 19, 2016, 50–56, doi 10.1016/j.ptsp.2015.12.007. 19 studies, 7,332 injuries in 2,617 dancers; hamstring strain, ankle tendinopathy and low-back pain most prevalent; no study of retired dancers. [D] AAM OA (CC BY-NC-ND) at UEA. Do not quote the repository abstract's "280%", which is a typo.
- Kenny, S. J., J. L. Whittaker & C. A. Emery. "Risk factors for musculoskeletal injury in preprofessional dancers: a systematic review", *BJSM* 50(16), 2016, 997. 47 studies; median quality 8/33; only previous injury and poor coping skills reach level-2 evidence. [D]
- Shaw et al. 2022 (workload) and Allen et al. 2012: see `../pass2_body.md` §4.

### Tier 2: should know

**2.1 Rival technique systems and their manuals.** What: national "schools" turned into teachable, examinable systems in the 20th century. Why: technique as a product with brands, examination boards and teacher-certification markets, like competing standards in design. Sources and facts:
- **Cecchetti:** Beaumont, Cyril W. & Stanislas Idzikowski (with Cecchetti), *A Manual of the Theory and Practice of Classical Theatrical Dancing (Méthode Cecchetti)*, London 1922 [D catalogue records]; Cecchetti Society founded London 1922 by Beaumont, merged into the ISTD 1924 "against Cecchetti's wishes" [P, Wikipedia]. The master's method was written down by a bookseller-critic and a dancer.
- **Vaganova:** *Osnovy klassicheskogo tantsa* (Leningrad 1934 [P]); English *Basic Principles of Classical Ballet* 1946 (Britannica [D]); Dover 1969 edition from the 4th Russian edition, trans. Anatole Chujoy [D, archive.org record; lending scan, in copyright].
- **RAD:** Association of Operatic Dancing of Great Britain founded 1920 by Adeline Genée, Tamara Karsavina, Edouard Espinosa, Phyllis Bedells, Philip Richardson and Lucia Cormani, to set "the standard by which ballet should be taught and examined" [D, Archives Hub blog Aug 2015]; later the Royal Academy of Dance.
- **Bournonville:** August Bournonville's *Études chorégraphiques* (1848, 1855, 1861) [P, modern edition seen in catalogue]; the "Bournonville schools", six daily classes, assembled after his death by Hans Beck and used at the Royal Danish Ballet School until 1951 [P, Dance Teacher]; Jürgensen, Knud Arne, *The Bournonville Tradition: The First Fifty Years, 1829–1879*, 2 vols, Dance Books 1997 [P].
- **Balanchine:** Schorer, Suki. *Suki Schorer on Balanchine Technique*, Knopf 1999 [D catalogue].
- **Comparative:** Warren, Gretchen Ward. *Classical Ballet Technique*, University Press of Florida 1989, 395 pp., 2,600+ photographs; Part I includes "ideal body structure and proportions" [D]. Hammond, *Ballet Basics* (college textbook, 5th edn [D]).

**2.2 Notation: writing down a moving body.** What: Feuillet (1700), Stepanov (1892), Laban's Kinetography (1928 [P]) and Labanotation, Benesh (1947–55). Why: the problem of the drawing: how to specify a three-dimensional, timed product on paper. Directly comparable with technical drawing. Facts and sources:
- Stepanov, Vladimir. *Alfavit dvizhenii chelovecheskogo tela* (Alphabet of the movements of the human body), 1892. [D, Harvard "Take Note" page]
- Benesh: Rudolf and Joan Benesh, developed 1947–55, presented publicly at the Royal Opera House September 1955; *Petrouchka* (1957) the earliest known full score; Faith Worth employed by the Royal Ballet as notator 1960; Benesh Institute 1962; incorporated in the RAD 15 April 1997. [D, RAD "History of the Benesh Institute" PDF, which gives the introductory book as both 1956 and 1958]
- Dance Notation Bureau, New York, co-founded 1940 by Ann Hutchinson (Guest), Eve Gentry, Janey Price and Helen Priest Rogers; Hutchinson's *Labanotation* 1954, preface by Balanchine. [D, Boston Globe obituary 16 Apr 2022]
- Hutchinson Guest, Ann. *Dance Notation: The Process of Recording Movement on Paper*, 1984 [P]; her Britannica article "Labanotation" [D].

**2.3 Film as the record of a technique.** What: Alexander Shiryaev (1867–1941), Mariinsky dancer, filmed dances 1906–09 on a 17.5 mm Biokam; refused permission to film the company, he animated 20–25 cm puppets frame by frame; his film was later used in restoring *La Bayadère* at the Mariinsky. [P, Wikipedia, citing Beumers, Bocharov & Robinson, *Alexander Shiryaev: Master of Movement*, Le Giornate del Cinema Muto 2009] Why: the camera as a notation machine, and a maker who turned to model-making when denied access to bodies.

**2.4 Reconstruction.** What: re-making ballets from notation, film and memory. Why: authenticity, authorship and the copy, all live questions in design history. Facts and sources:
- Sergeyev (Sergeev) notations: Houghton Library, Harvard Theatre Collection, MS Thr 245 (*Nikolai Sergeev Dance Notations and Music Scores for Ballets, 1888–1944*) and MS Thr 186 (*Swan Lake*); bought 1967 and 1969. [D, Harvard and Wikipedia]
- Wiley, Roland John. "Dances from Russia: An Introduction to the Sergeyev Collection", *Harvard Library Bulletin* 24(1), 1976. [P, cited not seen]
- Reconstructions: Vikharev's *Sleeping Beauty* (Mariinsky 1999); Ratmansky's *Harlequinade* (ABT 2018) and *Giselle* (Bolshoi 2019). [P] Doug Fullington: reconstruction is "necessarily interpretative"; later notations sometimes give "only the legs and feet, or the ground plan". [D, DanceTabs June 2015]
- Franko, Mark. "Repeatability, Reconstruction and Beyond", *Theatre Journal* 41(1), 1989 [P]; *The Oxford Handbook of Dance and Reenactment*, OUP 2017 [D; editor Franko P].
- Hodson, Millicent, "Searching for Nijinsky's *Sacre*" (set on the Bryn Mawr syllabus [D listing]); Hutchinson Guest and Claudia Jeschke decoding Nijinsky's own notation of *L'Après-midi d'un faune* [D, Boston Globe].

**2.5 Gender in technique and the male dancer.** What: how the technique splits into male and female vocabularies (pointe, partnering, jumps), the 19th-century Paris decline of the male dancer and the *travesty* dancer, and 20th-century anxiety about boys in ballet. Why: technique as a gendered specification. Sources:
- Foster, S. L. "The Ballerina's Phallic Pointe", in Foster (ed.), *Corporealities: Dancing Knowledge, Culture and Power*, Routledge 1996, pp. 1–24. [D for publication; pages from MIT OCW listing]
- Daly, Ann. "The Balanchine Woman: Of Hummingbirds and Channel Swimmers", *TDR* 31, 1987. [D listing on MIT OCW; issue and pages P: 31(1), 8–21]
- Fisher, Jennifer. "Tulle as Tool: Embracing the Conflict of the Ballerina as Powerhouse", *Dance Research Journal* 39(1), 2007, 2–24. [D, JSTOR Daily]
- Garafola, Lynn. "The Travesty Dancer in Nineteenth-Century Ballet", *Dance Research Journal* 17(2)/18(1), 1985–86 [P].
- Burt, Ramsay. *The Male Dancer: Bodies, Spectacle, Sexualities*, Routledge 1995 (later editions) [P]. Fisher, Jennifer & Anthony Shay (eds), *When Men Dance: Choreographing Masculinities Across Borders*, OUP 2009 [D].
- Foster, S. L. *Choreography and Narrative: Ballet's Staging of Story and Desire*, Indiana UP 1996, 392 pp., *Jason et Médée* (1771) to *Giselle* (1841) [D].

### Tier 3: could know

- **3.1 The ageing dancer.** Wainwright & Turner 2006 (above); Schwaiger, Elisabeth, *Ageing, Gender and Dancers' Bodies*, PhD, Victoria University 2006, 30 dancers aged 27–76; finds money, company structures and family drove retirement more than physical ageing [D, PDF downloadable]. Retirement at 42 at the Paris Opéra: `../pass2_body.md` §4.2.
- **3.2 Turnout as anatomy.** Turnout is mostly hip external rotation; dancers who lack it compensate at knee and foot. Burns, Grace, MA thesis (Chichester 2023), a review of 6 studies, finds a positive association between compensated turnout and lower-limb injury, "No definitive causation" [D; CC BY 4.0, restricted access]. A pointer only; a physiotherapy review should replace it.
- **3.3 Ballet in anthropology and sociology.** Wulff, Helena. *Ballet across Borders: Career and Culture in the World of Dancers*, Berg 1998 [D catalogue]; Kealiinohomoku (above); Novack, Cynthia, "Ballet, Gender and Cultural Politics", in Thomas (ed.), *Dance, Gender and Culture*, 1993 [D listing, MIT OCW].
- **3.4 The studio as apparatus.** Barre, mirror and raked floor: `../pass2_body.md` §3; Degas's *Dancers Practicing at the Barre* (Met, 1877) as the image.
- **3.5 Popular histories, to be read critically.** Kelly, Deirdre, *Ballerina: Sex, Scandal, and Suffering Behind the Symbol of Perfection*, Greystone 2012: journalistic; Quill & Quire calls it "accessible and fun, if not exhaustive" [D]. Homans, Jennifer, *Apollo's Angels: A History of Ballet*, Random House 2010 [D via Roma Tre syllabus]: the standard narrative history; strong on technique as culture, a trade book.
- **3.6 Disability and ballet now.** Joe Powell-Main: four years at the Royal Ballet School; a knee injury and a car accident; returned with Ballet Cymru on crutches or in a wheelchair; danced Kristen McNally's *Sleepwalker* in Royal Ballet Draft Works 2022 [D, Pointe Magazine 2 Nov 2022]. Ferri & Leahy, "Dance as a Powerful Tool to Advance Disability Inclusion", *Dance Research* 43(1), 2025, doi 10.3366/drs.2025.0439, CC BY-NC-SA (Stopgap, not ballet) [D].

## 3. People and makers

**Named by the record (the authors of technique).** Pierre Beauchamp(s) (commissioned notation, LoC [D]); Raoul-Auger Feuillet (*Chorégraphie* 1700 [D]); Pierre Rameau (1725 [P]); Kellom Tomlinson (1724/1735 [D]); Carlo Blasis (1820 [D]); Charles Didelot (wires, *Flore et Zéphire* [P]); Geneviève Gosselin (1791–1818; pointe 1813 or 1815, sources disagree [C]); Amalia Brugnoli (1822 or 1823 [C]); Filippo and Marie Taglioni (1832 [D date]); August Bournonville; Enrico Cecchetti; Agrippina Vaganova; George Balanchine; Vladimir Stepanov (1892 [D]); Alexander Gorsky; Nikolai Sergeyev; Rudolf Laban; Rudolf and Joan Benesh; Ann Hutchinson Guest; Alexander Shiryaev.

**Named but under-credited (the hands that wrote the technique down).**
- Cyril W. Beaumont and Stanislas Idzikowski, who wrote the 1922 Cecchetti manual [D].
- Hans Beck, who compiled the Bournonville schools [P].
- Anatole Chujoy, Vaganova's English translator [D]; John Barker, who translated the sample lesson with music [D].
- Jacques Dezais, joint author and publisher of the 1713 Feuillet [D]; John Weaver, English translator 1706 [D].
- Joan Benesh, a dancer, whose body knowledge made the notation workable; Faith Worth, the Royal Ballet's notator from 1960 [D].
- Sergeyev's assistant notators Alexander Chekrygin, Victor Rakhmanov, Nikolay Kremnev, S. Ponomaryev [P, Wikipedia]; the Fullington point that "Most ballets were notated by more than one person" [D].
- DNB co-founders Eve Gentry, Janey Price, Helen Priest Rogers [D]; RAD co-founder Lucia Cormani, usually dropped from the list [D].
- Eighteenth-century men on pointe: Antoine-Bonaventure Pitrot, Sandham [P, Fairfax].
- Claudia Jeschke (Nijinsky decoding with Hutchinson Guest) [D].

**Named now, as changers of the specification.** Cassa Pancho and Cira Robinson (bronze and brown pointe shoes, 2018) [D]; Pap Ndiaye and Constance Rivière (Paris Opéra report, 2021) and the company's five Black dancers who led the 2020 manifesto (names not in the source seen) [D]; Joe Powell-Main [D]; Michaela DePrince (a case in Annandale 2023 [D]).

**Not named.** The children selected and rejected at auditions (the raw material); class pianists, whose music sets the timing of every exercise; teachers in local schools who transmit the systems; the physiotherapists and conditioning staff whose work produced the 2021 injury data (named as co-authors only when they publish); the models in technique manuals (Warren's book does list its models [D], an exception worth showing); the dancers whose notated bodies stand behind every Stepanov score; the men and women of colour who pancaked shoes for decades before 2018.

## 5. How it is taught elsewhere

1. **Bryn Mawr College, "Dancing Histories/Writing Dance" (B225), Elizabeth June Bergman, Fall 2020** [D, syllabus .docx on Moodle]. ## 6. Gaps and traps

- **"Taglioni was the first on pointe."** Trap. Hammond argues the earliest practitioner was not a 19th-century ballerina and not only female; Fairfax gathers 18th-century men "on the toes"; Gosselin is given as 1813 or 1815 in the same article; Brugnoli as 1822 or 1823. Teach "earliest known" and the dispute itself. [C]
- **"Taglioni wore pointe shoes."** Her surviving shoe (V&A, c. 1842) is unblocked, stiffened only by darning [D]. The block came later (`../pass2_body.md` §1.1). Strength did the work the box later did.
- **"Beauchamp invented the five positions."** The LoC essay documents only that he was commissioned to create a notation and that Feuillet published one in 1700 [D]. Whether Beauchamp devised the positions, and the reported dispute over Feuillet using his system, rest on secondary accounts [P/C]. A ready-made Attribution case.
- **"The Vaganova (or Cecchetti) method" as one author's invention.** Each system is a synthesis of teachers and was written down or compiled by others (Beaumont and Idzikowski; Beck; translators) [D facts; A framing].
- **Manuals describe what should happen, not what did.** Prescriptive texts, especially before film. Hammond notes that masters described pointe casually [D], which means the manuals also under-record.
- **Notation is not a recording.** Stepanov scores vary; later ones give "only the legs and feet, or the ground plan" [D]. Any "authentic reconstruction" claim is an argument, not a fact.
- **Injury statistics.** Mattiussi is one company (Royal Ballet); Kenny et al. found low study quality (median 8/33); the UEA abstract's "280%" is a typo. Do not mix prevalence (Smith) with incidence per 1,000 hours (Mattiussi).
- **Eating-disorder figures** pool heterogeneous studies from 1966 to 2013 (Arcelus); teach "about three times the risk", with the date range.
- **Popular books are not the base.** Kelly (2012) and Homans (2010) are readable and useful as objects of critique; scholarly claims should rest on Foster, Hammond, Garafola, Franko, Aalten, Wainwright and Turner, and the medical literature.
- **"Flesh" means white.** Pink shoes and tights encoded one skin as neutral until 2018 (shoes) and the 2020s (tights in contracts; see `../pass2_body.md` §4.5 on the NYCB 2022 agreement). Avoid "first Black ballerina" framings; use "earliest known" and name the institution.
- **Missing from the record (and from this pass):** non-Western technique systems as scholarship (the Cuban school, Chinese and Japanese training) were not searched for this angle and need their own pass; the voices of children in training; the accompanist; disabled dancers in classical (not inclusive-contemporary) companies, where the scholarship is thin (one MA thesis found); teachers' own manuals outside Europe and the US.
- **Sources blocked or not opened in this pass:** PMC8754298 (a feminist analysis of disordered eating among young dancers; fetch refused); SSOAR copy of Wainwright et al. 2005 "Fractured identities"; Columbia copy of Garafola's travesty article; Project MUSE (Gottschild); the Paris Opéra report PDF itself.

## 7. Size

**Estimate:** 4 to 6 hours of learning in a foundation-year design course: about a third of a 12- to 15-hour ballet unit, or a free-standing short unit.

- **One page (15–20 minutes):** "The made body". Three objects: a Feuillet page of the five positions (LoC), Taglioni's unblocked shoe (V&A, link out) and a Degas barre picture (Met CC0). Claim: the technique is a design system with a specification (five positions, turnout), a factory (the class), a product (the dancer) and a cost (injury, exclusion). One dispute: who was earliest on pointe. One table: what the record names (method authors) and what it does not (scribes, children, accompanists).
- **One session (2 hours):** Read Foster's "Dancing Bodies" (extract) and Hammond's abstract; then a making exercise taken from the Bryn Mawr reconstruction idea: students invent a notation for a 16-count phrase, swap papers and try to perform each other's, then compare with Feuillet and Benesh. Debrief on what notation leaves out and who gets credited.
- **One unit (about 6 hours, four sessions):** (1) Codification: from court to manual (Feuillet, Blasis, the five positions as standard parts); (2) Pointe: technique before technology (Hammond, the Taglioni shoe, the pointe shoe from `pass2_body.md`); (3) The record: notation, film, reconstruction (Stepanov/Sergeyev, Benesh, Shiryaev); (4) The specification and its costs: the ideal body, race, disability, injury and ageing (Arcelus, Mattiussi, Ndiaye–Rivière, Freed/Ballet Black, Wainwright and Turner).
