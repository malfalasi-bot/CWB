# 03 Objects, makers and materials: scoping notes

Angle: the made things of ballet and the people who make them: pointe shoe, tutu, costume, wigs and make-up, scenery and props as built objects, floor, rosin, tights, materials and supply chains, dancewear firms, museum holdings.

Compiled 21 October 2026. Builds on `../pass2_body.md` (20 Oct 2026), which already holds verified tables on pointe-shoe construction, manufacturers, shoe counts and budgets, tutu hours by institution, and named and unnamed makers. Those tables are not repeated here. This file adds the scholarly base, the concepts, the archives and licences, the syllabi, and the traps. Raw findings with URLs are in the **Research log** at the end; the sections above cite them in short form.

Status key: **[D]** documented (seen at source this session or in pass2); **[P]** probable (secondary, or bibliographic detail not seen at source); **[C]** contested; **[A]** argued (an author's argument, or mine, marked as such).

## 2. The concepts

| Concept | The debate | Who argues what, where |
|---|---|---|
| **The pointe shoe as technology (and as a "technology of gender")** | Is the shoe a neutral tool, or does it shape what bodies may do and who may do it? | Hammond 1988 [D]: early pointe was gradual, not solely female; the "revolution" is hindsight. Oreamuno 2018 (student journal, OA) [D]: the shoe is a gendered object by repetition (Butler, de Lauretis), and a tool of embodied knowledge (Ingold, Clark & Chalmers). Ingold, "Culture on the Ground", *J. Material Culture* 9:3 (2004) [P]: footwear changes how the world is perceived through the feet. Gaynor Minden's patents vs traditional makers: the press debate over whether engineered shoes change technique [P, magazine level only; no scholarly treatment found] |
| **Attribution: designer, maker, wearer** | Who is the author of a costume? | Barbieri 2017 [P]: material costume "co-authors" performance. Met/Speelberg 2017 [D]: even Bakst's drawings were partly workshop copies. Karinska's own career (executing Bérard, Derain, Miró sketches, then designing) blurs the line [P]. V&A cataloguing practice puts the designer in the "maker" field [D]. The Freed mark names a maker by symbol only [D]. Jane Pritchard calls Madame Manya the "unsung hero" of the tutu (London Ballet Circle 2025) [D] |
| **The object biography** | Objects accumulate meaning through use, alteration, exchange | Kopytoff, "The cultural biography of things" (in Appadurai, ed., *The Social Life of Things*, 1986) [P]; Gosden & Marshall, "The cultural biography of objects", *World Archaeology* 31:2 (1999) [P]. Applied: Brown "13 Things" pointe-shoe page [D]; the NGA's reassembly of 400 fragments into 100 costumes (1973 purchase) [D]; Silva's shoe with a broken shank and plasters [D] |
| **The ephemeral and the record** | Performance vanishes; what remains is the costume, the shoe, the design | Peggy Phelan, *Unmarked* (1993) [P]: performance exists only in the present and cannot be saved or reproduced (paraphrase; not checked at source). Rebecca Schneider, *Performing Remains* (2011) [P]: remains are themselves performative. Fensham, "Repetition as a methodology: costumes, archives and choreography" and *Fabrications* (OUP) [P]: the costume as an archive of movement. Dickinson student project (2016) [D]: costume is the main evidence because the company was not filmed |
| **Craft, skill and the endangered trade** | Is hand-making a heritage to protect or a cost to cut? | Heritage Crafts Red List: pointe-shoe making critically endangered, two UK businesses [D]; Richard Sennett, *The Craftsman* (2008) [P]; Glenn Adamson, *Thinking Through Craft* (2007) [P]: craft as a process, defined against industry. Freed's makers train "two to three years" (Spitalfields Life 2013) [D] |
| **Default bodies and skin tone** | Pink satin and "flesh" tights as a designed default for white skin | Dixon Gottschild 2003 [P]; Ballet Black/Freed 2018 [D]; V&A record calls Freed's the "first skin tone pointe shoes" made in the UK [D], but Gaynor Minden offered shades from 2017 and dancers pancaked for decades [D]: the "first" is a trap and the labour came before the product |
| **Orientalism in costume and make-up** | *Schéhérazade*, *La Bayadère*, *Nutcracker* "Tea": exotic as design brief | Said, *Orientalism* (1978) [P]; Garafola, *Diaghilev's Ballets Russes* (OUP, 1989) [P]; Phil Chan, *Final Bow for Yellowface* (2020) [P]. Costume, wig and make-up are the means |
| **Costume as engineering for movement** | Design under the constraint of a moving body | Collins & Jarvis 2016 [D]: costume and choreography co-evolved. Karinska's bias-cut bodices, six to fifteen panels to move with the ribs (Bentley excerpt) [D]. Mariinsky tulle "softer at the bottom so it doesn't scratch" [P] |
| **Planetary weight of a disposable instrument** | A professional may use a pair per performance | Company counts (NYCB 9,000–11,000 pairs; RBO 6,000+; BRB c. 4,000: all fundraising figures, pass2) [D as claims]; ENB recycles 600 shoes a year [D]; polyester satin [D]. [A] The shoe is designed to fail; its short life is a design decision embedded in a craft tradition |

## 5. How it is taught elsewhere

No university syllabus dedicated to ballet's material culture was found. The angle appears inside object, costume and Russian-history courses:

1. **Brown University, Joukowsky Institute, "13 Things" (2008–09)**: one thing per week; students research a chosen thing. A student page on the pointe shoe reads Barringer & Schlesinger 2004; Bentley, *Smithsonian* 1984; Cunningham et al., *AJSM* 1998 (toe-box mechanics); Hammond 1988 and 1989; Ingold 2004 and 2007. The model fits a foundation design course directly: object biography grounded in anthropology and mechanics [D].
2. **Deree – American College of Greece, DR4330 *Costume Design* (Level 6, 2021)**: required Barbieri, *Costume in Performance*; Holt, *Modernizing Costume Design 1820–1920* (2020); Pantouvaki & McNeil (eds), *Performance Costume* (2021); recommended Maclaurin & Monks (eds), *Costume: Readings in Theatre Practice* (2014), Pollatsek, *Unbuttoned* (2016). No ballet named [D].
3. **NGA Teacher's Notes, *From Russia with Love* (1999)**: compare pairs of Ballets Russes costumes by design approach; notes on stencilling vs embroidery as an economy of making; conservators separating layers of alteration [D].
4. Supporting: Edinburgh College of Art DESI10077 (make one costume; reads Janet Arnold, *Patterns of Fashion 2*) [D]; Dickinson HIST254 student bibliography on the Ballets Russes (Leong 1998; Hansen 1985; Ruane 2009) [D]; Mida & Kim, *The Dress Detective* (Bloomsbury, 2015) as the object-based method used in fashion collections [P].

## 6. Gaps and traps

**Traps (what the obvious accounts get wrong):**
- **"Taglioni invented pointe" / "first pointe shoe."** Hammond 1988 shows earlier, gradual development and male practitioners. Say "earliest known".
- **"Tights are named after Maillot, a Paris Opéra costumier."** Etymonline gives *maillot* = swaddling, probably from *maille* (mesh); no costumier attested [C].
- **"Pointe shoes are silk satin."** Most are 100% polyester satin (Gonzalez et al. 2015). The V&A S.140 record lists "silk" in one materials field and "satin" in another: check.
- **"First skin-tone pointe shoes" (Freed 2018).** Gaynor Minden 2017; dancers pancaked shoes for decades (Silva's Chacott shoe). The product followed the dancers' labour.
- **Karinska as maker.** Catalogues put the designer in the maker field; Karinska ran workshops, and her daughter's work was credited to her.
- **Bakst "original" designs.** Some are workshop copies on transfer paper (Met).
- **"Authentic" Ballets Russes costumes.** Many are composites of 1910s and 1930s parts, reassembled by conservators (NGA).
- **Numbers.** Freed output ranges from 150,000 to 333,000 pairs a year by source; company shoe budgets are fundraising figures; ENB's CO2 saving and the Australian Ballet's tutu cost are company figures. Name the counter.
- **Harlequin "1979".** The firm's own history says 1977 (as Theatre Flooring), renamed 1984.
- **Gaynor Minden "1993 patent".** Launch 1993; patents 1990/1991 (pass2).
- **The "Crown" maker** is "24 years" in one Spitalfields piece and "Ray, twenty-six years" in another.

**Contested:** the date and maker of the earliest plate tutu (shape by 1890s; hooped flat plate 1930s–40s); whether engineered shoes alter technique; the reliability of Russian institutional sources since 2022.

**Missing from the record:**
- Wages, piece rates and contracts of shoemakers and costume makers (none found).
- Any named maker in Paris Opéra, ROH/RBO or Australian Ballet institutional sources.
- Production outside Western Europe and the US: Chacott (Japan), Grishko's Czech and North Macedonian plants, Chinese and other dancewear factories. No scholarship found.
- Raw-material chains: where Freed's hessian, leather and satin come from; who makes tutu net now (Italy is mentioned twice, unnamed).
- A scholarly business history of any dancewear firm, and a peer-reviewed history of the pointe shoe as an object (Barringer & Schlesinger is a practical handbook).
- Wig and make-up departments: almost no writing beyond job adverts and Chan's book.
- Accessible formats: no audio-described or tactile resources on these objects were found; handling a pointe shoe and a swatch of net in class would address this [A].

## 7. Size

**Estimate: 6–8 hours of learning** in a foundation-year design course, inside a ballet unit that shares time with other angles.

- **One page** can carry: *Who stamps the sole?* The pointe shoe from materials (polyester satin, hessian, paste, leather) to maker's mark to the dancer's needle, ending on the V&A brown shoes with Cira Robinson's handwriting. About 1,200 words, 4–6 images (Met CC0 Bakst design for contrast, V&A shoe at 768 px, Spitalfields Life linked, not reproduced).

- [D] Karinska (Wikipedia, probable): Varvara Jmoudsky, b. Kharkiv 3 Oct 1886, d. 18 Oct 1983; Paris costume business from 1920s; for Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo she took sketches by Bérard, Derain, Miró and "chose fabrics and decided how they would be made"; London studio (Cecil Beaton); New York from 1939; Karinska Inc. / Stage and Art Inc. run by nephew Vladimir "Vlady" Jmoudsky, sold to him for $1 in 1964; daughter Irène ran a Paris studio, some of whose work was credited to Karinska; 1948 Oscar (*Joan of Arc*, with Dorothy Jeakins); took over NYCB costume shop in 1964 under a Ford Foundation contract; c. 75 Balanchine ballets. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_Karinska
