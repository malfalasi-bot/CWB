# 04 Stage design, scenography and the visual arts: scoping notes

Angle: ballet as a site where painting, architecture, fashion and design met. Court spectacle and the baroque stage; the Romantic stage, gas and machinery; Imperial Russia's scenic studios; the Ballets Russes as a design event and its spill into fashion, Art Deco and interiors; Constructivism and the Soviet stage; Noguchi, Rauschenberg, Johns, Cunningham; the Royal Ballet's designers; lighting design as a profession; the bare stage; projection and digital scenography; the scenic workshops and their trades.

Status key: **[D]** documented (seen at source in this session, usually a catalogue record, museum page or publisher page); **[P]** probable (secondary, or standard account not checked at source); **[C]** contested; **[A]** argued (an author's argument, or my interpretation). The research log at the foot of the file gives the evidence for every [D].

**The thesis this angle offers the unit [A]:** a ballet's look is credited to one painter, but it was built by a chain of trades: scene painters, costumiers, gas men, carpenters, now projection designers. The record names the painter on the design sheet and loses the hands that turned a 30 cm watercolour into a 16 m cloth. The drawing survives and the set does not, so the history of ballet design has mostly been written as a history of drawings. This is the course's threshold concept (a web with obscured strands) in a single object.

## 2. The concepts

1. **The total work of art (Gesamtkunstwerk) and its critics.** Bellow argues that the Ballets Russes stage was a "mixed-media form inspired by" Wagner's total artwork, in which painters tested painting against "duration and dimension, figuration and embodiment" (Bellow 2013, via JDH review) [A]. Garafola (1989) places the synthesis inside an economic enterprise with a paying audience [A]. **Counter-argument:** Hanna Järvinen (review, *Dance Research Journal* 46.1, 2014) says such studies show "how easily dance becomes secondary to music and set design" [D quote, A argument]. The Musée Picasso says that in *Parade* "the dance … illustrates and underlines the sets and costumes" [D]. Students should weigh design primacy against dance primacy.

2. **Authorship of the set: designer, executant, studio.** Who is the author of a cloth painted by Shervashidze from a Picasso sketch? The NGA press release calls Shervashidze "Diaghilev's principal set designer" [D], although he was the painter. The Pompidou credits Picasso alone for *Parade* [D], while other accounts add assistants (Balla, via McCarren 2003, or Italian scene painters) [C]. The *Firebird* case is sharper still: Sapunov ignored Golovin's layered "laminating" method, so the cloth seen in 1910 was in part Sapunov's look [D]. Polunin (1927) is the scene painter's own account [D]. Aronson's "Remember the Designers!" (2021) argues the same omission one level up: critics credit composers, not designers [A]. **This is the course's Attribution thread in one object.**

3. **The ephemeral and the record.** Sets were painted to be struck. What survives is the design drawing, a few cloths and costumes, photographs and press. The Pompidou's conservator says the curtain is fragile because "it was originally an ephemeral object" [D]. The NGA Australia's director called its costumes "the most fragile works of art in the national collection" [D]. **Consequence [A]:** because drawings survive and sets do not, histories of ballet design are mostly histories of drawings by named painters. Reconstruction (Hodson and Archer) and re-making (Messel's ABT remake, which the V&A judged "unlike" the original) test what the record can rebuild.

4. **Scale and transposition.** A 30 cm watercolour becomes a 16 m cloth. The "continental method" paints on the floor with distemper [P], and the *Parade* curtain was painted horizontally [D]. Bellow's question is how a painter's style survives three dimensions, movement and light [A]. For a design student this is the gap between drawing and making.

5. **Orientalism and the "Russian" look.** Bakst's *Schéhérazade* and *Cléopâtre*, and the Poiret vogue. Wollen (1993) links fashion, orientalism and the liberated body [P]. Jane Sharp, "Beyond Orientalism" (2007), reads Russian modernism from the empire's edge [P]. Bellow calls Matisse's *Chant du rossignol* "disorienting decoration" [D chapter title]. Davis (2010) traces the commercial uses [D]. The debate: was the exotic a costume worn by Russians for Paris, an imperial gaze turned outward, or both? [A]

6. **Stage to street, and back.** Davis argues that Chanel's *Train Bleu* "slangy chic" was "an alternative strand of modernism" [D]. Poiret disputed that Bakst came first [C]. Miyake's 1991 Forsythe costumes ran the loop from fashion to stage [D/P]. The concept is that design moves through markets as well as through influence.

7. **Light as a material.** Gas from 1822 [D]; the white act as a lighting effect [P]; the 1928 projections in *Ode* [D]; Rosenthal turning lighting into an authored profession [P]. Light leaves no object behind, so in the archive it is the least visible part of a production [A].

8. **Absence as design.** Balanchine's practice clothes (1951) [D]; Cunningham and Cage's independence of the elements, with Rauschenberg [D for the collaboration, P for the theory]. Is a bare stage designed? A seminar question.

9. **The museum and the market make the costume an artwork.** The Sotheby's sales from 1966 [D], Austin's purchase c.1933 [D] and the exhibitions of 2010 and 2013 (227,660 visitors at the NGA [D]) turned work clothes into art objects, and the museums then credit them to the designer [A].

10. **Recovering women designers.** The McNay in 2024 aimed to "turn the focus from the prominent men" [D]. Goncharova and Exter were primary designers. The question is whether recovery reproduces the same designer-centred model one more time [A].

## 5. How it is taught elsewhere

1. **Barnard College, Columbia (Lynn Garafola, now emerita).** Courses include "Western Theatrical Dance from the Renaissance to the 1960s", "Balanchine and the Reinvention of Modern Ballet" and "Performing the Political: Russian Ballet" [D titles]. Students read Garafola, *Diaghilev's Ballets Russes* (1989) and *Legacies* (2005), and her essays "Making Dances: Process and Practice in Diaghilev's Ballets Russes" (2010) and "Workshop of the Muses" (in Bowlt ed., *A Feast of Wonders*, Skira 2009) [D for essays, P that they are on the reading lists]. Every course has a research component built on primary sources [D].
2. **American University (Juliet Bellow), "Art and Dance, 1860–1960"** [D title]. The probable readings are Bellow 2013, Davis 2010, the Pritchard catalogue, and her chapters on Rodin's dancers (Yale, 2025) [P]. This is the art-history route into ballet design.
3. **"Stravinsky and the Ballets Russes" (Spring 2013, Hannah Kosstrin and David Schiff; Reed College [P]).** It covers choreography, music and design across 1910–28, with four short papers and a research paper [D summary; full syllabus fetch blocked].
4. **Scenography programmes (University of Leeds MA Performance Design, taught by Joslin McKinney, with Christopher Baugh as emeritus) and US scene-design courses.** They read McKinney & Butterworth, *The Cambridge Introduction to Scenography* (2009); Baugh, *Theatre, Performance and Technology* (2005/2013); Aronson, *Looking into the Abyss* (2005) and ed. *Routledge Companion to Scenography* (2017); Howard, *What is Scenography?* (2002); Brockett et al., *Making the Scene* (2010) [D for texts]. A US syllabus example: Florida Atlantic University, Scene Design 2, requires Parker, Wolf & Block, *Scene Design and Stage Lighting*, and Payne, *The Scenographic Imagination* [D].

**What none of these does [A]:** none treats the scene painter or costumier as a subject. All start from the designer. That gap is the F1 unit's opening.

## 6. Gaps and traps

- **"First" claims everywhere.** *Ballet comique* is "the first ballet"; Torelli's "first effective machinery"; Rosenthal "the first lighting designer"; *Parade* "the first modern ballet" (dealer copy). Use "earliest known", or teach each as a claim.
- **The designer stands in for the maker.** Catalogues credit Picasso alone for *Parade* (Pompidou) or misname the painter as "set designer" (NGA on Shervashidze) [D]. The *Firebird* cloth was Sapunov's execution [D]. Teach the chain, not the name.
- **Contested attributions:**
  - The Romantic tutu credited to Lami [C].
  - *Ballet de la Nuit* credited to Torelli: "no definitive evidence" [C].
  - Ciceri's appointment as chief, 1815 or 1818 [C].
  - The place of the 1581 performance, Petit-Bourbon or the Louvre gallery [C].
  - Who helped paint the *Parade* curtain [C].
  - Whether Bakst or Poiret came first [C].
- **Wrong or shifting details in common accounts.** The brief's title for Davis is wrong; the book is *Ballets Russes Style* (2010). The NGA show's end date appears as 2 September and 6 October 2013 (it was extended) [D both]. Gabo's Wikipedia page gives *La Chatte* as 1926; it premiered in 1927 [D].
- **Ballets Russes centrism.** Most exhibitions and open images are of 1909–29. The Imperial studios, the Paris Opéra workshop, Soviet design (Virsaladze) and post-war national companies are thin by comparison. Asia barely appears: Miyake is the exception found here. Japanese ballet design, and companies in Latin America, Africa and China, were **not found** in this pass.
- **Copyright skews the image set.** Open images favour Bakst and the 19th century. A unit built only from free images will make ballet design look orientalist and pre-1925 [A].
- **The archive is drawings.** Sets were struck and repainted, and cloths were patched, stored in tractor garages and dropped in harbours (*Parade*) [D]. Students should not mistake a design sheet for what audiences saw.
- **Reconstructions are new works.** The V&A says Messel's 1976 ABT remake was "surprisingly unlike" the original [D]. Hodson and Archer's *Rite* is a 1987 work.
- **Planetary weight (largely missing from the literature):**
  - Coal gas for lighting from 1822.
  - Animal glue and pigments in distemper [D]; jute and cotton canvas [D].
  - Celluloid, plastics and steel in *La Chatte* [D]; synthetic ponyskin and "American cloth" in *Pas d'acier* [P].
  - Warehousing more than 20,000 costumes [D]; touring freight.
  - Fire risk from gaslight (Emma Livry, 1862 [P]).
  - Gas installation costs (*Aladin*, 170,000 francs [P]).
- **Accessibility.** Scenography is the most visual part of ballet. For blind and low-vision learners the unit needs described images, and could use audio-described performance practice (RBO offers audio description [P]). Tactile models such as the Noguchi maquettes are worth looking for.
- **Commons provenance trap.** Some Commons Bakst files come from reproduction-sale sites [D]. Use museum-sourced files only.
- **Blocked or unchecked in this pass:** the Met object record (334999; fetch blocked); the Scribd syllabus (blocked); the text of Polunin's book; whether NGA Washington owns PD designs; Harvard Theatre Collection holdings; the ROH Collections licence.

## 7. Size

- **Estimate [A]: 6–8 hours of learning** in a foundation-year design course. That is about a quarter of a ballet unit, because this is the angle closest to the students' own discipline. It breaks down as:
  - 2 × 90-minute sessions.
  - 2–3 hours of reading (one Garafola chapter, one Pritchard catalogue essay, and Polunin's preface if a library copy is found).
  - 1–2 hours making: scale up a design from a 1:25 drawing to a painted cloth, then write a one-paragraph credit that names every hand.
- **One page can carry: "Who painted the curtain?"** Picasso's *Parade* curtain (1917; 10.5 × 16.4 m; distemper on jute and cotton; Pompidou), set beside the Train Bleu cloth "designed by Picasso and painted by Shervashidze" and Sapunov's *Firebird*. One object, three credit lines, one argument about authorship. Images: link out for Picasso (©); use an open Bakst or Golovin design as the illustrated example.
- **One session can carry the chain from drawing to stage to museum through three objects:**
  1. A Ciceri sketch for the nuns' ballet (Gallica; gas, the workshop and the white act).
  2. A Bakst costume design (Met CC0) beside the BnF record "costumes exécutés par Marie Muelle".
  3. A Cunningham or Noguchi set as the post-war reversal.
- **One unit (if this angle led a whole unit) would run four sessions:**
  1. Machines and perspective (1581–1700).
  2. Gas and the white act (1822–41).
  3. The painter's stage and its painters (1890–1929, Russia to Paris).
  4. Light, absence and the screen (1948 to now).

  The assessment would be a credit list for one production that names every trade.
