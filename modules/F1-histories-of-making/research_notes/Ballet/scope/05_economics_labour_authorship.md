# Scope 05: Economics, labour, authorship and the record

**Angle.** Who pays for ballet and who gets paid; the workforce on stage and behind it; who owns a ballet and who is credited; how a work made of movement survives (notation, film, the répétiteur), and who controls that record; the data a learner can use to check claims.

**Status key.** **[D]** documented: seen at source in this pass (a primary document, the publisher's page, a finding aid, or the text itself). **[P]** probable: secondary report, or known from the scholarly literature but not re-checked here. **[C]** contested. **[A]** argued: a scholar's interpretation, or (marked "[A, scope]") an interpretation offered by this scoping note.

**Overlaps.** Scope 01 (history and institutions) covers the court, the Opéra and the Imperial Theatres as institutions; 03 (objects, makers, materials) covers the pointe shoe and tutu as objects. This file treats the same things as money, contracts, property and archives. The research log at the end holds the raw notes and every source this pass actually saw.

## 1. The knowledge map

### Tier 1: cannot be skipped

**1.1 Who pays: three funding models, and why the model shapes the art.** Ballet has been paid for by a court or state (Louis XIV's Académie, the Russian Imperial Theatres, Soviet and today's French state houses); by entrepreneurs and subscribers in a market (Paris Opéra under concession from 1831, London's King's Theatre, Diaghilev's Ballets Russes); and by today's mix of tickets, philanthropy and public grant (US: mostly donors and tickets; UK: about a seventh public). *Why it matters:* it is the clearest case in the course of the funder's structure leaving marks on form, casting and gender.
- Garafola, Lynn. "The Travesty Dancer in Nineteenth-Century Ballet." *Dance Research Journal* 17.2–18.1 (1985–86): 35–40. **Open access** (Columbia Academic Commons). [D]
- Drysdale, John Duncan. *Louis Véron and the Finances of the Académie Royale de Musique, 1827 to 1835.* PhD thesis, University of Southampton, 2000. **Open access** (Southampton ePrints). [D]
- Royal Ballet and Opera, *Annual Report 2024/25* and accounts on the Charity Commission register (charity no. 211775); Cour des comptes, *L'Opéra national de Paris* (Oct 2024). Primary, free to read. [D]

**1.2 The cost disease and the "income gap".** Live performance cannot raise its labour productivity: *Swan Lake* needs as many dancers and rehearsal hours now as in 1895, while wages rise with the rest of the economy, so costs outrun earned income and the gap must be filled by donors or the state. *Why:* it explains why every company's accounts look the way they do, and it is a portable tool for any craft that resists mechanisation.
- Baumol, William J. and William G. Bowen. *Performing Arts: The Economic Dilemma. A Study of Problems Common to Theater, Opera, Music, and Dance.* New York: Twentieth Century Fund, 1966. Borrowable on Internet Archive (controlled lending, not open licence). [D]
- Throsby, David. *Economics and Culture.* Cambridge UP, 2001. [D, cited on a syllabus]
- Towse, Ruth. *A Textbook of Cultural Economics.* Cambridge UP, 2010 (chapters on performing arts, artists' labour markets, copyright). [D, cited on a syllabus]

**1.3 Dancers as a workforce.** Ranks (corps, coryphée/first artist, soloist, principal) are pay grades; most US contracts guarantee 33–37 weeks, not a year; unions (AGMA in the US, Equity in the UK, French unions under a state-employer) set floors; careers end in the thirties; injury is normalised. *Why:* it turns "the corps de ballet" from a visual effect into a group of named, waged people, which is the course's Attribution thread made literal.
- AGMA collective bargaining agreements, published as PDFs (NYCB 2022–25; ABT 2023–26; Sarasota Ballet 2022–25). Primary. [D]
- Turner, Bryan S. and Steven P. Wainwright. "Corps de Ballet: The Case of the Injured Ballet Dancer." *Sociology of Health & Illness* 25.4 (2003). [D abstract]
- Franko, Mark. *The Work of Dance: Labor, Movement, and Identity in the 1930s.* Wesleyan UP, 2002. [D]
- Fitzgerald, Paula M. *Turning Pointe: The Lived Experience of Embodied Occupational Identity in Professional Ballet Dancers.* PhD thesis, Birkbeck, University of London. **Open access.** [D]

**1.4 Who owns a ballet.** Choreography became expressly copyrightable in the US only under the 1976 Act (in force 1978); before that it had to tell a story and be fixed in notation or film. Since then ballets are licensed property: the George Balanchine Trust licenses about 75 active works, sets fees by company budget and requires an approved répétiteur; Ashton left ballets to six friends and a nephew; a court gave Martha Graham's later dances to the institution that paid her ("work for hire"). *Why:* it is the sharpest example in the course of a design right (who owns a pattern) applied to a body.
- Kraut, Anthea. *Choreographing Copyright: Race, Gender, and Intellectual Property Rights in American Dance.* Oxford UP, 2015. [D]
- *Martha Graham School and Dance Foundation v. Martha Graham Center of Contemporary Dance*, 224 F. Supp. 2d 567 (S.D.N.Y. 2002); 380 F.3d 624 (2d Cir. 2004). US court opinions: **public domain.** [D]
- US Copyright Office: Nierman, Judith, "Dance Pioneer Hanya Holm Was First to Register Choreography," *Copyright Lore* (Feb 2015); and *Study 28: Copyright in Choreographic Works* (copyright revision studies, c. 1960). US government works: **public domain.** [D]
- George Balanchine Trust, "Licensing the Ballets" and FAQ pages; Frederick Ashton Foundation, "Ashton Ballet Owners." Primary, current. [D]

**1.5 The record: notation, film, the répétiteur.** A ballet survives as Stepanov or Benesh notation, as film, and in the bodies of people who danced it. The Imperial Directorate paid for notation from 1893; Nikolai Sergeyev carried the notebooks out of Russia after 1917, earned his living staging from them in London, and they were sold to Harvard in 1969. They underlie the Mariinsky's 1999 *Sleeping Beauty* and Ratmansky's productions. *Why:* the record is partial, owned and contested, which is the course's threshold concept in miniature.
- Harvard, Houghton Library, "Nikolai Sergeev dance notations and music scores for ballets, 1888–1944," MS Thr 245 (finding aid on HOLLIS for Archival Discovery). [D]
- Gupta, Maureen. *Nicolai Serguéeff and The Sleeping Beauty: A Living Ballet Tradition.* UBC Press (year to confirm). [D publisher page]
- Jordan, Stephanie, ed. *Preservation Politics: Dance Revived, Reconstructed, Remade.* London: Dance Books, 2000. [P, cited on a graduate reading list]
- George Balanchine Foundation, Interpreters Archive and Archive of Lost Choreography (master tapes at NYPL Jerome Robbins Dance Division). [D]

**1.6 The makers behind the stage.** Pointe-shoe makers paid by the piece and known to dancers only by a stamped symbol; costume and tutu makers (Royal Opera House: about 104 costume staff plus up to 40 freelancers, 2016); scene painters whose tariffs Véron cut in 1831; dressers; stage crew, whose strikes cancel performances. *Why:* it is the most direct route into the course's question of who is named in the record.
- Freed of London, "In the Making" (company page on makers' marks), with press interviews (CBS 2017; Business Insider 2021). [D company source; P press]
- Drysdale (1.1 above), ch. 3 on the costs of sets and costumes under Véron. [D]
- BnF / Bibliothèque-musée de l'Opéra, costume *maquettes* on Gallica (designers obliged to deposit designs from 1805; tailors' annotations and swatches on the sheets). [D]
- Join-Diéterle, Catherine, ed. *L'envers du décor: à la Comédie-Française et à l'Opéra de Paris au XIXe siècle.* Moulins: CNCS / Gourcuff Gradenigo, 2012. [D]

### Tier 2: should know

**2.1 The *Nutcracker* economy.** US companies depend on one seasonal ballet that uses local children and sells to families. *Nutcracker*/holiday income averaged 48% of surveyed companies' season (performance) revenue but 15% of total revenue (Dance/USA, 2017, as quoted by Dance Data Project); about 45% of NYCB's annual *ticket* revenue (NPR, 2025). *Why:* a case of design for a market, and the reason unions time disputes for December.
- Fisher, Jennifer. *"Nutcracker" Nation: How an Old World Ballet Became a Christmas Tradition in the New World.* Yale UP, 2003 (JSTOR; Internet Archive lending copy). [D]
- Dance Data Project, *Nutcracker and Holiday Productions 2023–2025* (data byte, PDF). [P, quoting Dance/USA]

**2.2 Philanthropy that chose a style.** The Ford Foundation's $7,756,750 for ballet (Dec 1963), most of it to Balanchine's NYCB and School of American Ballet and to companies in his orbit; ABT and modern dance got nothing. *Why:* a funder fixing a national style.
- Klapper, Melissa R. *Ballet Class: An American History.* Oxford UP, 2020, ch. "Brought to You by the Ford Foundation." [D chapter]
- Garafola, Lynn. "Lincoln Kirstein, City Center, and the Rockefeller Foundation." **Open access** (Columbia Academic Commons). [D]
- Bennett, Anne Barclay. *The Management of Philanthropic Funding for Institutional Stabilization: A History of the Ford Foundation and New York City Ballet.* EdD/PhD diss., Harvard Graduate School of Education, 1989. [D citation, unread]

**2.3 Public patronage as nation-building.** Britain: CEMA, then the Arts Council (charter 1946) under Keynes, whose wife Lydia Lopokova was a Diaghilev ballerina, funded Sadler's Wells Ballet into Covent Garden. US: the NEA's earliest grant ($100,000, Dec 1965) rescued ABT; its touring and matching-grant rules shaped what companies became. USSR: total state funding, then hard-currency touring as it collapsed. *Why:* the state as client.
- Guthrie, Kate. "Awakening 'Sleeping Beauty': The Creation of National Ballet in Britain." *Music & Letters* 96.3 (2015). [D]
- Wilbur, Sarah. *Funding Bodies: Five Decades of Dance Making at the National Endowment for the Arts.* Wesleyan UP, 2021. [D]
- Ezrahi, Christina. *Swans of the Kremlin: Ballet and Power in Soviet Russia.* U of Pittsburgh Press, 2012 (Project MUSE). [D]
- Arts Council of Great Britain annual reports 1945–; NEA, *National Endowment for the Arts: A History 1965–2008* (PDF, US government, public domain). [D]

**2.4 Pensions and strikes at the Paris Opéra.** A special retirement regime lets dancers retire with a pension at 42 (40 until 2010). It survived the 2019–20 strike (63 performances cancelled, over €12m lost, *Swan Lake* danced on the Garnier steps on 24 Dec 2019) and was left out of the 2023 reform. In Dec 2023 dancers struck over unpaid preparation time: make-up and dressing, about 42 hours a month against 6 paid. *Why:* the body's short working life written into law, and invisible labour priced.
- Laillier, Joël. "La dynamique de la vocation." *Sociologie du travail* (2011). **Open access** (OpenEdition). [D abstract]
- Cour des comptes report (2024) and Assemblée nationale budget annex (programme 131). [D]
- Press: AFP (via Straits Times, 3 Jan 2020); Euronews (20 Dec 2023); actu.fr (18 Jan 2023). [P]

**2.5 What training costs, and who pays.** The Paris school has been free to pupils since Louis XIV's 1713 decree (per the Opéra's own history). The Royal Ballet School charges £34,554–£47,455 a year (2026–27) but about 90% of pupils get means-tested support, mostly from the Department for Education's Music and Dance Scheme (130 RBS pupils in 2023/24). Japan has no state school system for ballet: about 4,260 private studios (2021). *Why:* the state as hidden funder of the workforce pipeline from age 11.
- Royal Ballet School fees page; gov.uk Music and Dance Scheme; written parliamentary answer, 22 Oct 2024. [D]
- Opéra national de Paris, "Histoire – École de danse." [D]

**2.6 Data on who gets to author.** Dance Data Project: women choreographed 30.6% of works presented by the largest 150 US companies in 2023/24; 30% of artistic directors worldwide are women; 7.1% of *Nutcracker*/holiday productions at the largest 50 US companies were by women. *Why:* a quantitative route into Attribution that students can update themselves.
- Dance Data Project, *Global Leadership Report 2025*; *Season Overview 2023–2024*. [D]

**2.7 The Ballets Russes as a business.** Garafola reads Diaghilev's company "as a totality: its art, enterprise, and audience." Its Part II is titled "Enterprise": bankers organised by Astruc in 1909, the 1916 American tour, Monte Carlo's casino company and press-baron guarantors in the 1920s. *Why:* modernist design history's most famous collaborations as a funding story.
- Garafola, Lynn. *Diaghilev's Ballets Russes.* Oxford UP, 1989 (Internet Archive lending copy). [D]

## 2. The concepts

**The cost disease and the income gap.** Baumol and Bowen (1966) argue that performing arts organisations face a structural, widening gap between earned income and costs, because performers' productivity cannot rise while their wages must track the economy. The book's Part III ("Sources of financial support") makes the case for public support [D TOC; A]. Throsby (2001) adds a second axis: cultural value is not captured by price, so subsidy can be justified on cultural as well as economic grounds [A, P]. *Debate:* recordings, cinema relays and streaming (the Paris Opéra's own platform since 2023; Royal Ballet relays) are partial productivity escapes, but they raise rights and pay questions (see 3.7) [A, scope].

**Patronage shapes form.** Garafola (1989) argues that the scattered guarantors of the 1920s Ballets Russes contributed to its loss of "aesthetic coherence," a run of fashionable one-offs [A, via *Partisan Review* review]. Wilbur (2021) argues that NEA grant rules ("leveraging, touring, and incorporating") instituted norms of production that favoured proscenium ballet and modern dance and built "a growing army of intermediary presenters, managers, fund developers, and trustees" [D, A]. Klapper (2020) argues Ford's 1963 grants "cemented Balanchine's centrality" [A]. Kirstein himself compared the scholarship scheme to "the Soviet system" (TIME, Dec 1963) [D].

**The marketplace and gender.** Garafola (1985) argues that when Paris and London theatres became private enterprises, subscribers gained power, stars were paid for "drawing power rather than rank," pensions were cut "for purposes of economy," women en travesti replaced male dancers, and the ballerina became an ideal while the danseuse was "debased ... as worker." Court-funded Copenhagen and St Petersburg kept their men [D, A]. This is the strongest argument in the angle for a design-history audience: a change of business model changed the bodies on stage.

**Ballet as labour, or as a calling.** Turner and Wainwright (2003) treat ballet as a Weberian "calling" in which injury is accepted as a sign of commitment, and the corps' solidarity hides fatigue [A]. Franko (2002) reads 1930s dance through labour organisation and argues that representing work could itself count as labour [A]. Laillier (2011) shows Paris Opéra dancers rebuilding a sense of vocation ("carrières morales") when their ambitions fail [A]. Fitzgerald (Birkbeck) notes ballet is "poorly understood as 'work'" [D]. The unions' own documents (AGMA, Equity) are the counter-voice: guaranteed weeks, floors, heat limits, rest rules [D].

**Authorship as property, raced and gendered.** Kraut (2015) argues that US dance-makers "embraced intellectual property rights as a means to both consolidate and contest racial and gendered power": Loïe Fuller's 1892 suit as a white woman's claim to property; Black performers such as Johnny Hudgins seeking copyright as a defence against appropriation; Hanya Holm's 1952 registration [D blurb, A]. Picart (2013) frames copyright as "whiteness as status property" [P].

**The institution as author (work for hire).** The Graham courts held that dances made while the Center paid Graham belong to the Center [D]. The pattern is old: in 1860 Pugni contracted to compose and orchestrate "according to the orders of the Imperial Directorate" with no further fees, and Petipa's 1862 contract made composing ballets a salaried duty [D via Makarova; A, scope]. Modern union contracts reopen the question: Sarasota Ballet's CBA requires fees and royalties to be negotiated when a dancer choreographs for the company [D].

**A ballet as an inheritable asset.** Balanchine and Ashton both left ballets to individuals rather than to their companies [P]. Ashton chose bequests by expected earnings ("Oh no, that won't get you any money," of *Capriol Suite*, as recalled by heir Tony Dyson) [P]. Owners "exercise full artistic and financial control" (Ashton Foundation) [D], including over casting and design; the Royal Ballet has had to yield to the Balanchine Trust and to Cranko's heirs (Arts Desk, 2011) [P]. Kraut supplies the frame of property; the Trust's licensing pages supply the mechanism.

**Fixation: the dance has to become a document to be owned.** US law protects only what is fixed. Holm's 1952 deposit was Labanotation on microfilm [D]. Lakes (2005) argues fixation sits awkwardly with dance, because choreography is made in rehearsal on dancers and written down afterwards [D, A]. A Royal Ballet notator says the Benesh score "helps for the copyrights" [D]. Notation is therefore both a tool for memory and a title deed [A, scope].

**Reconstruction, "after Petipa," and authenticity.** The Sergeyev notations record Imperial-era revivals rather than premieres; arms and transitions are rarely written; Ratmansky "assembled" missing numbers and calls the notations "the closest we can get" [P, interview]. The 1999 Mariinsky reconstruction "generated a heated discussion" and was soon dropped [P]. Credits such as "after Marius Petipa" (Nureyev and others) keep the dead choreographer's name while the living stager owns the version [P; A, scope]. Gupta (UBC Press) treats *Sleeping Beauty* as a "living tradition" passed through five productions [D]. Croce's "magic empathy" (via Harss) is the critics' position that fidelity is more than following the score [P].

**The répétiteur as living archive and as gatekeeper.** Licensors require approved répétiteurs (Balanchine Trust; Ashton Foundation), freelance and paid separately from the licence [D]. The Balanchine Foundation films original interpreters coaching current dancers so that their knowledge outlives them [D]. *Argued here:* the répétiteur is both the archive and its lock, since the body that remembers is also the body that licenses [A, scope].

**The invisible maker.** One designer, "up to 50 makers" (ROH head of costume, 2015) [P]; makers known to ballerinas by a stamped bell, fish or crown, "paid per piece" [P]; Joan Benesh's co-invention filed under "Rudolf Benesh" in reference works [D, A]; a "dresser's diary" that turns out to have been written by a male Opéra administrator (Tamvaco, as cited in a HAL thesis) [D as cited]. These carry the course's Attribution thread [A, scope].

**Data as argument.** Dance Data Project counts are transparent but advocacy-funded and gender-binary; Dance/USA figures reach students mostly second-hand. *Concept:* a statistic is a made object with an author [A, scope].

- **Margaret Lawford,** the Fund's original Resettlement Officer (1973) [D]: the administrator of dancers' second lives.

## 6. Gaps and traps

1. **"Ballet was always state-funded."** False for the formative Romantic era: the Paris Opéra was run as a private concession from 1831 to 1854 (Drysdale; HAL thesis), and London's houses were commercial. Garafola ties the Romantic aesthetic to that market [D, A].
2. **The Louis XIV pension date is contested [C].** Press accounts give 1698 (Sud Ouest, 2023) or 1713 (actu.fr, 2023) for the pension. The Opéra and Universalis date the dance conservatory decree to 1713, but give different days (11 January in Universalis, 16 July in French Wikipedia). Retirement at 40, raised to 42 in 2010, is press-reported [P]. Check the primary *règlement* (Archives nationales / BMO) before teaching a date.
3. **"*Nutcracker* earns half of a company's revenue."** Usually "of ticket revenue" or "of season revenue"; Dance/USA's figure for total revenue is about 15% [P]. Britannica's "One Good Fact" conflates them.
4. **"Holm was the first to copyright a dance."** Say "earliest known US registration of choreography" (1952). Fuller sued in 1892 and lost; Hudgins won British protection in the 1920s (Kraut) [D/P].
5. **The choreographer as sole author.** Choreography is made on dancers; notation preserves performers' names (Kschessinska, Pavlova, Vaganova in the *Sleeping Beauty* notes) [P]. Copyright law, programme credits and licensing all erase this (Lakes; Kraut) [A].
6. **Notation as "the original."** The Stepanov scores were written in real time about 13 years after the 1890 premiere, mostly without arms, and incomplete. They are evidence, not the work [P].
7. **The archive gap at the Romantic Opéra.** The director's decision registers are missing from 1831 to 1854; police vice archives were destroyed in 1871 and 1945. Popular accounts of a Véron-designed sex market (blogs) outrun the documents. Garafola argues it was "officially countenanced" [A]. Teach as argued; cite Robin-Challan and Garafola, not blogs [C].
8. **Survivor bias in the record of ownership.** Balanchine, Ashton and Graham are fully documented because their works earn money. The claims of Black vernacular dancers survive mainly through Kraut's recovery work. Women choreographers' works are under-presented (DDP).
9. **Composite accounts hide ballet.** The RBO's accounts combine opera and ballet; the Paris subsidy line includes the Nanterre school. Students should not divide totals in two.
10. **Comparing money across centuries.** Roubles in 1860, francs in 1831, dollars in 1963: use purchasing-power tools and ratios within one payroll (for example, Rosati earned about 14 times Surovshchikova-Petipa). Do not convert naively [A, scope].
11. **Romantic tropes about makers.** "Makers never meet the dancers" (CBS) is a press angle. Freed sends fitters to companies, and makers are assigned ballerinas (Make It British) [P].
12. **Contested secondary claims.** Ezrahi's passing description of dancers as "the regime's slaves" was called "absurd" by her AHR reviewer [C]. DCD's own pages disagree on whether 1973 covered "five" or "nine" funded companies [D inconsistency]. Wikipedia's list of Sergeyev-based stagings is convenient but should be checked against production credits.
13. **Status of 2025 disputes.** The outcome of the NYCB–AGMA negotiations after November 2025 was not confirmed in this pass. Check the AGMA site before teaching.
14. **What is missing from this pass.** Danish, Cuban and Chinese state ballet economics; Dance/USA's original survey; IRS 990 figures; the IOTPD's career-length data; pointe-shoe consumption per dancer per season (a Planetary Weight hook: the shoes are hand-made, use paste, burlap and satin, and are worn out in days, but no reliable count was found); the Nureyev Foundation's legal structure.
15. **Western bias.** Japan (studio economy), Mexico (precarity studies) and the Soviet Union help, but the scholarly base for this angle is overwhelmingly US, UK and French.

## 7. Size

**Estimate:** 6–8 hours of learning in a foundation-year design course, within a larger ballet unit. That is about 2 hours of reading, 2–3 hours of taught session(s), and 2–3 hours of a data or object exercise.

- **One page could carry:** "Who owns *Swan Lake*?" Petipa's 1862 contract, then Sergeyev's notebooks, then a Balanchine Trust licence and its programme credit line, then the Freed maker's stamp. It would end with the question of whose name goes on the programme. This uses one primary image from each of Houghton, Gallica and NYPL, plus a quoted credit formula.
- **One session (2 hours) could carry:** "Follow the money." Students compare three payrolls: the Imperial Theatres 1859/60 (Makarova), an AGMA corps minimum (ABT 2024), and the RBO's 2024/25 income pie. They apply Baumol's income gap and read Garafola 1985 (6 pages, open access) on how a business model changed who danced. Output: a one-paragraph argument, with a chart, about who paid and who was paid.
- **One unit (2–3 weeks) could carry:** "Authorship and the record." Week 1: patronage and form (Ford 1963, ACGB 1946, the NEA via Wilbur). Week 2: copyright, estates and licensing (Kraut; the Graham opinions; the Ashton owners list). Week 3: the record and its makers (notation, the répétiteur archive, costume bibles, makers' marks), with an assessed piece: a "credits page" for one ballet that names every role the programme omits.
- **Must-assign readings if time is short:** Garafola 1985 (OA); Kraut 2015, introduction; one AGMA agreement (primary); the Balanchine Trust licensing page (primary); Baumol and Bowen, ch. "Anatomy of the income gap."
