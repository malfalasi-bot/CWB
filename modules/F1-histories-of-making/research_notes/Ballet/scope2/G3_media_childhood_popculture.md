# G3. How the world meets ballet without going to the theatre: media, childhood and popular culture

Gap pass for R2 §2.6 (media world), §3.3 (training as an industry; ballet and childhood), §3.4 (popular culture) and the transmedia row of §5. Written 25 October 2026. Does not repeat: Shiryaev (scope 02 §2.3), *Barbie in the Nutcracker* product facts and the *Nutcracker* licensing family (products B6), balletcore numbers (products), competitions Varna/Moscow/Jackson (scope 01), Gulf opera-house dates (scope 01 §3.5).

**One-line answer.** Most people have never sat in a ballet audience. They know ballet from pictures. First came lithographs and cartes-de-visite. Then Degas, the Ballets Russes studio portrait, *The Red Shoes*, *Fantasia*, a music box, *Angelina Ballerina*, a manga, the Christmas *Nutcracker* on television, a cinema relay, World Ballet Day, an Instagram reel. Each of these windows changed what "ballet" means to the public, and most of them were designed by someone other than a choreographer. Ballet's childhood market (classes, exams, competitions, toys, books) brings in the next dancers and the next audiences. It is also the biggest product ballet has. [A, built on the evidence below]

## 3. Stories (13)

Each is short, sourced in the log, and could open a lesson.

**S1. The wedding present Rambert could not give away (London, 1845 → 1968).** [D, V&A] Marie Rambert bought one of A. E. Chalon's 1845 *Souvenir d'adieu* lithographs of Taglioni as a wedding present for someone else. Then she "could not bear to give it away" and kept collecting. The prints hung in the bar of her Mercury Theatre until she gave them to the V&A in 1968. *Why it matters*: a souvenir made to sell one ballerina's farewell turned into a dance company's founding image and then into a national collection. The image of a world outlasted the performances it advertised.

**S2. The string under the arm (Paris, 1870s).** [D, RA] On a carte-de-visite of the star Marie Sanlaville, you can see that her raised arm is held up by a string, so that she could keep the pose through the long exposure. Degas borrowed the pose for *Two Dancers on the Stage*, painted the arm free, and in 1875 painted *Dancer Posing for a Photograph*. *Why*: the early "photograph of ballet" is a photograph of stillness, and propped stillness at that. The medium's limits wrote the image.

**S3. Pavlova plays the girl who cannot speak (Hollywood, 1916).** [D] Anna Pavlova, the most famous dancer alive, made one feature film: Universal's *The Dumb Girl of Portici*, directed by Lois Weber. She plays a mute girl and dances only in a short prologue and epilogue. *Why*: the earliest ballet star to reach a mass cinema audience did so through mime and acting, not through her dancing. A screen window chooses what it can carry.

**S4. "A thousand dance careers" (Britain and the US, 1948).** [D/P] *The Red Shoes* had a slow start in Britain, built its audience in US cinemas, and became one of the most lucrative British films. Critic Ian Christie's line that it "launched a thousand dance careers" is quoted everywhere. Moira Shearer was embarrassed by how many girls it influenced and said the dancing "wasn't terribly good". *Why*: a fiction about a ballet company did more to recruit dancers than any company did. The recruit came for the myth, and the myth was death for art.

**S5. Balanchine plays the magician on Christmas Day (CBS, 1958).** [D] On 25 December 1958, CBS's *Playhouse 90* broadcast New York City Ballet's *Nutcracker*, narrated by June Lockhart, with Balanchine himself as Drosselmeier, the godfather who brings the magic toy. The cast included Arthur Mitchell and Edward Villella and children from the School of American Ballet. *Why*: the choreographer stepped into his own world as its maker-figure, inside the family's living room on the holiday the story is set on. The ritual of the US *Nutcracker* season was built partly on television.

**S6. The swans on every channel (Moscow, 19 August 1991).** [D/C] As tanks entered Moscow, Soviet state television showed *Swan Lake*, again and again, for three days. Viewers had learned to read it: the ballet had filled the screens after the deaths of Brezhnev, Andropov and Chernenko. A Yeltsin ally saw "the swans dancing. For five minutes, 10, for an hour" and flew to Moscow. TASS later reported that the Bessmertnova recording was already scheduled for that Monday's *Musical Teletheatre*, so the first showing may have been chance. *Why*: a world can become so familiar that its appearance carries a message unrelated to its story. Here it was the state's "stand by" signal.

**S7. A country learns ballet from comics (Japan, 1971–81).** [D] In the high-growth years most Japanese girls could not take ballet; Ono Sayako notes only urban upper and new-middle-class families could. They read it instead: Yamagishi Ryōko's *Arabesque* (1971), the TBS TV drama *Akai kutsu* (1972) and Ariyoshi Kyōko's *SWAN* (from 1976). In *SWAN*, Hijiri Masumi from rural Hokkaidō is trained by a Russian star after a national competition. The Kyoto International Manga Museum says the Japanese image of ballet "spread and became the norm thanks to manga". *Why*: a medium with its own conventions (huge eyes, flowing panels, the rival, the Russian master) wrote a nation's picture of a foreign art. Japan now has thousands of studios.

**S8. The back room goes global (2012–2016).** [D, company figures] In March 2012 the Royal Ballet streamed a nine-hour day of class and rehearsal on YouTube with *The Guardian*. On 1 October 2014 five companies passed a live stream around the world, starting "at the beginning of the dancers' day" in Melbourne. Organisers counted 502,823 views in 2014. In 2016 San Francisco Ballet alone counted 385,000 viewers, "more than 120 times the capacity of its home theater". *Why*: the most hidden part of the world (the daily class) became its most watched product. Backstage turned into front stage.

**S9. 85 per cent or 5 per cent (2010–2011).** [D] After Natalie Portman won the Best Actress Oscar for *Black Swan*, choreographer Benjamin Millepied said "85% of that movie is Natalie". Her dance double, ABT soloist Sarah Lane, answered: "Of the full body shots, I would say 5 percent are Natalie". She said the studio had covered this up to help the Oscar campaign; Fox Searchlight denied it. Sixty years earlier, Powell and Pressburger had "cast dancers instead". *Why*: in screen ballet, the credit line is an argument about whose body made the world.

**S10. "You have the wrong body for ballet" (2014).** [D] Under Armour's largest women's campaign (a reported $15 m) opened with Misty Copeland rising on pointe while a child's voice reads a rejection letter listing her "wrong" feet, torso and bust. It drew 4 million YouTube views in its first week. *Why*: the world's rules of exclusion were turned into a story about getting past them, and sold sportswear.

**S11. A laugh on breakfast television (US, 22 August 2019).** [D] *Good Morning America* host Lara Spencer laughed that six-year-old Prince George was taking ballet: "We'll see how long that lasts." Dancers answered under #BoysDanceToo, Christopher Wheeldon recalled being bullied as a boy in class, and Spencer apologised. Risner's research had already documented teasing and violence against boys who dance. *Why*: one broadcast moment exposed the gender rule at the door of the world, and social media answered.

**S12. A mother, a daughter, and an argument online (Eastern Province, Saudi Arabia, 2020).** [D] A mother opened a ballet institute in the Eastern Province because her four-year-old daughter loved ballet. She told state TV it was the first of its kind in the region. Some Saudis condemned it on social media; artists, including men, posted support. A Jeddah teacher hoped for "real technical ballet, not creative commercial training". *Why*: the threshold of a world in a new place is argued in public. The argument covers whether the world belongs here at all, and also which version of it is the real one.

**S13. Ballet with poetry instead of music (London, 2019 → the Gulf?).** [D] Maisie Alexandra Byers, RAD-trained, converted to Islam and founded Grace & Poise Academy: female-only ballet classes accompanied by poetry instead of music, so girls can "train professionally within the boundaries of Islam". In 2021 she named Saudi Arabia as a planned market. *Why*: a world can be adapted by changing one of its core rules (music). That raises the design question this course asks of any world: which rules are load-bearing?

## 5. Lessons for world-builders

1. **Most people will meet your world through a window, not the original.** For ballet those windows were the print, the photograph, the film, the toy, the comic and the feed. Design the windows as carefully as the stage. They are the world for most of its public.
2. **Each medium rewrites the world to its own limits.** A long exposure needs stillness. A Disney short needs no plot. A TikTok needs a trick in seven seconds. A Soviet broadcast needs something safe. List the limits of each window before you commission it.
3. **A window can carry a meaning the world never intended.** *Swan Lake* became a Soviet emergency signal, and "Arabian Coffee" plays differently in Dubai. Once a world is famous, its images get used for other messages. Plan for that, because you cannot stop it.
4. **Showing the back room can become the main product.** World Ballet Day's daily class and the open rehearsal show this. The process of making a world is part of the world now.
5. **Stories about your world will be told by others, and their myths will stick.** *The Red Shoes*, *Black Swan* and *Billy Elliot* made ballet's stock characters. If you do not supply better stories, fiction will supply worse ones.
6. **The childhood entry tier is both how a world recruits and its biggest market.** The first class, the pink tutu, the exam and the competition bring in the next makers and audiences. They also decide who can afford to come in and which gender is welcome. Price, gender and access at the entry tier are design decisions.
7. **A world grown by many hands needs a few invariants.** *The Nutcracker* survives every remake because a few elements persist: the gift on a winter night, the toy that comes alive, the battle, the journey, the sweets. Name your invariants, and let everything else vary.
8. **Credit the bodies behind the image.** Photographers, doubles, the dancers in a stock photo and the répétiteur in a relay all shape the world, and the credit line usually forgets them. Write credits into the brief.
9. **When a world enters a new place, it gets argued over in public.** The Saudi studio and the modesty-adapted class show the question every adaptation faces: which rules carry the weight of the world, and which can bend?

## 7. Gaps and traps

**Traps (wording and dates)**
1. **"Firsts" are claims.** "The first and oldest dance school in the UAE" (The Ballet Centre Dubai: its own claim), "the first Emirati ballerina" (Grazia's word), "first of its kind in the Eastern region" (founder on state TV), Gordon Anthony's "first" UK colour photo book (Wikipedia), and the CBS *Nutcracker* as "first" on TV. NBC had already shown excerpts in 1954–55 (Balanchine Foundation). Write "earliest known" or "claimed".
2. **The brief's "cinema relays from about 2009"**: the ROH's first *live* cinema relay was September 2008 (opera; a specialist hobby site, though ROH itself says "since 2008"). NT Live began 25 June 2009. The Met's *Live in HD* began in Dec 2006. The start date of the Bolshoi's ballet relays was not confirmed (Pathé's archive lists seasons from 2015–16).
3. **The 1991 *Swan Lake***: whether the first showing was planned in advance is [C] (TASS says it was scheduled two weeks earlier). The pre-1991 funeral broadcasts are [P] from popular histories. TASS is a Russian state agency, so read it with the post-2022 caution already noted in scope 03.
4. **"Launched a thousand careers"** (*Red Shoes*) and "inspired more boys" (*Billy Elliot*) are critics' and the BFI's phrases. No enrolment data was found for either. Do not turn them into numbers.
5. **The spinning ballerina in the music box has no documented origin date.** Retail blogs disagree (mid-century; Frank Mills, 1979). Date it only from a dated museum object.
6. **Instagram's effect on technique** (extensions, turns) is [A] by critics and teachers. Body-image links are documented in small or cross-sectional studies. Causation is not shown.
7. **Company and platform figures**: WBD views, ROH's 750, 850 or 1,300 cinemas, YAGP's 5,000 entrants and $500,000, RAD's 200,000 exams, Under Armour's 4 million views, Isabella Boylston's followers. Label each as the source's figure.
8. **"Chandelier" is not ballet.** It is competition dance read as ballet by the public. The Annabelle films are not ballet either.
9. ***Nutcracker* dates**: Dumas 1844 versus 1845 [C]. Old Style and New Style dates (Suite 7/19 March 1892; ballet 6/18 December 1892).
10. **Japanese names in Japanese order**: Ariyoshi Kyōko, Yamagishi Ryōko, Itō Ikuko, Satō Jun'ichi, Asakura George, Ono Sayako, Monden Masafumi, Nakamura Kazuha (LE SSERAFIM's Kazuha; the name is from fan press [P]).
11. **Living persons and children**: the Saudi founder and her daughter are deliberately left unnamed. So are child dancers in the Riyadh and Dubai studio posts. Maddie Ziegler was 11. Use the living-persons protocol.
12. **Copyright**: Hoppé (UK to end 2042), Swope, Gordon Anthony, Greenfield, Shitagi, all manga and anime, Disney, Mattel, ROH relay footage. Use none of these as images; link only.
