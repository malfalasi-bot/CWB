# W2. Modernism, 1909–1960: the ballet as a made work

Scope: the Ballets Russes years and their aftermath, plus Soviet and American counterpoints. Each work is read as a concept product: premise, roots, originator, development, world and rules, team and credit, production, reception and afterlife, documents and open images, sources, Concept DNA reading.

Evidence labels: **[documented]** seen at source in this pass; **[probable]** from reliable secondary literature; **[contested]** both readings given; **[argued]** our interpretation. "Earliest known", never "first". Quotes kept under 25 words. URLs given only where seen in a search result.

Overlap note: the `../scope/` files already cover Diaghilev's finances, the Ballets Russes as institution, stage design in general, costume objects and authorship disputes. This file avoids repeating them and points to them where needed.

## 0. Framing documents for the period

### 0.1 Diaghilev's working method (committee, commission, season as collection)

Finance, contracts and labour are in `../scope/05`; the institution in `../scope/01`. This section is only about **how concepts were generated**.

**1. The committee.** Before 1909 the *Mir iskusstva* partners gathered in Diaghilev's St Petersburg flat. Key members: Benois and Bakst; General Bezobrazov, a privy councillor and balletomane used as "company manager"; the critic Valerian Svetlov; Walter Nouvel; at first Diaghilev's secretary Aleksey Mavrin; augmented by Fokine, the régisseur Serge Grigoriev, and occasionally Valentin Serov and Nikolai Tcherepnin [documented: text of Jane Pritchard's introduction to *Diaghilev and the Ballets Russes 1909–1929* (V&A, 2010), seen in a third-party copy; cite the book]. The committee's earliest stage project was the aborted Imperial *Sylvia* (1900–01), pushed by Nouvel and Benois, which taught the group to design a ballet as a team [documented: same]. The New Yorker's review of Buckle described *Mir iskusstva* staff meetings with "unrestrained and often explosive discussions" around the silent nanny [documented: https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1980/05/12/inside-the-ballets-russes]. *Firebird* is the type case of a committee-made scenario (§2). Garafola traces the company's origins to "Diaghilev and his circle" and to "Fokine's revolutionary secession within the Russian Imperial Ballet" [documented: publisher's description, archive.org].

**2. The commission.** Diaghilev rarely wrote a scenario. He chose *people*, then matched them: "Diaghilev commissioned ballet music" from Tcherepnin, Debussy, Ravel, Satie, Falla, Strauss, Prokofiev and others [documented: Wikipedia, Diaghilev]. He tested composers on small jobs first (Stravinsky orchestrated Chopin for *Les Sylphides*, finished by March 1909, before the *Firebird* commission) [probable: Wikipedia]. He commissioned the score to fit a slot in the season and recast when people failed (Tcherepnin, then Liadov, then Stravinsky) [documented: CSO; Britannica]. Not every idea was his: *Parade* "appears to have been initiated" by Cocteau, Picasso and Satie with support from Picasso's patron Eugenia Errázuriz before Diaghilev was involved, but "all aspects of a production had to meet Diaghilev's exacting requirements" [documented: Pritchard text]. In the late 1920s Boris Kochno took a more active role as producer [documented: same].

**3. The editor's veto.** Diaghilev's power lay in rejection and replacement: Golovin's *Firebird* costumes replaced by Bakst's [documented: NGA Australia]; Goncharova's first *Noces* designs set aside; Bauchant's *Apollon* costumes replaced by Chanel's a year later [documented: NYPL]; a Picasso gouache reassigned as a curtain [documented: V&A]. Diaghilev acted as an editor-in-chief rather than an author [argued].

**4. The season as a collection.** Ballets Russes evenings were mixed bills of short one-act works (typically three or four) rather than Petipa's evening-long ballets; the 1909 season mixed *Le Pavillon d'Armide*, the *Polovtsian Dances*, *Les Sylphides*, *Cléopâtre* and the pastiche *Le Festin* [documented: Wikipedia, Ballets Russes]. Each programme balanced registers: a Romantic "white" work, an exotic spectacle, a Russian folk piece, later a modernist "shock" [argued]. The 1909 season lost 76,000 francs, and from 1911 Diaghilev ran the company year-round on tour rather than as a summer season [documented: V&A introduction, https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/diaghilev-and-the-ballets-russes]. Seasons were also branded around themes or events (1924 coincided with the Olympics: *Le Train Bleu* and the Picasso front cloth with an Auric fanfare) [documented: V&A]. For Creative World this is the clearest historical model of a **collection map**: a portfolio of short works, each with its own world and rules, sold together under one house brand and touring as a set [argued].

**5. Diaghilev's own manifesto.** His 1898 *Mir iskusstva* programme essays (translated online by the Sheffield Russian Visual Arts Project) stand behind the whole method [documented as existing: Reynolds 2005 acknowledgements]. Chanel's remark that "Diaghilev invented Russia for foreigners" [probable: quoted in Wikipedia] states the early house concept in one line.

### 0.2 Fokine's five principles (letter to *The Times*, 6 July 1914)

**The document.** "The New Russian Ballet: Conventions in Dancing: M. Fokine's Principles and Aims", *The Times* (London), Monday 6 July 1914. A copy (4 pp.) sits in the Fokine papers at NYPL's Jerome Robbins Dance Division, finding aid dan19632 [documented: NYPL finding aid PDF, https://nyplorg-data-archives.s3.amazonaws.com/uploads/collection/generated_finding_aids/dan19632.pdf]. The same folder holds Fokine's later essays "The New Ballet" (*Argus*, 1 Nov 1916) and "The Dance is Poetry Without Words" (New York, 1924) [documented, same finding aid]. Fokine had argued the same reforms in a 1904 memorandum to the Imperial Theatres, attached to his scenario for *Daphnis and Chloë*, which was not acted on [probable: ABT biography says the ideas came "ten years" before the letter; Garafola 1989].

**The five principles, as paraphrased by ABT and Britannica** [documented: ABT, https://www.abt.org/people/michel-fokine/ ; Britannica, https://www.britannica.com/biography/Michel-Fokine]:
1. **A new form for each work.** Create "a new form of movement corresponding to the subject matter, period and character of the music", not combinations of "ready-made and established steps".
2. **Dance must carry the drama.** Dancing and mimetic gesture have no meaning unless they express dramatic action.
3. **The whole body speaks.** Conventional hand-mime only when the style demands it; otherwise the whole body: "expressive from head to foot".
4. **The group is not ornament.** Expressiveness extends from face to body, from one body to groups and crowds.
5. **Alliance of the arts on equal terms.** The new ballet refuses "to be the slave" of music or scenic decoration and grants freedom to painter and musician.

**Why it matters for this course** [argued]. This is the clearest early design brief in ballet history written as rules. Principle 1 is a demand that every work invent its own grammar: in Concept DNA terms, every ballet must have its own *grammars* field rather than inherit the Petipa house style. Principle 5 is a statement about the *team*: credit as alliance, not hierarchy. Note the timing: Fokine wrote it in London in July 1914, after his break with Diaghilev over Nijinsky's promotion, partly to claim authorship of a reform that Nijinsky's *Faune* and *Sacre* had already pushed past [probable: Britannica notes relations "deteriorated when Diaghilev launched Nijinsky as choreographer"]. A manifesto is also a credit claim.

**Anti-DNA implied by the letter** [argued]: the tutu-and-pointe uniform for all subjects; the divertissement inserted for display; the mime "language" of hand signs; music written to the ballet master's order (ironically, Fokine himself did exactly this to Stravinsky on *Firebird*, below).

## 1. *Schéhérazade* (1910)

**1. Premise.** A shah, told his wives are unfaithful, pretends to leave; the harem admits the slaves; an orgy; the shah returns and has everyone killed; Zobeide stabs herself. Makers' frame: a "choreographic drama" set to existing symphonic music [probable]. Scholars' frame: the Ballets Russes' Orient as a commodity sold to Paris, sex and violence projected onto an imagined East (Garafola 1989; the Orientalism thread in `../scope/03`) [probable].

**2. Roots.** *The Thousand and One Nights*, but via the frame story only (the shah's betrayal), not any tale Scheherazade tells; the ballet borrows the book's title and drops its narrator [argued]. Music: Rimsky-Korsakov's symphonic suite *Scheherazade*, op. 35 (1888), cut and reordered; his widow protested at what she saw as the "disarrangement" of the music [probable: Wikipedia summary of the record]. Visual: Persian miniature and Paris's fashion for the Orient; *Cléopâtre* (1909) had already proven the formula with Ida Rubinstein [documented: LoC timeline says Diaghilev contracted Rubinstein for *Cléopâtre* 1909 and her last Ballets Russes performances were *Schéhérazade* 1910, https://www.loc.gov/collections/ballets-russes-de-serge-diaghilev/articles-and-essays/timeline-of-ballets-russes/].

**3. Originator and development.** Contested. The 1910 programme credited the libretto to Bakst; Benois held that he had written the scenario, and the misattribution was one cause of his 1910–11 estrangement from Diaghilev [contested: Benois, *Reminiscences of the Russian Ballet* (1941) gives his version; most secondary accounts, e.g. Garafola 1989 and Scheijen 2009, accept Benois's role; the credit line still often reads "Fokine and Bakst"]. Fokine shaped the action to the music. Diaghilev chose the music, which places the commission upstream of the story [probable].

**4. The world it built.** Setting: one enclosed harem room, green walls, a huge orange-red carpet, hanging lamps, the vanishing point closed by blue doors [probable from surviving designs]. Palette: saturated complementaries (emerald, vermilion, orange, violet) against the pastel Belle Époque; contemporaries called it "a perfect tornado of color" and Bakst an "erotomaniac" [documented: Met Perspectives, https://www.metmuseum.org/perspectives/leon-bakst-designs-ballets-russes]. Movement: no pointe-work for the harem, body waves, closely partnered contact, crowd scenes; the male star (Nijinsky, Golden Slave) as erotic object [probable]. Rules [argued]: everything happens in one room; colour does the storytelling; dance is mime-driven and ensemble-heavy (Fokine principles 2–4 in action). Anti-DNA [argued]: no classical vocabulary, no white tutu, no happy ending, no individual virtue.

**5. Team and credit.** Music Rimsky-Korsakov (posthumous, unconsenting); libretto Bakst (credited) / Benois (claimed); choreography Fokine; sets and costumes Bakst; Zobeide Ida Rubinstein; Golden Slave Nijinsky; Shah Alexis Bulgakov [probable]. Dropped from the credit: the scene painters and costume makers (see `../scope/04` §1.4 on executants).

**6. Production.** Premiere 4 June 1910, Théâtre national de l'Opéra (Palais Garnier), Paris [documented: Histoire des arts / Philharmonie de Paris, https://histoiredesarts.culture.gouv.fr/Toutes-les-ressources/Philharmonie-Cite-de-la-musique/Rimski-Korsakov-Sheherazade]. Audience: the Paris *tout-Paris* subscription public that the 1909 season had built [probable].

**7. Reception and afterlife.** An immediate hit and the company's longest-lived "exotic" title. Its strongest afterlife is in fashion and interiors: Poiret's harem trousers, lampshade tunics and the "Thousand and Second Night" party (1911) [documented in `../scope/06`, Met Heilbrunn Timeline; not repeated here]. Revived by the Mariinsky (Andris Liepa reconstruction lineage) and others [probable]. Today taught chiefly as a case study in Orientalism (Said 1978; Garafola) [probable].

**8. Documents and images.** Bakst set design variant (McNay Art Museum, Tobin gift) [documented: NGA brochure, https://www.nga.gov/sites/default/files/2025-07/diaghilev-brochure.pdf]. Bakst died 1924: his designs are public domain in life+70 and life+80 jurisdictions; check each museum's own licence (Met items marked "Public Domain" are CC0 under Open Access) [argued from rights rules]. George Barbier's pochoir of Nijinsky as the Golden Slave (Met 1978.567) [documented: Met Perspectives]. LoC "Ballets Russes de Serge Diaghilev" digital collection holds programmes [documented].

**9. Best sources.** Lynn Garafola, *Diaghilev's Ballets Russes* (OUP, 1989; borrowable on archive.org) [documented: archive.org record, https://archive.org/details/diaghilevsballet1989gara]; Alexandre Benois, *Reminiscences of the Russian Ballet* (1941) [probable]; Charles S. Mayer, "Ida Rubinstein: A Twentieth-Century Cleopatra", *Dance Research Journal* 20/2 (1988) [probable]; Hanna Järvinen, *Dancing Genius* (Palgrave, 2014) for the "Russianness" and exoticism problem [probable].

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].** *Premise*: desire and punishment in a closed room. *Perimeter*: one room, one night. *Rules*: colour is the narrator. *Anti-DNA*: classical ballet's purity. *Adjacent translation*: the strongest of any work here, out into couture and interiors within twelve months. *Rights and money*: a dead composer's music used against his family's wishes; the credit dispute is a *handover* failure.

## 2. *The Firebird* / *L'Oiseau de feu* (1910)

**1. Premise.** Ivan Tsarevich, hunting, captures and frees the Firebird, who gives him a feather; with it he breaks the spell of the sorcerer Kashchei the Deathless, frees thirteen princesses and marries the Tsarevna. Makers' frame: the earliest Russian ballet made *as Russian* for export, with a new Russian score. The Mariinsky's own programme calls it "an export Russian fairy tale" that "conquered Paris" [documented: https://www.mariinsky.ru/en/playbill/repertoire/ballet/jarpt/]. Scholars (Taruskin 1996) read it as Rimsky-Korsakov's musical language, a Kuchkist fairy-opera made into ballet [probable; CSO quotes Francis Maes calling the score "based on the models of the operas of Rimsky-Korsakov", https://cso.org/experience/article/6677/the-legacy-of-composer-anatoly-liadov-floats].

**2. Roots.**
- **Poem**: Benois recalled that Pyotr Potemkin, a poet in Diaghilev's circle, proposed the subject, citing Yakov Polonsky's poem "A Winter's Journey" (1844): the dreamer rides "on a wolf's back" to fight "a sorcerer-tsar" where "Firebirds sing by night" [probable: Wikipedia citing Benois; the same lines are quoted by NYCB and the Houston Symphony].
- **Tales**: Alexander Afanasyev's *Russian Folk Tales* (1855–63), chiefly "Koshchei the Deathless" and "The Tale of Ivan Tsarevich, the Firebird and the Gray Wolf"; also Pyotr Yershov's verse tale *The Little Humpbacked Horse* (1834) [probable: Wikipedia; tale titles documented in the Project MUSE table of contents of *The Complete Folktales of A. N. Afanas'ev*, https://muse.jhu.edu/book/35450]. The ballet merges at least two tale-types and adds the thirteen princesses and Kashchei's soul in an egg [probable].
- **Music**: Rimsky-Korsakov (Stravinsky's teacher, d. 1908); the Infernal Dance reworks a passage from Rimsky's *Mlada*; the Khorovod uses a folk song Rimsky also used [probable: Houston Symphony notes].
- **Visual**: the neo-Russian craft revival at Abramtsevo and Talashkino, through Golovin; Ivan Bilibin's tale illustrations as the shared image-world [documented for Golovin's Abramtsevo/Talashkino background: NGA Australia, https://digital.nga.gov.au/archive/exhibition/balletsrusses/default.cfm%3Fmnuid=3&galid=7.html].

**3. Originator and development.** The idea came from the group, not from one person. Over the winter of 1908–09 Diaghilev's friends met, informally led by Fokine, to devise a scenario: Fokine, Benois, Nikolai Tcherepnin, Golovin, with the sculptor Dmitri Stelletsky and the writer Walter Nouvel and others contributing [probable: Wikipedia; NYCB lists Fokine, Benois, Tcherepnin, Bakst, Nouvel, Golovin]. The writer Aleksei Remizov is also named in the literature as consulted on the tale [probable; Taruskin]. Music: first Tcherepnin (withdrew), then Anatoly Liadov, who did not deliver; it is "not known" whether Liadov declined or failed [documented: CSO]. Stravinsky, aged 27, received the commission in December 1909 (Britannica) [documented: https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Firebird]. Score finished by May 1910. **Method**: Fokine dictated to the composer passage by passage. Fokine recalled asking that Ivan's folk melody be given "just a hint of it, by means of separate notes" as Ivan appears at the wall, with Stravinsky playing and Fokine acting the scene, "the piano substituting for the wall" [documented: quoted by Houston Symphony, https://houstonsymphony.org/stravinsky-firebird-complete]. NYCB notes Stravinsky would "decidedly avoid" this arrangement afterwards [documented: https://www.nycballet.com/discover/stories/spotlight-on-firebird]. **Abandoned direction**: Golovin's costumes for the Firebird, Tsarevna and Ivan were replaced by Bakst's on Diaghilev's orders [documented: NGA Australia; LoC timeline]. Pavlova refused the title role, reportedly disliking the music; Karsavina danced it [probable: Britannica says "the principal ballerina" refused].

**4. The world it built.** Setting: Kashchei's overgrown, sinister garden with the golden apple tree, a "rich and decorative Symbolist décor" [documented: NGA Australia]. Costume language: Golovin's Slavic festival dress, white belted tunics stencilled to imitate brocade and braid, hooped overdresses [documented: NGA Australia]; Bakst's Firebird in red-orange with feathered headdress and harem trousers. Movement grammar: Fokine split the world into two vocabularies, which became the model [argued]: the Firebird on pointe with classical virtuosity (the supernatural), the princesses in barefoot-style round dance (the human, folk), and Kashchei's grotesque monsters in angular character dance. Music grammar: chromatic, "magic" harmony for the supernatural, diatonic folk tunes for the humans, a direct inheritance from Rimsky [probable]. Anti-DNA [argued]: no French or Italian vocabulary as default; no inserted divertissement; no imported story.

**5. Team and credit.** Music Stravinsky; scenario "Fokine" in most credits [documented: NGA Australia "Libretto: Michel Fokine, from a Russian folk tale"; Mariinsky "Libretto by Michel Fokine"], although the scenario was a committee product; choreography Fokine; sets Golovin; costumes Golovin and Bakst; scene painting Nikolai Sapunov executed Golovin's cloth [documented in `../scope/04` §1.4]; conductor Gabriel Pierné [documented: Histoire des arts]; Firebird Tamara Karsavina, Ivan Fokine. **Who is dropped**: Potemkin, Remizov, Stelletsky, Nouvel, Tcherepnin; Golovin often loses the costumes to Bakst. Critic Henri Ghéon wrote that "Stravinsky, Fokine and Golovin, in my eyes, are but one name" [documented: quoted in NGA Australia].

**6. Production.** Premiere 25 June 1910, Théâtre national de l'Opéra (Palais Garnier) [documented: NGA Australia; Britannica]. Made for Paris, not Petersburg: it was not staged in Russia until Fedor Lopukhov's avant-garde version (Petrograd, 1921) and entered the Mariinsky repertoire in Fokine's version only in 1994 (Andris Liepa reconstruction, 28 May 1994) [documented: Mariinsky].

**7. Reception and afterlife.** Immediate success; Diaghilev at rehearsal: "Mark him well, he is a man on the eve of celebrity" [probable: Wikipedia quoting memoirs]. Remakes: Goncharova's new sets and costumes for the 1926 revival (Lyceum Theatre, London, 25 Nov 1926), some made by altering 1910 costumes with new trims; costumier Vera Sudeikina [documented: NGA Australia]. Golovin's backdrop reused by the Monte Carlo Russian Ballet in Melbourne, 1936 [documented: NGA Australia]. Adolph Bolm's 1945 Ballet Theatre version with Chagall designs; Balanchine's 1949 version for Ballet Society/NYCB reused the Chagall designs, with Maria Tallchief, and Balanchine said of the tale: "You can't make a ballet out of it" [documented: NYCB]. Concert suites (1911, 1919, 1945) made the music travel without the dance [documented: Indianapolis Symphony]. Adjacent translation: the Firebird as a brand of Russianness, from Fabergé-style souvenirs to the Disney *Fantasia 2000* finale [probable].

**8. Documents and images.** Golovin and Bakst designs: Bakhrushin Theatre Museum (Moscow), St Petersburg State Museum of Theatre and Music, V&A, NGA Australia [probable]. Both designers died before 1931, so designs are PD in most jurisdictions; museum photographs may carry their own terms [argued]. V&A holds Goncharova's 1926 *Firebird* backcloth (non-commercial up to 768 px) [documented in `../scope/04`]. LoC holds the 1910 souvenir programme [documented: LoC timeline references "Souvenir Program The Firebird"]. NGA Australia's online exhibition "Ballets Russes: The Art of Costume" has object records [documented].

**9. Best sources.** Richard Taruskin, *Stravinsky and the Russian Traditions* (2 vols, University of California Press, 1996) [probable]; Stephen Walsh, *Stravinsky: A Creative Spring* (Knopf, 1999) [probable]; Michel Fokine, *Memoirs of a Ballet Master* (1961) [probable]; Tim Scholl, *From Petipa to Balanchine* (Routledge, 1994) [probable]; *The Complete Folktales of A. N. Afanas'ev*, ed. Jack Haney (University Press of Mississippi, 2014–15; on Project MUSE) [documented].

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].** *Premise*: a national fairy tale for a foreign audience. *Sources*: a poem as trigger, a folk-tale collection as quarry. *Grammars*: two vocabularies (classical for magic, folk for human) as the core rule. *Handover*: the committee to the choreographer to the composer, with the choreographer setting the musical rules. *Lifecycle*: the most remade score of the century; each new choreographer and designer renews the "Russian" look.

## 3. *Petrushka* (1911)

**1. Premise.** At the Shrovetide fair in 1830s St Petersburg, a showman brings three puppets to life: Petrushka, the Ballerina and the Moor. Petrushka loves the Ballerina, she prefers the Moor, the Moor kills Petrushka in the crowd, and Petrushka's ghost jeers at the showman from the booth roof. Stravinsky's frame: "a puppet suddenly endowed with life" [documented: Boosey & Hawkes repertoire note, https://www.boosey.com/cr/music/Igor-Stravinsky-Petrushka/4358]. Scholars' frame: the artist as soul trapped in a mechanism; Russian popular theatre (*balagan*) as a modernist source (Taruskin; Catriona Kelly, *Petrushka: The Russian Carnival Puppet Theatre*, 1990) [probable].

**2. Roots.** The Russian glove-puppet Petrushka, cousin of Pulcinella and Punch [documented: LA Phil notes the commedia descent]. The Maslenitsa (Butter Week) fairground booths (*balagany*) on Admiralty Square, which Benois remembered from childhood; the fair had in fact moved to the Field of Mars by his time, but Benois always set the ballet on Admiralty Square [probable: Simple Wikipedia summarising Benois]. Street-hawker cries and popular songs in the score, including a French music-hall tune ("Elle avait un' jambe en bois") that led to a royalty claim [probable]. The Romantic doll-ballet line (*Coppélia*) answered and inverted: the doll is the one with a soul [argued].

**3. Originator and development.** **Documented sequence.** Summer 1910, at Lausanne/Clarens, Stravinsky wrote a concert piece for piano and orchestra; he then named it after the puppet. When Diaghilev visited, expecting sketches of the *Sacre*, Stravinsky played the piece instead; Diaghilev "would not leave it alone" and persuaded him to develop "the puppet's sufferings" into a ballet [documented: Stravinsky's *Autobiography* quoted by the BSO, https://www.bso.org/works/petrushka-1947-version-2]. Composed between August 1910 and 26 May 1911 at Lausanne, Clarens, Beaulieu and Rome [documented: BSO]. Diaghilev brought in Benois, who had broken with him over *Schéhérazade*; Benois called Petrushka "my friend since my earliest childhood" [documented: BSO]. Benois gave the puppet a full-length body and the two companions, and drew while the libretto was written, so his colleagues saw the world before it was danced [probable: Simple Wikipedia]. Stravinsky had written much of the music, chiefly the second tableau, before Benois joined [probable: Wikipedia]. **Credit dispute**: after the premiere Stravinsky and Benois each attributed the libretto to the other [probable]. The score is dedicated to Benois, whose name appears on the title page as co-author of the "scènes burlesques" [documented: BSO].

**4. The world it built.** Two nested worlds, which is the ballet's central rule [argued]. The public world: the fair, a crowd of coachmen, nursemaids, gypsies, a dancing bear, merchants, all in character dance; and the private world: the puppets' cells, a black room with a devil-portrait for Petrushka and a gaudy room for the Moor. Music: the "Petrushka chord", C major and F-sharp major triads together, as the puppet's signature [documented: LA Phil, https://www.laphil.com/works/petrushka]. Movement: Petrushka's turned-in, broken-wristed, rag-doll body, the Ballerina's wooden classical steps (classical technique as mechanism), the Moor's splayed, turned-out-and-in brutality. Fokine used the classical vocabulary *as parody* for a doll [argued]. Palette: Benois's painted fairground booths and drop curtain (the showman as a demon in the clouds), strong folk-print colour. Anti-DNA [argued]: no hero, no happy wedding, no apotheosis except the ghost's mockery.

**5. Team and credit.** Music Stravinsky; scenario Stravinsky and Benois [documented: LoC Nijinska Collection essay, https://www.loc.gov/collections/bronislava-nijinska/articles-and-essays/petrouchka]; sets and costumes Benois; choreography Fokine; conductor Pierre Monteux; Petrushka Nijinsky, Ballerina Karsavina, Moor Alexander Orlov, Showman Enrico Cecchetti [documented: Wikipedia; Boosey]. Benois designed eleven productions over his life, always from the 1911 model [probable: Simple Wikipedia]. The drop curtain was painted in Paris workshops; executants usually unnamed [argued].

**6. Production.** Premiere 13 June 1911, Théâtre du Châtelet, Paris [documented: LoC; Boosey]. Rehearsals partly in Rome and on the stage of the Paris Opéra [documented: BSO quotes Fokine]. The orchestra at first refused to play, until Diaghilev arrived [documented: Nijinska's memoir quoted by LoC].

**7. Reception and afterlife.** Triumph; Nijinska: "a triumph for Stravinsky, for Benois, for Fokine" [documented: LoC]. *Petrouchka* became the earliest known work fully notated in Benesh (1957) [documented in `../scope/02`]. Fokine restaged it himself (Royal Danish Ballet 1925, Ballet Theatre 1942); the Joffrey's 1970 revival with Rudolf Nureyev [probable]. Stravinsky's *Trois mouvements de Pétrouchka* for piano (1921, for Arthur Rubinstein) and the 1947 revised score turned it back into concert music [probable]. Adjacent translation: the sad clown and the puppet with a soul pass into film (Chaplin) and puppetry discourse [argued].

**8. Documents and images.** LoC Bronislava Nijinska Collection: photograph of Nijinsky as Petrushka, 1911, Music Division [documented]. Benois designs: Bakhrushin; St Petersburg Theatre Museum; Wadsworth Atheneum (Lifar collection) [probable]. Benois died 1960, so his designs remain in copyright in life+70 regions until 2031 [argued]. Photographs of 1911 dancers are typically PD [argued].

**9. Best sources.** Stravinsky, *An Autobiography* (1936) [documented via BSO quotation]; Benois, *Reminiscences of the Russian Ballet* (1941); Andrew Wachtel ed., *Petrushka: Sources and Contexts* (Northwestern UP, 1998) [probable]; Catriona Kelly, *Petrushka: The Russian Carnival Puppet Theatre* (CUP, 1990) [probable]; Taruskin 1996.

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].** *Origin*: a sound before a story; the concept was found in a piece of music and then given a world. *Perimeter*: one fair, four days of Butter Week, a booth and its cells. *Rules*: public crowd vs private soul; classical technique as mechanism. *Obligations*: Benois's memory as the source of truth for the look. *Lifecycle*: the designer kept redesigning the same world for 40 years: one person as the guardian of the canon.

## 4. *L'Après-midi d'un faune* (1912)

**1. Premise.** A faun on a hillock, idling, watches seven nymphs on their way to bathe; he approaches, they flee; one returns, flirts, flees and drops her scarf; he carries it back to the rock and lies on it in a final erotic gesture [documented: NGA Australia, https://digital.nga.gov.au/archive/exhibition/balletsrusses/default.cfm%3Fmnuid=3&galid=14.html]. About eleven to twelve minutes of dance. Makers' frame: a living bas-relief. Scholars' frame: the earliest known choreography in the Ballets Russes to refuse the classical vocabulary wholesale; Järvinen argues the scandal came because it did *not* fit the erotic spectacle expected of the company [documented: Järvinen, "On Nijinsky's 'L'Après-midi d'un Faune' (1912)", 2009, JSTOR 40264006 abstract].

**2. Roots.** Stéphane Mallarmé's eclogue *L'Après-midi d'un faune* (1876); Debussy's *Prélude à l'après-midi d'un faune* (1894), chosen by Diaghilev [documented: NGA Australia]. Visual: Greek red- and black-figure vases, Egyptian and Assyrian reliefs seen in the Louvre (the Campana collection) by Bakst, Nijinsky and Diaghilev [probable: Wikipedia; NGA Australia says "frozen attitudes of Greek dancers seen in Attic art"]. Answer to: Isadora Duncan's free "Greek" dancing and its imitators; Grace Robert argued the ballet was an "aesthetic antidote" to them [probable]. Also to Bakst's own earlier *Narcisse* (1911) [documented: NGA Australia].

**3. Originator and development.** Contested authorship. Diaghilev, Nijinsky and Bakst developed the idea together from the Louvre visits [probable]. Grace Robert (*Borzoi Book of Ballet*) claimed Bakst made the choreography and Nijinsky executed it; Bronislava Nijinska said Bakst and Diaghilev saw rehearsals only at the end, and that she and her brother worked it out alone [contested: both reported in Simple Wikipedia]. Nijinska's *Early Memoirs* (1981) records her brother working out the poses on her body in 1911 [probable]. More than 90 rehearsals were needed for a short work, which was unprecedented [probable: commonly cited from Buckle]. Nijinsky told the dancers not to act: "It is all in the choreography" [documented: History Today, https://www.historytoday.com/archive/premiere-l%E2%80%99apr%C3%A8s-midi-d%E2%80%99un-faune].

**4. The world it built.** **Rules** [documented in part, argued in part]: bodies move in profile, as on a frieze, the torso turned frontally and the legs and head in profile; feet bare, flat, walking on the heel; arms angular; movement in a shallow strip downstage, parallel to the footlights; no jumps except the faun's one leap; no turnout; the music's flowing line set against a deliberately jerky, stop-start movement. Rodin praised the "deliberately awkward" and "jerky" movement [documented: Met, Rodin *Nijinsky (?)*, https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/208044]. **Palette**: Bakst's layered, "darkly glowing Symbolist set" in blotched greens, ochre and russet; the nymphs in pleated, metallic-patterned tunics, "architectural" underskirts; the faun in a piebald body-stocking, short tail, vine leaves, horned wig [documented: NGA Australia]. **Anti-DNA**: classical technique itself; virtuosity; the narrative climax at full stage. The grammar is two-dimensional against ballet's three-dimensional display [argued].

**5. Team and credit.** Choreography Nijinsky (also the Faun); music Debussy; text source Mallarmé; sets, costumes and programme illustration Bakst; Head Nymph Lydia Nelidova; Nijinska was cast as a nymph but, pregnant, did not dance the premiere [probable]. Photographs Baron Adolph de Meyer (album 1914) [probable]. Debussy disliked the result [probable].

**6. Production.** Premiere 29 May 1912, Théâtre du Châtelet, Paris [documented: Met; NGA Australia]. The ballet was repeated at once in the same evening after a mixed reaction [probable].

**7. Reception and afterlife.** **The scandal.** Gaston Calmette's front-page *Figaro* attack (30 May) condemned "vile movements of erotic bestiality" [documented: History Today quotation]. Rodin's defence appeared the same morning, 30 May, in *Le Matin* under his name (the text was probably drafted by the critic Roger Marx) [probable; the Met says only that "the following morning, a front-page newspaper editorial signed by Rodin" praised it]. Calmette then attacked Rodin, which turned the affair into a political quarrel about the state-funded Hôtel Biron [documented: Järvinen abstract notes Calmette's "faux pas" in turning on Rodin]. Police attended the second performance, which sold out [probable: History Today]. **Notation and reconstruction**: Nijinsky notated *Faune* in his own system in 1915, when interned in Budapest; Ann Hutchinson Guest and Claudia Jeschke decoded it in the late 1980s and published *Nijinsky's Faune Restored* (Gordon and Breach, 1991), with the score transcribed into Labanotation [documented: book review, EUP, https://www.euppublishing.com/doi/pdf/10.2307/1290684; Google Books record]. Earlier, the Marie Rambert lineage survived through Elisabeth Schooling and William Chappell, whose reconstruction the Joffrey Ballet danced (filmed for NYPL, 15 March 1991) [documented: NYPL Digital Collections record]. So at least two versions claim to be "the" *Faune*: the memory lineage (Rambert/Schooling/Chappell) and the notation lineage (Guest/Jeschke) [argued]. Remakes: Jerome Robbins's *Afternoon of a Faun* (1953), dancers in a studio before a mirror (the fourth wall as mirror) [probable]. Rodin's small sculpture of Nijinsky (1912) [documented: Met].

**8. Documents and images.** Nijinsky's notation manuscript (British Library holds the score; Romola Nijinsky's materials) [probable]. De Meyer photographs (1912, published 1914): PD in most places [argued]. Met, Rodin *Nijinsky (?)*, 1991.446: "Public Domain" with download [documented]. NYPL Joffrey video (restricted viewing) [documented]. Bakst programme illustration: PD [argued].

**9. Best sources.** Hanna Järvinen, "On Nijinsky's *L'Après-midi d'un Faune* (1912)", *Dance Research* 27/1 (2009) [documented: JSTOR]; Ann Hutchinson Guest and Claudia Jeschke, *Nijinsky's Faune Restored* (1991) [documented]; Jean-Michel Nectoux ed., *Nijinsky: Prélude à l'après-midi d'un faune* (Thames & Hudson, 1990) [documented: named in the EUP review]; Bronislava Nijinska, *Early Memoirs* (1981) [probable]; Richard Buckle, *Nijinsky* (1971) [probable].

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].** *Sources*: a poem, a piece of music and a museum gallery. *Rules*: a short list of physical constraints (profile, flat feet, no turnout, one leap) that a student can copy. *Anti-DNA*: the whole classical vocabulary. *Obligations*: fidelity to an image (the vase) rather than to a story. *Grammars*: the earliest example in this file of a work whose grammar *is* the concept. *Lifecycle*: two rival reconstructions, plus Robbins's re-reading.

## 5. *The Rite of Spring* / *Le Sacre du printemps* (1913)

**1. Premise.** "Pictures of Pagan Russia in Two Parts": Part I, *The Adoration of the Earth* (augurs, abduction, spring rounds, rival tribes, the Sage's kiss to the earth); Part II, *The Sacrifice* (the maidens' mystic circles, the choice of the Chosen One, the ancestors, and her sacrificial dance to death) [documented: Britannica list of numbers, https://www.britannica.com/topic/The-Rite-of-Spring]. Stravinsky's frame (1914, to Koussevitzky): "the mystery and great surge of the creative power of Spring" [probable: quoted LibreTexts]. Roerich's frame: "scenes of the joy of Earth and the exultation of Sky in a Slavic context" [probable: Roerich statement quoted by Northwestern, https://max.mmlc.northwestern.edu/mdenner/Drama/plays/spring/1spring.html]. Scholars: Taruskin reads it as the culmination of Silver Age "neonationalism", the professional appropriation of folk art (UC Press summary of *Stravinsky and the Russian Traditions*) [documented: https://www.ucpress.edu/books/stravinsky-and-the-russian-traditions-volume-one/epub-pdf]; others read it as modernism's primitivist myth of collective violence (Eksteins, *Rites of Spring*, 1989) [probable].

**2. Roots.**
- **Archaeology and ethnography (Roerich).** Roerich trained as an archaeologist, excavated burial mounds, painted the "Stone Age" Slavs (e.g. *The Idols*, 1901) and had designed Diaghilev's 1909 *Polovtsian Dances* [probable: Wikipedia; "foremost Russian expert on folk art and ancient rituals"]. Sources Roerich and Stravinsky drew on include the *Primary Chronicle* (12th century) and Afanasyev's *The Poetic Views of the Slavs on Nature* (1865–69), the study of pagan survivals in peasant custom [probable: Wikipedia following Taruskin].
- **Craft revival (Talashkino).** Princess Maria Tenisheva's estate near Smolensk, with craft workshops from 1900 (ceramics, carving, embroidery) under Sergei Maliutin, then under Roerich's influence after 1903; a museum of "ethnographic art and art-crafts" later moved to Smolensk; Roerich painted the murals and mosaics of the estate's Church of the Holy Spirit [documented: Roerich's own essay "Talashkino" (*The Scholar*, 1941 reprint), Nicholas Roerich Museum archive PDF, https://nrm.s3.amazonaws.com/website/archive/01_Nicholas_Roerich%27s_writings/Periodicals%20-%20articles%20of%20N.Roerich/N.%20Roerich%20-%20Talashkino%20%28reprint%20from%20The%20Scholar%2C%201941%29.pdf; probable for dates: Wikipedia "Talashkino"]. Tenisheva was a sponsor of *Mir iskusstva* [probable].
- **Folk music.** The opening bassoon melody is from Anton Juszkiewicz's (Antanas Juška's) *Melodje ludowe litewskie* (Kraków, 1900), daina no. 157; Stravinsky admitted only that one; Lawrence Morton and later scholars found more (about six dainos in all) and Taruskin found Russian prototypes [documented: Draugas News summary, https://www.draugas.org/news/the-lithuanian-roots-of-igor-stravinsky-and-the-rite-of-spring; Taruskin, "Russian Folk Melodies in *The Rite of Spring*", *JAMS* 33/3 (1980) 501–543, JSTOR 831304]. Stravinsky later called himself "the vessel through which *Le Sacre* passed" [documented: Mariinsky programme quoting *Dialogues*].
- **Earlier works it answers.** The Romantic "dancing to death" (the Wilis of *Giselle*) recast as collective ritual rather than individual love [argued; also noted by Houston Symphony notes].

**3. Originator and development.** **Contested origin.**
- *Stravinsky's version*: while finishing *Firebird* in spring 1910 he had "a fleeting vision": "sage elders, seated in a circle, watched a young girl dance herself to death" [documented: *Autobiography*, quoted by Houston Symphony, https://houstonsymphony.org/stravinsky-rite-of-spring/]. In the 1913 *Montjoie!* interview with Ricciotto Canudo he said instead that his first idea had been *musical* [documented: van den Toorn, *Stravinsky and The Rite of Spring*, UC Press 1987, open e-book, https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/public/book/stravinsky-and-the-rite-of-spring.html].
- *Roerich's version*: the ballet was "conceived by Stravinsky and myself"; the dances of the scenes were his [probable: Roerich statement quoted by Northwestern]. Taruskin argues that Roerich's role in the concept was larger than Stravinsky later allowed, and that the idea fits Roerich's prior painting and writing [probable; Taruskin 1996]. Van den Toorn summarises: the two-part division (day and night, with the sacrifice moved to Part II) was Stravinsky's; the rites of the individual numbers were "in large part suggested by Roerich" [documented: van den Toorn, ch. 1].
- *Our reading* [argued]: the composer had an image; the painter-archaeologist had a world. Neither alone is the concept.
**Documented sequence.** May 1910: Stravinsky discusses the idea with Roerich [probable: Wikipedia]. Working title *Velikaia zhertva* (The Great Sacrifice); 9 Aug 1910 letter: "I have started work (sketches) on the Great Sacrifice", calling it "our child" [documented: van den Toorn quoting the letter]. Diaghilev hears of it in Lausanne, late 1910, but *Petrushka* intervenes [documented: Mariinsky quoting Stravinsky]. July 1911: Stravinsky goes to Talashkino to meet Roerich, and "in a few days' time the plan of the action onstage and the names of the dances had been worked out"; Roerich sketched backdrops and costumes "based on actual examples in the collection of Princess Tenisheva" [documented: Mariinsky programme quoting Stravinsky, https://www.mariinsky.ru/en/playbill/repertoire/ballet/le_sacre_du_printemps]. Russian title becomes *Vesna sviashchennaia* (Sacred Spring); the French *Le Sacre du printemps* was Bakst's coinage, and Stravinsky said "The Coronation of Spring" was closer to his idea [documented: same]. 26 Sept 1911, letter to Roerich: "The picture of the old woman in a squirrel fur sticks in my mind" [documented: van den Toorn]. Score sketched at Ustilug and Clarens, 1911–12 [probable]. Postponed from 1912 to 1913 because Nijinsky's *Faune* and Fokine's ballets took the 1912 season [probable]. Debussy, after playing it four-hands with Stravinsky: "It haunts me like a good nightmare" [documented: Houston Symphony quotation].

**Choreography.** Nijinsky made it in roughly 120 rehearsals over winter 1912–13, with Marie Rambert brought in from Émile Jaques-Dalcroze's Hellerau institute as rhythm assistant to help the dancers count the score [probable: Rambert, *Quicksilver*, 1972; Hodson 1996]. Rambert remembered the basic stance: feet turned in, the body bent at the torso, "a questioning" [documented: All Arts/NYPL exhibition account, https://www.allarts.org/2024/11/joffrey-riteofspring-nypl-lpa/]. Hodson traced specific group shapes to Roerich's paintings and designs [documented: title and abstract of Hodson, "Nijinsky's Choreographic Method: Visual Sources from Roerich for *Le Sacre du printemps*", *Dance Research Journal* 18/2 (1986–87), Cambridge Core].

**4. The world it built.** Setting: Part I a green hill with rocks under a big sky; Part II a sacred hill at night. Palette: Roerich's earth reds, ochres, greens and dark blues; costumes of loose smocks, long shirts, felt-like fabrics with hand-painted or stencilled geometric borders and circles, bast shoes, braided wigs, hats; the elders in bearskins (the bear as forefather) [probable: Roerich's own description quoted by Northwestern; surviving costumes]. Movement grammar [probable/argued]: turned-in feet; heavy, earthbound jumps landing flat; heads tilted on the hand; elbows bent; stamping; mass groups moving in circles and blocks; the soloist the opposite of a ballerina (convulsive leaps, no line). Music grammar: ostinati, additive and shifting metres, polychords (the "Augurs" chord), short folk cells repeated rather than developed (the JSTOR excerpt on short "primitive motives" repeated "à la ostinato") [documented in part]. Anti-DNA [argued]: turnout, verticality, elevation as lightness, the individual hero, mime, a love story, and beauty as the aim. The concept *inverts every term* of the ballet blanc.

**5. Team and credit.** Music Stravinsky; scenario Stravinsky and Roerich (Roerich's name on the title page of the earliest published score with the composer's) [documented: Britannica]; sets and costumes Roerich; choreography Nijinsky; rhythm assistant Rambert; conductor Pierre Monteux; Chosen One Maria Piltz; producer Diaghilev; title by Bakst [documented: Mariinsky quotation; others probable]. Costume makers and painters are not usually named [argued]. The French critic Jacques Rivière's essay "Le Sacre du printemps" (*NRF*, Nov 1913) became the first serious reading [probable].

**6. Production.** Premiere 29 May 1913, Théâtre des Champs-Élysées, Paris, newly opened that spring by Gabriel Astruc [documented for date and theatre: Britannica; probable for Astruc]. Danced only about eight times in 1913 (Paris and London) before it was dropped [probable; Britannica says "only seven performances"]. Audience: a split house of fashionable subscribers and young avant-garde partisans; the uproar began before the curtain rose and grew with the stamping "Augurs" [documented: LibreTexts summary of eyewitnesses]. Taruskin: "it was not Stravinsky's music that did the shocking" [documented: LibreTexts quotation].

**7. Reception and afterlife.**
- **The score lived, the dance died.** Concert premiere April 1914 (Monteux, Paris) made the music canonical [probable]. Nijinsky's choreography lost.
- **Massine 1920**: a new choreography for Diaghilev with Roerich's designs [documented: Britannica says it was "superseded by new choreography from Léonide Massine"].
- **Hodson–Archer 1987**: Millicent Hodson began intensive research in 1979 (doctoral thesis *Nijinsky's New Dance*, UC Berkeley) and with Kenneth Archer (Roerich scholar, sets and costumes) rebuilt it for the Joffrey Ballet; premiere Los Angeles, 30 Sept 1987 [documented: NYT 25 Oct 1987; All Arts]. Sources: prompt books, Valentine Gross's pastels and drawings made during performances, Rambert's annotated score, photographs, reviews, Roerich's designs, interviews with Rambert and Lydia Sokolova [documented: OC Register 2013, All Arts]. Hodson's method was moving "between the last known movement and the next known movement" [documented: All Arts]. Robert Joffrey: "There is really no such thing as a 're-creation.' It has to look new" [documented: All Arts]. Today the Mariinsky credits it as "Choreography by Millicent Hodson (1987) inspired by Vaslav Nijinsky (1913)" with "Revived sets and costumes © 1987 Kenneth Archer" [documented: Mariinsky]. That credit line is the clearest example in this file of a reconstruction becoming a new authored and copyrighted work [argued; also flagged in `../scope/04`]. Robert Craft and others disputed the reconstruction's claims [probable: Levy, "The Rite of Spring at Seventy-Five", 1987, JSTOR 1478181, notes Archer's brief "in counterpoint to Craft's"].
- **Remakes**: more than 200 choreographies by one count, including Mary Wigman (1957), Maurice Béjart (1959), Pina Bausch (1975, on a floor of peat), Martha Graham (1984) [probable]. Walt Disney's *Fantasia* (1940) turned it into dinosaurs and planetary formation [probable].

**8. Documents and images.** Stravinsky's sketches: facsimile *The Rite of Spring: Sketches 1911–1913* (Boosey & Hawkes, 1969), Smithsonian Libraries record [documented: https://www.si.edu/object/siris_sil_707263]; the sketchbook is held by the Paul Sacher Stiftung, Basel [probable]. Roerich designs: Bakhrushin, Russian Museum, Nicholas Roerich Museum NY, V&A [probable]. Roerich died 1947: PD in life+70 jurisdictions [argued]. 1913 photographs of dancers in costume (Rambert among them) [documented: LibreTexts figure captions]. Valentine Gross pastels: Musée d'Orsay / Bibliothèque-musée de l'Opéra [probable]. NYPL film "The search for Nijinsky's Rite of spring" [documented in `../scope/04`]. 1940 Stravinsky/NY Philharmonic recording on the US National Recording Registry [documented: LoC essay PDF].

**9. Best sources.** Pieter C. van den Toorn, *Stravinsky and The Rite of Spring: The Beginnings of a Musical Language* (UC Press, 1987) **[open: UC Press E-Books public collection]**; Richard Taruskin, *Stravinsky and the Russian Traditions* (1996), and "Russian Folk Melodies in *The Rite of Spring*", *JAMS* 33/3 (1980); Millicent Hodson, *Nijinsky's Crime Against Grace* (Pendragon, 1996); Hodson, "Visual Sources from Roerich", *DRJ* (1986–87); Hanna Järvinen, "Critical Silence: The Unseemly Rite of Spring", in *Dancing Genius* (2014) [probable]; Severine Neff, Maureen Carr and Gretchen Horlacher eds, *The Rite of Spring at 100* (Indiana UP, 2017; chapters incl. "Stravinsky, Roerich, and Old Slavic Rituals" and "Racism at *The Rite*") [documented: JSTOR book contents, https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt2005z70]; Modris Eksteins, *Rites of Spring* (1989) [probable]; Davinia Caddy, *The Ballets Russes and Beyond* (CUP, 2012) [probable].

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].** *Premise*: a community renews itself by killing one of its own. *Sources*: an archaeologist-painter's research and a craft colony's collection; a folk-song anthology. *Rules*: turned-in, earthbound, collective. *Anti-DNA*: the entire ballet blanc, term by term. *Obligations*: the stated ambition to be "real" ritual (archaeological sincerity) even where the ritual is invented. *Lifecycle*: the canonical case of a work whose score survived and whose dance had to be rebuilt; the rebuilt version is now itself a copyrighted authored object. *Rights and money*: the folk melodies were borrowed unacknowledged; the reconstruction's sets and costumes are © 1987.

## 6. *Parade* (1917)

**1. Premise.** Cocteau's argument: "Three managers, ferocious, vulgar, superhuman, inhuman" advertise a show for which a Chinese conjurer, an American girl and two acrobats serve as the "parade", the free sample outside a fairground booth; the crowd takes the sample for the show and never goes in [documented: Théâtre du Châtelet magazine, quoting Cocteau's argument in French, https://www.chatelet.com/magazine/1917-parade-ou-lesprit-nouveau/]. Subtitle "ballet réaliste". Makers' frame: theatre within theatre, giving the real (city, managers) as constructed and the living dancers as artificial [documented: Châtelet]. Scholars: Christine Reynolds argues that "réaliste" was a serious aesthetic claim shared by all four collaborators, each seeking what was "fundamental, or real" about his art [documented: Reynolds, PhD thesis, Liverpool 2005, open access, https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/3174994/1/427062.pdf]. The Routledge Encyclopedia of Modernism frames it as dance exploring "the unstable relationship between elite and popular culture" [documented: https://www.rem.routledge.com/articles/parade-1].

**2. Roots.** Fairground and music-hall *parades* on Paris boulevards; American popular culture (silent serials, Pearl White, Chaplin, ragtime, the ocean liner); Chinese stage magicians; Cubist collage; Futurist noise music (Russolo) and Meyerhold's theatre of the mask, which Cocteau absorbed in 1913 [documented: Reynolds thesis chapter titles "Cocteau in 1913: Meyerhold, the Futurists and Le Potomak", "The principle of the mask"]. Cocteau's own aborted ballet *David* (1914), also with a fairground setting, is the direct forerunner [documented: Reynolds; *David* sketchbooks at the Harry Ransom Center, Austin]. Picasso's earlier circus *saltimbanques* (Rose Period) return in the curtain [probable].

**3. Originator and development.** **Originator: Cocteau**, a poet, not a dancer or composer. Cocteau dated the impetus to Diaghilev's 1912 command "Étonne-moi!" ("Astonish me") [documented as Cocteau's own account: Routledge Encyclopedia of Modernism]. He instigated *Parade* in 1916, chose Satie, then recruited Picasso; Massine was already Diaghilev's choreographer [documented: Reynolds preface]. Satie composed from 1 May 1916; after Picasso joined, Satie's method changed: his sketchbooks copy Cézanne's sphere-cube-cone dictum and he restructured the parts as sphere, cube and cylinder [documented: Reynolds abstract and ch. 3]. Contracts were signed only in January 1917; the project "threatened several times to overturn" [documented: Reynolds]. February 1917: the collaborators (minus Satie) met in Rome to work [probable: Wikipedia]; Giacomo Balla helped with the curtain and designs [probable: Wikipedia]. **Contested additions**: Cocteau added noise-makers (typewriter, siren, pistol, "bouteillophone") partly against Satie's wishes [probable: Wikipedia, citation flagged]; Satie still asked Cocteau in March 1917 for the text the American girl would type [documented: Reynolds appendix, 24 March 1917]. **Abandoned direction**: Cocteau's spoken text through a megaphone was cut [probable]. Picasso's Managers, Cubist constructions about 3 m tall in card and wood, reduced Massine's choreography for them "to mere footwork" [documented: Reynolds abstract].

**4. The world it built.** Two scales and two realities [argued]: the curtain (a soft, sentimental Rose-Period scene of circus performers at supper with a winged horse, painted in a muted naturalism) against the decor and Managers (hard Cubist city fragments, skyscrapers). Costumes: the conjurer in vermilion, yellow and black Chinese costume; the American girl in a sailor jacket and short pleated skirt; the acrobats in white and blue tights with spiral patterns; the Managers as walking buildings; the pantomime Horse (two dancers) [probable]. Movement: Massine's "realist" mime based on Stanislavsky-trained acting, cinematic gesture (the American girl rides a car, cranks a camera, swims, types), circus tricks, a one-step [documented: Reynolds ch. 5 on Massine's drama training; Poulenc in Châtelet magazine: "on dansait un one step dans Parade"]. Music: Satie's flat, repetitive, music-hall-derived score with the "Ragtime du paquebot" and noise instruments [documented: Interlude/Châtelet]. Anti-DNA [argued]: no Russian exoticism, no myth, no virtuosity, no romance, and no story that reaches its show: the work is all prologue.

**5. Team and credit.** Libretto Cocteau; music Satie; sets, costumes and curtain Picasso; choreography Massine (also the Chinese Conjurer); American girl Maria Chabelska; acrobats Lydia Lopokova and Nicholas Zverev; conductor Ernest Ansermet; programme essay Apollinaire [documented: LoC item record, https://www.loc.gov/item/ihas.200185227; Wikipedia]. Assistants on the curtain: Balla named; others contested (see `../scope/04` §3.6, "who helped paint the *Parade* curtain"). Dropped: Cocteau's noise contributions are rarely credited to him in the score.

**6. Production.** Premiere 18 May 1917, Théâtre du Châtelet, Paris, a wartime charity gala [documented: LoC]. "More than a year's work" by four collaborators [documented: Reynolds]. Audience: society and the Montparnasse avant-garde; Cubism was associated in wartime eyes with Germany (Kahnweiler, "Kub") [documented: Reynolds introduction].

**7. Reception and afterlife.** Uproar: the gallery shouted "Vive Picasso!", musicians "Vive Satie!" [documented: Poulenc, *Moi et mes amis* (1963), in Châtelet magazine]. Cocteau said only Apollinaire's uniform and head bandage saved them from "women, armed with hatpins"; Paul Morand's diary recorded "a lot of applause and a few jeers" [documented: Reynolds quoting both, showing two incompatible eyewitness accounts]. Critic Jean Poueigh's hostile review led Satie to send him an insulting postcard; Poueigh sued and Satie was sentenced (suspended) for libel [probable]. **Apollinaire's "sur-réalisme"**: in the programme note, often titled "Parade et l'esprit nouveau", he hailed the "new alliance" of decor and choreography and saw in *Parade* "une sorte de sur-réalisme", the starting point of an "esprit nouveau" [documented: Châtelet magazine; Cairn article noting he moved from "surnaturaliste" to "surréalisme" by May 1917, https://shs.cairn.info/revue-litterature-2017-4-page-28?lang=fr; BnF family guide to its *L'Invention du surréalisme* exhibition says the Surrealists took the word from this programme, https://www.bnf.fr/sites/default/files/2021-01/livret-jeu.pdf]. Not staged in Paris again until 1920 [documented: Reynolds]. Revivals: Massine restaged it for the Joffrey (1973) and for the Paris Opéra (replica Manager costumes 1979, shown by the BnF) [documented for the 1979 replicas: BnF]. The curtain (Centre Pompidou) is the most travelled object [documented in `../scope/04`]. Adjacent translation: the word "surrealism" itself, which became a movement in 1924.

**8. Documents and images.** LoC programme (1928 concert programme record) [documented]; Satie manuscripts at the BnF Music Department; Fonds Kochno at the Bibliothèque-musée de l'Opéra; Picasso's correspondence at the Musée Picasso; *David* sketchbooks at the Harry Ransom Center [documented: Reynolds acknowledgements]. Rights: Picasso (d. 1973), Cocteau (d. 1963), Massine (d. 1979) all in copyright; Satie (d. 1925) music PD in life+70 countries; 1917 photographs by Harry Lachman of Chabelska [documented: Wikipedia caption]. Link out for Picasso images (© Succession Picasso).

**9. Best sources.** Christine M. Reynolds, *The Ballet Parade (1917): An Experiment in Realism* (PhD, University of Liverpool, 2005) **[open access; appendix of letters]**; Deborah Menaker Rothschild, *Picasso's "Parade": From Street to Stage* (Sotheby's, 1991) [documented: named by Reynolds]; Richard Axsom, *"Parade": Cubism as Theater* (Garland, 1979) [documented: named by Reynolds]; Robert Orledge, *Satie the Composer* (CUP, 1990) [probable]; Peter Read, "Cubism Breaks Cover" [documented in `../scope/04`]; John Richardson, *A Life of Picasso*, vol. 2 [documented: cited by Reynolds].

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].** *Originator*: a poet-impresario who assembles a team rather than makes a part. *Premise*: the sample is the show. *Rules*: popular forms in an elite frame; real noises in music. *Anti-DNA*: Russianness (the company's own brand). *Adjacent translation*: produced a word that named a movement. *Handover*: Picasso's designs constrained the choreographer: the designer as rule-maker.

## 7. *Les Noces* (1923)

**1. Premise.** A Russian peasant wedding in four scenes: the blessing of the bride (her braids are combed and dressed), the blessing of the groom, the bride's departure from her parents' house, the wedding feast ending at the bedroom door [documented: NYCB repertory page, https://www.nycballet.com/discover/ballet-repertory/les-noces]. Stravinsky's frame: "choreographed Russian scenes with singing and music", a "sequence of typical wedding episodes" as ritual and "impersonal action", not a wedding plot "à la russe" [documented: Mariinsky programme, https://www.mariinsky.ru/en/playbill/playbill/2022/2/13/1_1900/]. Nijinska's frame and later readings: marriage as a community's machine, the bride's loss; called "protofeminist" [probable: Wikipedia].

**2. Roots.** Text: Russian wedding songs and lyrics, chiefly from Pyotr Kireevsky's collection of folk songs, published 1911 [documented: Mariinsky credits "based on Russian folk songs from the anthology by folklorist Pyotr Kireyevsky"]. Ritual: the peasant wedding as lament and transfer of the bride. Music: Orthodox chant and peasant heterophony; the score passed through versions with pianola, harmonium and cimbaloms (1919) before settling in 1923 on four soloists, chorus, four pianos and percussion [documented: Wikipedia]. Visual: Goncharova's neo-primitivism and her Russian icon and *lubok* sources [probable].

**3. Originator and development.** Stravinsky conceived it in 1913 and finished it in short score by October 1917 [documented: Wikipedia]. Diaghilev announced it for 1916 but the war delayed it [documented: Morgan Library large-print labels, https://www.themorgan.org/sites/default/files/pdf/exhibitions/BalletsRussesLargePrintLabels_0.pdf]. Goncharova began designs in 1915 in a "festive" colourful folk style (bride and groom escorted by two firebirds) [documented: Morgan]. Choreographers considered or begun: Fokine, then Nijinsky, then Massine, then Bronislava Nijinska [probable: Simple Wikipedia; contested detail]. Goncharova made two complete designs before her third was accepted; the first two did not suit the dance or score [probable: Simple Wikipedia]. **The key decision**: Nijinska rejected Goncharova's colourful peasant costumes and pushed for unified, almost uniform work-clothes; the final costumes were in brown and white only [documented: Mariinsky, "bicoloured brown and white costumes"]. Nijinska's own account says she wanted the dancers like a single mass, and that Diaghilev initially resisted her ideas [probable: Nijinska, "Creation of *Les Noces*", trans. Jean Johnson, *Dance Magazine* Dec 1974]. Stravinsky dedicated the work to Diaghilev [documented: Wikipedia].

**4. The world it built.** Rules [documented/argued]: the ensemble is the protagonist; bride and groom are "mere parts of the combined ensemble"; movement "needing no pantomime, stage props and realistic costumes" [documented: Mariinsky]. Women on pointe, but the pointe used as a percussive stab into the floor, not for lightness; torsos bent violently forward, back, sideways [documented: NYRB, Macaulay, https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2023/01/19/the-encyclopedia-of-the-dance-bronislava-nijinska]; few jumps, heads low, crouched groups, "massive shapes and blocks" [documented: Britannica, https://www.britannica.com/topic/Les-Noces]. The bride's two long braids, held by the women like ropes, and a pyramid of stacked heads [probable]. Palette: brown and white costumes, set of plain flats in muted ochre and grey-blue with a small window [probable]. Musicians visible on stage (pianos at the sides in 1923) [probable]. Anti-DNA [argued]: colour, folklore as souvenir, individual romance, mime.

**5. Team and credit.** Music and libretto Stravinsky; choreography Nijinska; design Goncharova; conductor Ernest Ansermet [documented: Wikipedia; Mariinsky]. The Morgan calls Nijinska "the Ballets Russes' only female choreographer" [documented]. Pianists at the 1962 Covent Garden revival were four composers: John Gardner, Malcolm Williamson, Richard Rodney Bennett and Edmund Rubbra [probable: Wikipedia]. Goncharova's partner Mikhail Larionov is often unnamed but advised [argued].

**6. Production.** Premiere 13 June 1923, Théâtre de la Gaîté-Lyrique, Paris [documented: Mariinsky]. Rehearsals in Monte Carlo, 1923 [documented: Morgan, programme and rehearsal photographs].

**7. Reception and afterlife.** H. G. Wells's open letter (1926) defending the London run: "I do not know of any other ballet so interesting, so amusing, so fresh or nearly so exciting" [probable: quoted in Wikipedia]. Revived by de Basil (1936). Nijinska herself staged it for Frederick Ashton's Royal Ballet in 1966, which is why the choreography survives close to the original; the Royal Ballet ended its 50th-anniversary gala (1981) with its final scene [documented: NYRB Macaulay]. Mariinsky staged it in 2003 with designs reproduced by Boris Kaminsky and Tatiana Noginova [documented: Mariinsky]. Remakes: Jerome Robbins (ABT 1965, Oliver Smith design, musicians on stage) [documented: NYCB]; Jiří Kylián (1982); Angelin Preljocaj (1989) [probable].

**8. Documents and images.** Morgan Library (Robert Owen Lehman collection): Stravinsky's manuscript with his own neo-primitivist cover design, sketches, 1923 programme [documented: Morgan]. Goncharova designs: V&A, Bakhrushin, Tretyakov [probable]; Goncharova d. 1962, so in copyright to 2033 in life+70 regions [argued]. LoC Nijinska Collection holds her notes [probable].

**9. Best sources.** Lynn Garafola, *La Nijinska: Choreographer of the Modern* (OUP, 2022) [probable]; Bronislava Nijinska, *Early Memoirs* (1981); Taruskin 1996 vol. 2 on *Svadebka* [documented: UC Press summary names *Les Noces*]; Alastair Macaulay, "The Encyclopedia of the Dance", *NYRB* 19 Jan 2023 [documented]; Sally Banes, *Dancing Women* (1998), ch. on *Les Noces* [probable].

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].** *Premise*: a wedding as a ritual that consumes the individual. *Rules*: ensemble over individual; two colours; pointe as percussion. *Anti-DNA*: the designer's own first, colourful world, refused by the choreographer. *Handover*: the clearest case here of a design being *stripped back* to fit the movement concept. *Lifecycle*: the original survives because the choreographer lived to restage it.

## 8. *Le Train Bleu* (1924)

**1. Premise.** On a fashionable beach at the end of the line of the Paris-to-Riviera luxury train, flirtations among bathers, a tennis champion, a golfer and the chorus of "poules" and "gigolos"; nothing happens but sport and courtship [documented: LoC Nijinska Collection, https://www.loc.gov/collections/bronislava-nijinska/articles-and-essays/la-train-blue; V&A]. Billed as an "opérette dansée" [documented: V&A]. LoC frame: "the shallowness of modern love" [documented]. Scholars: Mary E. Davis reads Chanel's "slangy chic" as an alternative strand of modernism [documented in `../scope/04`].

**2. Roots.** The *Train Bleu* itself, the first-class sleeper from Calais and Paris to the Côte d'Azur, in service from 1922 and nicknamed for its blue-and-gold cars in 1923 [documented: V&A gigolo costume record, https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O1115811/costume-for-a-gigolo-in-theatre-costume-coco-chanel]. The Paris Olympics of 1924 (the ballet ran during the VIII Olympiad) [documented: V&A Sasha photograph record]. The tennis star Suzanne Lenglen and the Prince of Wales (the golfer) [documented: LoC]. Cocteau's earlier *Les Mariés de la Tour Eiffel* (1921) for the rival Ballets Suédois [documented: V&A; the record dates it 1923, which is probably an error]. Offenbach operetta as the musical model [documented: V&A].

**3. Originator and development.** Cocteau had the idea after seeing the young English dancer Anton Dolin perform acrobatics [documented: Museo Thyssen press dossier, https://www.museothyssen.org/sites/default/files/document/2022-10/NdP_PicassoChanel_ING_1.pdf]. Milhaud was commissioned to write in the style of Offenbach, with popular-song rhythms but no quotations [documented: V&A]. It was Cocteau's last direct involvement with Diaghilev; his libretto "was necessarily simplified to make the ballet work in dance terms" [documented: V&A]. Nijinska choreographed and danced the tennis player. Costumes evolved in rehearsal: Lydia Sokolova described a pink hand-knitted bathing suit, and a team of women binding her hair until they chose a dark suede skull cap, which "set a fashion" [documented: V&A La Perlouse record, quoting Sokolova's *Dancing for Diaghilev*, p. 221]. **The curtain was not made for it**: Picasso's small gouache *Two Women Running on the Beach (The Race)* (Dinard, 1922, 34 × 42.5 cm), seen by Diaghilev in Picasso's studio, was enlarged in 24 hours by Prince Alexander Schervashidze to 6.78 × 8 m on a 10 × 11 m canvas, first shown on 26 May 1924 as a season front cloth with an Auric fanfare; Dolin recalled it became attached to *Le Train Bleu* only at the London Coliseum, 24 Nov 1924; Picasso signed it "Dédié à Diaghilev" [documented: V&A front cloth record, https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O171462]. Credit lines and painters are treated in `../scope/03` and `../scope/04`.

**4. The world it built.** Setting: Henri Laurens's neo-Cubist beach: two angular bathing cabins on the sand, his trademark fish as wing pieces, a neutral backcloth, a ramp, the sea-line marked on the floor cloth [documented: V&A gigolo record]; "twisted beach umbrellas" [documented: Thyssen]. Costumes: real sportswear rather than stage dress: knitted wool bathing suits (hand-knitted for the leads, machine-knitted for the chorus), tennis whites for Nijinska's Lenglen with headband, golf plus-fours and Fair Isle-style sweater for Woizikovsky's golfer; palette "subdued in brown, grey, pink, blue and black"; earrings of wax-coated china "pearls" [documented: V&A records]. Movement: acrobatics (Dolin's handstands and flips), sports gestures (serving, swinging, swimming strokes), slow-motion and frozen poses like snapshots or film [probable]. Anti-DNA [argued]: costume as fantasy; Russianness; the past; and the line between fashion and stage. The world is the present tense.

**5. Team and credit.** Libretto Cocteau; music Milhaud; choreography Nijinska; set Laurens; costumes Chanel; curtain Picasso (painted by Schervashidze); leads Dolin (Beau Gosse), Sokolova (Perlouse), Nijinska (tennis), Leon Woizikovsky (golfer) [documented: LoC; V&A; French Wikipedia]. It is the only Ballets Russes production for which Chanel designed all the costumes; she was also a financial supporter of the company [documented: V&A; ENB]. Critic Cyril Beaumont found the costumes "uninteresting" [documented: V&A].

**6. Production.** Premiere 20 June 1924, Théâtre des Champs-Élysées, Paris, in the Olympic season [documented: LoC]. Audience: the very people on the train.

**7. Reception and afterlife.** Light success; topical, so short-lived; Sokolova: "great fun while it lasted" [documented: V&A]. Dolin's departure ended it [probable]. Revivals: Paris Opéra 1992 (costume reproductions later shown at Thyssen's *Picasso/Chanel*, 2022) [documented: Thyssen]; Oakland Ballet and others; English National Ballet's 2025 reimagining by Stina Quagebeur at V&A East Storehouse, in Lycra versions of Chanel's designs "with the kind permission of CHANEL", danced in front of the original cloth [documented: ENB, https://www.ballet.org.uk/blog/reimagining-le-train-bleu]. Adjacent translation: the sportswear look moved from stage back to street; Chanel the brand still licenses and patronises ballet [argued; patronage in `../scope/03`].

**8. Documents and images.** V&A holds the front cloth, Sokolova's Perlouse costume, a gigolo costume and Sasha's 1924 stage photographs [documented]. LoC Nijinska Collection photograph of the 1924 production [documented]. Harvard Theatre Collection holds the earrings [documented: V&A]. Rights: Chanel, Picasso, Cocteau, Milhaud (d. 1974) all in copyright; Laurens (d. 1954) PD in life+70 regions since 2025 [argued]; V&A images non-commercial.

**9. Best sources.** Mary E. Davis, *Classic Chic: Music, Fashion, and Modernism* (University of California Press, 2006) [probable]; Lynn Garafola, *La Nijinska* (2022); Lydia Sokolova, *Dancing for Diaghilev* (1960) [documented: cited by V&A]; Anton Dolin, *Autobiography* (1960) [probable]; Museo Thyssen, *Picasso/Chanel* (2022) [documented].

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].** *Premise*: modern leisure as spectacle. *Rules*: real clothes, real sports, real places. *Adjacent translation*: in both directions at once (fashion onto stage, stage back to fashion). *Rights and money*: a living brand controls the costumes a century later. *Collection map*: the curtain was a season asset reassigned to a ballet after the fact, which shows the "season as collection" logic in an object.

## 9. *Apollon musagète* (1928)

**1. Premise.** Apollo is born on Delos, receives the lyre, instructs three Muses (Calliope, poetry; Polyhymnia, mime; Terpsichore, dance), chooses Terpsichore, and leads them to Parnassus [documented: Wikipedia numbers list]. Stravinsky's frame: a *ballet blanc* in which the avoidance of conflict, narrative and "psychological or expressive intent" is the point [probable: Wikipedia, citing Stravinsky]. Balanchine's frame: "the turning point of my life"; on hearing the score "all he could see was pristine white" [documented: PNB, https://www.pnb.org/repertory/apollo; Wikipedia]. Fuller form of his statement, from "The Dance Element in Stravinsky's Music" (1947): the score's "discipline and restraint" and "sustained oneness of tone and feeling" taught him that he could leave ideas out [probable: the last phrase documented in the Bolshoi page, https://www.bolshoirussia.com/performance/Apollon_Musagete; the rest from the 1947 essay]. Scholars: the founding work of ballet neoclassicism; PNB calls it "the fountainhead of contemporary classicism" [documented].

**2. Roots.** Greek myth via French 17th-century classicism: Stravinsky's model was Lully and the *tragédie lyrique*; the Calliope variation is headed "the Alexandrine" and set to a couplet from Boileau [documented for the heading: Wikipedia list; probable for Boileau]. The ballet blanc tradition (*Les Sylphides*, *Swan Lake* Act II) as the deliberate frame [argued]. Stravinsky's neoclassicism since *Pulcinella* (1920) [documented: PNB].

**3. Originator and development.** **The commission came first, and from America.** Elizabeth Sprague Coolidge commissioned the score in 1927 for a festival of contemporary music at the Library of Congress: $1,000, six dancers, a small orchestra, at most half an hour, free choice of subject [probable: Wikipedia; the commission itself documented in the LoC Adolph Bolm finding aid, https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/gdc/gdcfindingaidpdfs/mu010024/mu010024.pdf]. Stravinsky chose the subject and wrote the scenario; he reserved the European rights for Diaghilev and "had no interest in the US project" [documented as the Bolshoi page's claim]. **Two premieres, two choreographers**: Adolph Bolm's version, Library of Congress, 27 April 1928, with Bolm as Apollo, Ruth Page as Terpsichore, designs by Nicolas Remisoff, members of the Philadelphia Orchestra under Hans Kindler [documented: LoC record https://www.loc.gov/item/m56000693; Bolm finding aid; LoC "In the Muse" blog, 31 March 2026, https://blogs.loc.gov/music/2026/03/discovering-choreographer-ruth-page-at-the-library]. Balanchine's version for Diaghilev followed six weeks later and "quickly superseded" Bolm's [documented: Bolshoi page]. A clean case of the score as the stable work and the choreography as a competing interpretation [argued].

**4. The world it built.** Rules [documented/argued]: classical vocabulary only, but bent (flexed hands, turned-in steps, angular partnering, the Muses' troika, the fan of arabesques as closing image); string orchestra only; white and near-monochrome; no conflict, only instruction and choice. Design: André Bauchant's naïve-painter scenery of rocks and Apollo's chariot, which Wikipedia calls "baroque"; Chanel's 1929 costumes replaced Bauchant's: Apollo in a reworked toga with a diagonal cut, a belt and laced sandals [documented: Wikipedia]. Lifar's shoe, "originally hand-painted gold", has ribbons crossed "to evoke an ancient Grecian sandal"; NYPL notes that "ballet blanc" here describes "not only costume color but also a classical minimalism in choreographic style" [documented: NYPL exhibition item, https://www.nypl.org/events/exhibitions/galleries/performance/item/5444]. Anti-DNA [argued]: the Ballets Russes' painterly spectacle (the decor is what Balanchine later threw away); narrative; exoticism.

**5. Team and credit.** Music and libretto Stravinsky; choreography Balanchine (aged 24); scenery and costumes Bauchant (1928), costumes Chanel (1929); scenery executed by Alexander Shervashidze; costumes made under "Mme A. Youkine" [documented: Wikipedia; Bolshoi]. Apollo Serge Lifar; Muses Alice Nikitina or Alexandra Danilova (Terpsichore), Lubov Tchernicheva, Felia Doubrovska; Stravinsky conducted [documented for Stravinsky conducting: Bolshoi; others probable]. Coolidge and the Library of Congress are usually missing from the Balanchine credit [argued].

**6. Production.** Washington, 27 April 1928 (Bolm, Coolidge Auditorium); Paris, 12 June 1928, Théâtre Sarah-Bernhardt (Balanchine, Ballets Russes) [documented: LoC; Bolshoi].

**7. Reception and afterlife.** Balanchine shortened the title to *Apollo* in the 1950s, which Stravinsky came to prefer [documented: Wikipedia]. **Concept-stripping over fifty years**: Balanchine discarded the painted decor; in 1978 he cut the prologue of Apollo's birth, and for Baryshnikov (1979) omitted Apollo's first variation and rechoreographed the ending, which now closes on the fan tableau rather than the climb to Parnassus [documented: Bolshoi page; Wikipedia]. The maker edited his own concept down towards its rule ("white", "restraint"), treating a canonical work as a draft [argued]. Stagings are licensed by the George Balanchine Trust (see `../scope/05`) [probable].

**8. Documents and images.** LoC: printed score record, Bolm collection photographs, Coolidge Foundation files [documented]. NYPL: Lifar's shoe and photographs [documented]. Bauchant (d. 1958) and Chanel in copyright [argued]; LoC photographs of the Bolm production need checking record by record.

**9. Best sources.** Charles M. Joseph, *Stravinsky and Balanchine: A Journey of Invention* (Yale, 2002) [probable]; Stephanie Jordan, *Moving Music* (Dance Books, 2000) and *Stravinsky Dances* (2007) [probable]; Balanchine, "The Dance Element in Stravinsky's Music", *Dance Index* (1947) [probable]; Garafola 1989.

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].** *Premise*: art's god learns restraint and chooses dance. *Rules*: white, strings, classical steps bent modern. *Anti-DNA*: everything that made the Ballets Russes famous (colour, the painter's decor, the exotic story). *Handover*: commissioned in Washington, owned in Paris, remade in New York. *Lifecycle*: the author's own cuts as the strongest revision; the concept grows clearer as it loses material.

## 10. Soviet concept-making under the state: *The Red Poppy* (1927) and *The Flames of Paris* (1932)

Why both: *The Red Poppy* shows the early, improvised Soviet answer (a contest, a newspaper story, old ballet with a new flag); *The Flames of Paris* shows the formed method (a research-based "drambalet" with a theatre director, a musicologist-composer and an anniversary deadline). Together they show concept-making when the client is the state. `../scope/01` already covers the institutional frame (Ezrahi; Bolshoi tours) and is not repeated.

### 10a. *The Red Poppy* (*Krasnyi mak*, 1927)

**1. Premise.** In a 1920s Chinese port, a Soviet ship unloads grain; the dancer Tao Hoa (Tai-Choa) is exploited by her fiancé-manager Li Shan-fu and the English harbourmaster; she falls for the Soviet Captain, gives him a red poppy, refuses to poison him, and dies saving him, handing the poppy to a Chinese girl as an emblem of liberation [documented: Oxford Dictionary of Dance entry, https://www.oxfordreference.com/display/10.1093/oi/authority.20110803100409455]. Makers' frame, as Soviet tradition repeats it: "the first Soviet ballet with a modern revolutionary theme" [probable: Wikipedia; a claim to teach as a claim, since Lopukhov and others staged revolutionary-themed works in the early 1920s]. Scholars: Oxford calls it "an early example of Socialist Realism, created to fulfil State demands for populist and politically correct art" [documented: Oxford]. Simon Morrison and others read it as Orientalist: the heroine "a combination of orientalist clichés", danced in yellowface by Ekaterina Geltser [documented: BBC Culture, Eveline Chao, 28 June 2017, quoting Morrison, https://www.bbc.com/culture/article/20170628-the-ballet-that-caused-an-international-row].

**2. Roots.** News: *Pravda* reports in 1926–27 of a Soviet ship detained in a Chinese port and of the Nationalist massacres of Chinese communists [documented: BBC; Interlude]. Ballet: Petipa's exotic multi-act form (dream scene, divertissements) kept intact, with an opium dream in place of a Kingdom of the Shades [argued]. Music: Glière's late-Romantic idiom; the "Sailors' Dance" variations on the folk tune "Yablochko" [probable; Interlude says "based on a Russian folk-tune"].

**3. Originator and development.** **Contested originator.** One account: the Bolshoi held a 1925 contest for a modern ballet; Glière entered, found the *Pravda* story and engaged Mikhail Kurilko to write the scenario [documented as a claim: Interlude, https://interlude.hk/red-poppy-reinhold-gliere-yekaterina-geltzer]. Another: the designer Kurilko pitched the ballet from two *Pravda* stories [documented as a claim: BBC, following Morrison]. Approval was not automatic: Morrison writes it was cleared once events in China made it resonate "with the current political situation" [documented: BBC quoting Morrison, *Bolshoi Confidential*, 2016]. The choreography was divided, Lev Lashchilin for Acts I and III and Vasily Tikhomirov for Act II [probable: Wikipedia; Oxford names both]. Glière labelled the opening in manuscript "lifeless China" [documented: BBC].

**4. The world it built.** Two vocabularies as ideology [argued]: the Soviet sailors in vigorous, collective, folk-derived dance (the Sailors' Dance as a mass number); the Chinese heroine in pointe-work and exotic port de bras; the West in decadent ballroom (the Charleston); the villain "Sir Hips" [documented for the Charleston and Sir Hips: BBC]. The template it set: "revolutionary themes" and "collective athletic movement" [documented: BBC]. Anti-DNA [argued]: abstraction, ambiguity, the aristocratic hero. Critic Alexander Tcherepnin: "You don't make a statue of a Red Army officer out of whipped cream" [documented: Oxford].

**5. Team and credit.** Music Glière; libretto and design Kurilko; choreography Lashchilin and Tikhomirov; Tao Hoa Geltser (alternating with Viktorina Kriger); Captain Alexei Bulgakov; Harbourmaster Lashchilin [documented: Oxford; probable: Alison's Studio summary]. The real client, the state and its arts bureaucracy, is not on the credit line [argued].

**6. Production.** Premiere 14 June 1927, Bolshoi Theatre, Moscow [documented: Oxford]. Rewards later: Geltser received state honours; Glière became People's Artist of the USSR [probable: Interlude].

**7. Reception and afterlife.** A hit and a staple across the USSR [documented: BBC]. Remakes: Lopukhov (Leningrad, 1929); Zakharov (Kirov) and Lavrovsky (Bolshoi), both 1949; Igor Schwezoff's one-act version for the Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo (Cleveland, 9 Oct 1943) in which the villain became Japanese and the sailors included Americans and British, the allies of the moment [documented: Oxford; Wikipedia]. 1957: expanded and retitled *The Red Flower*, the heroine recast as a freedom fighter, "the pigtail was cut", "in deference to the Chinese"; after the Sino-Soviet split, the title reverted to *The Red Poppy* [documented: BBC; the date of the rename is given as 1949 by Interlude, so contested]. 2015 Russian-Italian co-production in Rostov [documented: BBC]. The concept was rewritten each time the alliance changed: geopolitics as revision engine [argued].

**8. Documents and images.** Bolshoi Theatre Museum; Bakhrushin Museum (Kurilko designs); Soviet postage stamp of a scene [documented: Wikipedia infobox caption]. Rights: Kurilko (d. 1969) and Glière (d. 1956) in copyright in most life+70 regions until 2040 and 2027 respectively [argued].

**9. Best sources.** Simon Morrison, *Bolshoi Confidential* (Norton, 2016) [documented: cited by BBC]; Christina Ezrahi, *Swans of the Kremlin* (Pittsburgh, 2012) [probable]; Elizabeth Souritz, *Soviet Choreographers in the 1920s* (Duke, 1990) [probable]; Eveline Chao, BBC Culture (2017) [documented].

### 10b. *The Flames of Paris* (*Plamia Parizha*, 1932)

**1. Premise.** Marseille peasants Jeanne and her brother Jérôme march to Paris with the Marseillais battalion, join the storming of the Tuileries (10 August 1792) and celebrate the Republic; an aristocratic actress, Mireille de Poitiers, and the court provide the old world [probable: Wikipedia character list]. Frame: an allegory of October made on the French model for October's 15th anniversary [documented: Marquee TV programme text; DanceTabs].

**2. Roots.** Félix Gras's Provençal novel *Li Rouge dou Miejour* (1896; French *Les Rouges du Midi*) [documented: Wikipedia]. Music: songs of the French Revolution (*Ça ira*, the *Carmagnole*, the *Marseillaise*, sung in the score) and period theatre music; Boris Asafiev, a leading musicologist, worked as a composer-arranger-historian [documented for revolutionary songs: DanceTabs; probable for borrowings from Grétry, Méhul and Lully, which other sources cite]. The French Revolution was the Bolsheviks' own chosen ancestor [argued].

**3. Originator and development.** A team built around research and drama: libretto Nikolai Volkov and the designer Vladimir Dmitriev; stage director Sergei Radlov, a theatre (not ballet) director; choreographer Vasily Vainonen [documented: Wikipedia]. The state's demand: DanceTabs summarises that Stalin "had decreed" relevant ballets for the masses, which is a secondary paraphrase rather than a cited decree [probable, treat with caution]. The making method of the drambalet (dramatic ballet): a realistic scenario, worked out like a play, with every dance motivated by the plot [probable: Souritz; Ezrahi].

**4. The world it built.** Rules [argued from documented features]: the crowd is the protagonist ("one of its main characters was in fact the population", Marquee [documented]); folk and character dances (Auvergne, Marseille, Basque) given "artistic equality with classical ballet" and the corps made "as important as the soloists" [documented: DanceTabs]; the aristocrats get the period court ballet (a pastiche *Rinaldo and Armida* within the ballet) as the decadent style; the revolutionaries get bravura jumps, which survive as the gala *Flames of Paris* pas de deux [probable]. Anti-DNA [argued]: the fairy tale, the sylph, the abstract, the individual's inner life.

**5. Team and credit.** As above; premiere cast Natalia Dudinskaya (Mireille), Vakhtang Chabukiani (Jérôme), Olga Jordan (Jeanne), Nina Anisimova (Thérèse), Konstantin Sergeyev (Mistral) [documented: Wikipedia]. Stalin Prize awarded [documented: DanceTabs].

**6. Production.** Premiere 7 November 1932, Kirov (Mariinsky) Theatre, Leningrad, the 15th anniversary of the October Revolution; Bolshoi premiere 6 July 1933 [documented: Wikipedia; ROH Collections record]. A work made for a date, like a state ceremony [argued].

**7. Reception and afterlife.** Became an anniversary staple [documented: Marquee]. Alexei Ratmansky's 2008 Bolshoi remake used surviving Vainonen fragments but turned the message: "instead of endorsing revolutionary fervour, he warns of the inevitable consequences", adding the aristocrat Adeline who is guillotined [documented: DanceTabs, https://dancetabs.com/2013/08/bolshoi-ballet-the-flames-of-paris-london; Marquee]. Mikhail Messerer's 2013 Mikhailovsky reconstruction instead followed Vainonen's 1947 Bolshoi version as closely as possible [documented: Wikipedia; DanceTabs]. Two remakes of one source, opposite politics [argued]. Adjacent translation: the pas de deux as a free-standing competition and gala number cut loose from its ideology [documented: BalletBox].

**8. Documents and images.** Mariinsky and Bolshoi museum archives; St Petersburg Theatre Library; Dmitriev designs (Bakhrushin) [probable]. Film fragments of Chabukiani [probable]. Rights: most makers died after 1955; treat as in copyright [argued].

**9. Best sources.** Christina Ezrahi, *Swans of the Kremlin* (2012) [probable]; Simon Morrison, *Bolshoi Confidential* (2016) [probable]; Elizabeth Souritz, essays on the drambalet [probable]; Janice Ross, *Like a Bomb Going Off: Leonid Yakobson and Ballet as Resistance in Soviet Russia* (Yale, 2015) [probable].

**10. Concept DNA reading for both [argued].** *Originator*: the state sets the brief (contest, anniversary), the team answers it. *Premise*: history as a usable allegory. *Rules*: the collective is the hero; folk dance equals classical dance; every dance is motivated. *Anti-DNA*: the West's decadence as a written-in style (the Charleston; the court ballet). *Obligations*: ideological correctness, revisable when politics change. *Lifecycle*: titles, villains and nationalities rewritten to match alliances (*Red Poppy*); remade to reverse its meaning (*Flames*, 2008). *Rights and money*: prizes and titles replace box office as the reward.

## 11. *Agon* (1957)

**1. Premise.** Twelve dancers (eight women, four men) in twelve short dances plus a prelude and two interludes; *agon* is Greek for "contest" [documented: Balanchine Foundation catalogue no. 316, https://balanchine.org/balanchine-catalogue/catalogue-of-works/316-agon-1957]. No story; Balanchine saw it as "a measured construction in space, demonstrated by moving bodies" [documented: LA Phil quoting Balanchine, https://www.laphil.com/works/agon] and called it "the quintessential contemporary ballet" [documented: PNB, https://www.pnb.org/repertory/agon]. Edwin Denby: it "recalls court dance as much as a cubist still life recalls a pipe or guitar" [documented: PNB]. Later readings emphasise the race and gender politics of the central pas de deux (Brenda Dixon Gottschild; Sally Banes) [probable].

**2. Roots.** **Kirstein's letter (1953)**: "a competition before the gods . . . the gods are tired and old; the dancers reanimate them by a series of historic dances" [documented: quoted in *The New Yorker*, "The Spelling of Agon", 12 July 1993, https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1993/07/12/1993-07-12-084-tny-cards-000145703]. **The dance manual**: Kirstein sent Stravinsky a newly published edition and English translation of François de Lauze's *Apologie de la danse* (1623), whose editor compared de Lauze with Arbeau's *Orchésographie* (1588) and Marin Mersenne's *Harmonie universelle* (1636–37); Mersenne's rhythmic descriptions and musical examples of the Bransle simple, Bransle gay, Bransle de Poitou, Sarabande and Galliard gave Stravinsky his models [documented: Mark D. Richardson, "The 'Bransle Gay' Dance Rhythm in Stravinsky's Ballet *Agon*", *Mitteilungen der Paul Sacher Stiftung* 16 (2003), open PDF, https://www.paul-sacher-stiftung.ch/dam/de/forschung-und-publikationen/mitteilungen/16/mitteilungen_der_paul_pacher_sacher_stiftung_16_mark_d_richardson.pdf]. The edition is Joan Wildeblood's (London, 1952) [probable]. **Music**: Schoenberg's and Webern's serialism, which Stravinsky took up in the 1950s; *Agon* was his earliest work to use a full twelve-tone row [documented: Wikipedia]. **Answers**: the "Greek" trilogy (*Apollo*; *Orpheus*, 1948) that it was commissioned to complete [documented: Balanchine catalogue].

**3. Originator and development.** **Originators: Balanchine (the contest idea, though not the title), relayed and funded by Kirstein** [documented: the *New Yorker* says the idea "had been his", Balanchine's, "relayed by Lincoln Kirstein"]. Commissioned by New York City Ballet with funds from the Rockefeller Foundation; dedicated to Kirstein and Balanchine [documented: Balanchine catalogue]. Stravinsky began in December 1953, broke off in 1954, resumed in 1956 and finished on 27 April 1957 [documented: Wikipedia]. **The structure was co-designed in advance**: composer and choreographer "together designed the structure of the ballet during the creation of the music"; the outline specifies, "with exact timings, the basic movements for twelve dancers" [documented: Balanchine catalogue]. **Abandoned direction**: the Greek-gods scenario and the trilogy frame: "the historical references are pretexts" [documented: Balanchine catalogue]. The music had its concert premiere first: UCLA Royce Hall, 17 June 1957, under Robert Craft [documented: Wikipedia].

**4. The world it built.** Rules [documented/argued]: the number twelve everywhere (dancers, sections, tone row) [documented: LA Phil]; each section a different instrumental combination for a different combination of dancers [documented: LA Phil]; court-dance titles as pretexts, with "a scattering of baroque steps and arm movements" [documented: PNB]; classical vocabulary pushed to off-balance, flexed feet, thrust hips and interlocked limbs. **The look**: no scenery; practice clothes, the women in black leotards and pink tights, the men in white T-shirts and black tights; lighting by Nananne Porcher [documented for lighting: Balanchine catalogue; for black-and-white practice dress: Esplanade, https://www.esplanade.com/offstage/articles/2020/pas-de-what]. The Kennedy Center's teaching note links the black and white to Stravinsky's piano writing in "major and minor keys (white and black)" [documented as their interpretation, https://www.kennedy-center.org/education/resources-for-educators/classroom-resources/media-and-interactives/media/dance/george-balanchine--agon]. Practice clothes refuse costume as a design field: the body and the music are the whole world [argued]. **The pas de deux** was made for Diana Adams (white) and Arthur Mitchell (Black); in 1957 the pairing "sparked controversy and backlash" [documented: Boston Ballet, https://www.bostonballet.org/stories/celebrating-black-dance-history]. Whether Balanchine meant it as a social statement is contested: Esplanade says "perhaps" in response to 1950s America; the Kennedy Center says it was "probably interpreted as a political statement" [documented as competing readings]. Anti-DNA [argued]: story, decor, costume, Greek myth (dropped), tonal comfort.

**5. Team and credit.** Music Stravinsky; choreography Balanchine; lighting Porcher; no designer; premiere cast Todd Bolender, Barbara Milberg, Barbara Walczak, Roy Tobias, Jonathan Watts, Melissa Hayden, Diana Adams, Arthur Mitchell; conductor Leon Barzin [documented: Balanchine catalogue]. Kirstein's role (idea, manual, money) appears in the dedication, not the credit line [argued]. The dancers as co-authors: Balanchine "choreographed the pas de deux especially for Mitchell and Adams" [documented: Boston Ballet].

**6. Production.** Preview 27 November 1957 (March of Dimes benefit); premiere 1 December 1957, City Center of Music and Drama, New York [documented: Balanchine catalogue]. Martha Swope's rehearsal photographs show Stravinsky in the studio with Balanchine, Adams and Mitchell [documented: NYPL Digital Collections, https://old-digitalcollections.nypl.org/items/98807c86-12bc-f9fe-e040-e00a180639db].

**7. Reception and afterlife.** Critics called it "a living textbook on the art of blending music and motion" [documented: LA Phil]. The practice-clothes look became NYCB's house style for its "black and white" ballets (*Episodes*, 1959; *The Four Temperaments*, stripped of its Seligmann costumes in 1951; *Stravinsky Violin Concerto*, 1972) [probable]. Licensed by the George Balanchine Trust; the Foundation's Interpreters Archive filmed coaching of the pas de deux in 2005 [documented: Balanchine catalogue]. Mitchell went on to found Dance Theatre of Harlem (1969) [probable]. Adjacent translation: the leotard-and-tights look entered fashion photography and design as the image of "pure" dance [argued].

**8. Documents and images.** NYPL Jerome Robbins Dance Division: Martha Swope photographs (rights reserved) [documented]. Paul Sacher Stiftung, Basel: Stravinsky's *Agon* sketches [documented: Richardson]. Balanchine Foundation catalogue (open web) [documented]. No open images; link out [argued].

**9. Best sources.** Mark D. Richardson (2003) **[open PDF]**; Charles M. Joseph, *Stravinsky and Balanchine* (Yale, 2002) [probable]; Stephanie Jordan, "Agon: A Musical/Choreographic Analysis", *Dance Research Journal* 25/2 (1993) [probable]; Brenda Dixon Gottschild, *Digging the Africanist Presence in American Performance* (Greenwood, 1996) [probable]; Sally Banes, "Balanchine and Black Dance", in *Writing Dancing in the Age of Postmodernism* (1994) [probable]; Balanchine Foundation catalogue entry 316 [documented].

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].** *Originator*: the producer sends a source text and the money; the makers keep the source as "pretext". *Sources*: a 1623 dance manual read through a 1952 translation. *Rules*: twelve; matched instrumental and bodily combinations; timings fixed before rehearsal. *Anti-DNA*: costume and decor themselves. *Grammars*: the concept *is* a grammar (combinatorics) rather than a world. *Collection map*: it defines a house look (the black-and-white ballets). *Rights and money*: foundation money at the start; a trust controls stagings now.

### 12.2 Who originates [argued, from the cases above]

- **The originator is usually not the choreographer.** Of twelve works, the idea came from a composer (Petrushka, Sacre, Noces), a poet or writer (Firebird's poem, Parade, Train Bleu), a painter or designer (Schéhérazade, Sacre, Red Poppy), a patron or producer (Apollon, Agon) or the state (Red Poppy, Flames). Only *Faune* is plausibly a choreographer's own concept, and that is contested.
- **Origin stories are themselves contested claims.** *Schéhérazade* (Benois vs Bakst), *Petrushka* (Stravinsky and Benois each crediting the other), *Faune* (Bakst vs Nijinsky), *Sacre* (vision vs world), *Red Poppy* (composer vs designer). Memoirs written decades later rearrange credit. Students should treat "who had the idea" as an argument with sources on each side.
- **Producers originate by brief, not by idea.** Diaghilev's "Étonne-moi"; Coolidge's six dancers and half an hour; the Bolshoi's contest; an anniversary date; Kirstein's letter and a dance manual. The brief is a constraint that generates the concept.

### 12.3 What the sources are

- **A text the makers did not write**: a poem (Polonsky, Mallarmé), a folk-tale or folk-song collection (Afanasyev, Kireevsky, Juszkiewicz), a novel (Gras), a manual (de Lauze), a newspaper (*Pravda*).
- **A collection the makers could visit**: the Louvre's vases, Tenisheva's peasant dress at Talashkino, Benois's childhood fair. Most concepts were rooted in specific, nameable objects.
- **Earlier music used as found material**: Rimsky-Korsakov's suite, Debussy's prélude, revolutionary songs; in *Petrushka* a music-hall tune that brought a royalty claim.
- **Borrowing is a recurring dispute**: Rimsky's widow; Stravinsky's denied folk melodies; Orientalism in *Schéhérazade* and *Red Poppy* (and its 1957 revision under Chinese pressure); the Russia "invented for foreigners".

### 12.4 How teams form

- **Circle first, then commission.** The Ballets Russes grew out of a friendship circle (the Nevsky Pickwickians, *Mir iskusstva*) that later commissioned outsiders (Stravinsky, Picasso, Chanel).
- **A small, fixed nucleus plus one new "star" maker per work** (Golovin, Roerich, Picasso, Goncharova, Chanel, Laurens, Bauchant). The new maker brings a new world.
- **The editor's veto** (Diaghilev; Nijinska over Goncharova; Balanchine over his own decor) does as much concept work as invention.
- **Power shifts between roles over time**: choreographer over composer (*Firebird*), composer and painter over choreographer (*Sacre*), poet as convenor (*Parade*), designer as constraint (*Parade*'s Managers), composer and choreographer as equal planners with timings agreed in advance (*Agon*). Fokine's principle 5, "complete equality", described an aim the company rarely achieved.

### 12.5 How worlds get rules

- **Each work's rules are stated as refusals.** The strongest concepts are defined by a short list of things they will not do: no turnout (*Faune*, *Sacre*), no colour (*Noces*, *Apollon*, *Agon*), no costume (*Agon*), no story (*Agon*), no Russia (*Parade*, *Train Bleu*), no individual hero (*Sacre*, *Noces*, *Flames*). In Concept DNA terms the *anti-DNA* field generates the *rules* field.
- **A vocabulary is split to carry meaning**: classical vs folk (*Firebird*), classical as mechanism (*Petrushka*), classical as decadence (*Flames*' court ballet, *Red Poppy*'s Charleston). The choice of which movement language belongs to whom is the work's ideology.
- **A single visual or numeric constraint organises everything**: the frieze (*Faune*), one room (*Schéhérazade*), two colours (*Noces*), white (*Apollon*), twelve (*Agon*).
- **Music and design increasingly set the rules for dance**: Fokine's 1914 letter asked for equal partnership; in practice the 1910s often let the painter or composer fix the world before the choreographer arrived.

### 12.6 How works are remade

- **Score survives, dance does not.** The music of *Firebird*, *Sacre* and *Apollon* became concert repertory; the choreography had to be rebuilt or replaced.
- **Four kinds of remake** recur:
  1. *Re-design* of the same choreography (*Firebird* 1926; Benois's eleven *Petrushkas*);
  2. *Reconstruction* from documents, which becomes a new authored and copyrighted work (*Faune* 1989–91; *Sacre* 1987, "© 1987 Kenneth Archer");
  3. *Re-choreography* to the same score (*Sacre*: Massine, Bausch; *Noces*: Robbins; *Apollon*: Bolm vs Balanchine);
  4. *Re-meaning* by politics or by the author (*Red Poppy* renamed; *Flames* 2008 reversed; Balanchine cutting *Apollo*).
- **The maker alive is the best archive**: *Les Noces* survives because Nijinska restaged it in 1966; *Faune* survives because Nijinsky notated it while interned.
- **Adjacent translation runs both ways**: stage to fashion (*Schéhérazade*, *Train Bleu*), fashion to stage (Chanel), stage to language ("surrealism"), stage to concert hall (the suites) and to cinema (*Fantasia*).

### 12.7 Teaching use [argued]

For a design course, the most transferable lessons are: (1) a concept is usually assembled by a team around a brief and a found source; (2) write the anti-DNA first, because these works became famous for what they refused; (3) keep a development record, since the credit fights here came from missing ones; (4) expect the world to be remade, and decide in advance which rules must survive a remake (the *Apollo* lesson).

### 12.8 Gaps and cautions

- Several "probable" items rest on Wikipedia summaries of standard secondary sources (Buckle, Garafola, Taruskin, Walsh); they should be checked against those books before teaching.
- Dates of Soviet renamings (*The Red Flower*: 1949 vs 1957) and the originator of *The Red Poppy* are contested in the sources seen.
- Rights statements here are reasoning from death dates (life+70), not legal advice; each museum's own licence governs its photographs.
- Japanese names: none arose in this period's works.
