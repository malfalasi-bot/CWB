## 1. *The Red Detachment of Women* (Beijing, 1964)

红色娘子军 *Hongse niangzi jun*. Central Opera and Dance Drama Theatre ballet troupe (today the National Ballet of China, 中央芭蕾舞团).

**1. Premise.** A beaten servant girl on Hainan Island escapes a landlord, joins an all-women company of the Red Army, and grows from personal revenge to class consciousness, ending as the detachment's Party representative [documented, Wikipedia synopsis and Chinese press]. The makers framed it as making workers, peasants and soldiers "the masters of the stage" in a Western form; Wilcox reads it as the work that introduced a new genre, "revolutionary modern ballet" (革命现代芭蕾舞剧), and argues it was "a revolt from within" the dance field by a ballet minority, not the main socialist dance legacy [documented, Wilcox 2019 ch. 4].

**2. Roots.**
- History: the Women's Special Company of the 2nd Independent Division of the Qiongya Red Army, formed May 1931 on Hainan [probable, Wikipedia].
- Literary/film chain: Liang Xin (梁信), a writer in the Central-South Military Region, went to Hainan in 1958 to "experience life", saw photographs of the women soldiers in a memorial hall and wrote a script first titled 《琼岛英雄花》 ("Heroic Flowers of Qiong Island"); August First Studio rejected it; Xie Jin (谢晋) at Shanghai Tianma Studio took it up and retitled it; the colour film (1961; some sources say 1960/1962 release) won the first Hundred Flowers awards [documented, zh.wikipedia, en.wikipedia film page]. The ballet is thus an adaptation of a film, an inversion of the usual stage-to-screen order, which the Chinese press itself remarks on [documented, Guangming Daily 30 Oct 2024].
- Music: two songs from the film, "Song of the Women's Company" (娘子军连歌, words Liang Xin, music Huang Zhun 黄准) and "Army and People Are One", were built into the score; the "Women's Company" theme runs through the whole ballet; "万泉河水清又清" is arranged from a Li folk song, "Five Finger Mountain" [documented, wenming.cn 2022].
- Earlier dance works it answers: Wilcox shows that Chinese-dance works had already staged women soldiers with rifles (the dance "Female Civilian Soldiers" 1964) and Hainan Li people (*Five Red Clouds*, 1959); *Red Detachment* re-did this material in ballet vocabulary [documented, Wilcox ch. 3–4].
- Soviet model: the troupe had learned ballet from Soviet teachers (Petr Gusev 1957–60, staging the Chinese *Swan Lake*) and danced *Swan Lake*, *Le Corsaire*, *Giselle*, *La Esmeralda*; the Soviet "drambalet" of heroic narrative is the formal template [documented, Wilcox].

**3. Who had it and how it was developed.**
- Originator: the political prompt came from Premier Zhou Enlai. After watching the troupe's Western repertory (Chinese sources: *Notre-Dame de Paris*, i.e. *La Esmeralda*, late 1963) he asked whether, after ten years of Western and Soviet ballets, they could make one on a revolutionary theme [documented, Guangming Daily 2024; wenming.cn 2022; Mullis 2017 citing Roberts 2010: beyond "princes and fairies"].
- Candidate subjects proposed and abandoned: *Dazhi and Her Father* (达吉和她的父亲), *Red Crag* (红岩), *Wang Gui and Li Xiangxiang* (王贵与李香香) [documented, Guangming Daily 2024]. Reasons recorded for choosing *Red Detachment*: a female lead suits ballet's emphasis on the ballerina; the film's songs were usable; Hainan's coconut palms, Wanquan River and Five Finger Mountain gave "national colour"; a mass audience already knew the story [documented, same].
- Committee: in January 1964 the Ministry of Culture, Musicians' and Dancers' Associations held a "Capital Music and Dance Forum"; Lin Mohan (林默涵, vice-minister) then formed the creative team: choreographers Li Chengxiang (李承祥), Jiang Zuhui (蒋祖慧), Wang Xixian (王希贤); composers Wu Zuqiang (吴祖强), Du Mingxin (杜鸣心), Dai Hongwei (戴宏威), Shi Wanchun (施万春), Wang Yanqiao (王燕樵), the music "completed under collective discussion" [documented, wenming.cn 2022]. Designers named: Ma Yunhong (马运洪) and Liang Ye (梁晔) [documented, Guangming Daily 2024].
- Fieldwork: the team (with dancers Bai Shuxiang 白淑湘, Zhong Runliang, Wang Guohua, Li Xinying) went to Hainan: military training, life in Li villages, interviews with surviving women veterans, collection of folk song, Qiong opera, dance and design material, then drafted the scenario outline [documented, Guangming Daily 2024; Harris 2010 and Wikipedia note 1964 fieldwork].
- Premiere and sanction: Tianqiao Theatre, Beijing, 26 September 1964 (Chinese sources) or "early October 1964" (Wilcox) [contested]. Mao's verdict, quoted in every later account: "方向是对的，革命是成功的，艺术也是好的" (the direction is right, the revolution successful, the art good) [probable; widely quoted, not seen in primary source].
- Revision by the state: in 1965 Jiang Qing ordered changes: the heroine renamed from Wu Qionghua (吴琼花) to Wu Qinghua (吴清华); the male Party representative Hong Changqing made the central hero; music and dances altered [documented, zh.wikipedia; probable at scholarly level]. Wikipedia's film article summarises research that Jiang Qing's influence was significant "only later in the process" though she later claimed the creation [contested: Jiang Qing as author vs. Zhou Enlai/troupe as originators]. After 1976 dancers condemned her and said she "knew nothing about the arts" [documented, Wilcox ch. 5 quoting *Dance* 1976].

**4. The world it built.**
- Setting: Yelinzhai, Hainan; Ten-Year Civil War (1927–37). Six scenes with prologue in the filmed version.
- Movement rules: ballet vocabulary with changed "quality": the arabesque with raised fist, greater speed and force; pointe kept as a sign of upward revolutionary will (Qinghua rises on pointe as she receives her rifle) [documented, wenming.cn]. Xiqu (Chinese opera) tumbling and martial-arts props in battle scenes; heroic poses "almost always" ballet lines [documented, Wilcox]. Signature images: rifle drill on pointe, bayonet dance, "倒踢紫金冠" back-kick leap.
- Music: Western symphony orchestra with Hainan folk melody, choral song; three leitmotifs (Hong Changqing, Wu Qionghua, the Detachment) [documented, wenming.cn].
- Palette and costume: grey-blue uniforms with red armbands and stars, shorts and puttees for the women soldiers (rather than tutus); the landlord's manor in rich, "feudal" colour; tropical palms. [probable, from film stills.]
- Anti-DNA (explicit): no princes and fairies; no love duet (the hero and heroine never partner romantically, a point Roberts and Bai Di analyse); no supernatural; no tragic ending; the enemy never sympathetic [argued, from Mullis 2017 and Roberts 2010].
- Grammar against the conventions of its day: a ballerina corps of soldiers, weapons as props, the hero a Party commissar, the ballet blanc replaced by drill. Huang Boshou's early review said the makers "broke down the previous conventions of ballet" [documented, quoted in Wilcox].

**5. The team and the credit.** In the Cultural Revolution the credit line became collective: the 1972 Foreign Languages Press book is authored by "China Ballet Troupe" (Zhongguo Wuju Tuan) [documented, Wikipedia bibliography]. Individual names (Li, Jiang, Wang; Wu Zuqiang et al.) returned to the record after 1976. Dropped or minimised: Liang Xin and Huang Zhun (the film's makers), Xie Jin; Tian Han, who wrote the parallel Peking opera version in 1964 and died in prison in 1968 [probable]. First cast: Bai Shuxiang (Wu Qionghua), Liu Qingtang (Hong Changqing; later Vice-Minister of Culture and jailed after 1976) [documented, zh.wikipedia; Wilcox].

**6. Production.** State troupe, state budget; workshop of the Central Opera and Dance Drama Theatre; Tianqiao Theatre premiere; later performances in the Great Hall of the People for leaders and foreign delegations [documented, chinaculture.org]. Audience: the Party leadership first, then the national mass audience via film. No budget figures found.

**7. Reception and afterlife.**
- Named one of the eight "model works" (样板戏) by 1966–67; filmed 1970/71 (Beijing Film Studio, dir. Pan Wenzhan and Fu Jie) with Xue Jinghua and Liu Qingtang, the film becoming the fixed "text" [documented, Wikipedia].
- Performed for Richard Nixon, February 1972; used in John Adams's *Nixon in China* (1987) Act II [documented].
- Adjacent translation: Peking opera (1964; filmed 1972), stamps, picture books (连环画), Hung Liu's paintings, Godard-era French Maoism and the film *Les Chinois à Paris* (1974) [documented, Ma Nan 2020], a 2006 TV series, Red Tourism park at Qionghai (1998) [documented].
- Remakes: still in National Ballet of China repertory (60th anniversary 2024, six generations of Qionghua dancers) [documented]; Wen Hui's documentary theatre *RED* (Shanghai 2015) re-reads it as collective memory [documented].
- Which version is "the work": the 1970 film version (post-Jiang Qing) is what is danced; the 1964 version survives only in photographs [documented, Wilcox].

**Kept / changed / why (the 1965 state revision as a remake).**

| Kept from film | Changed for ballet (1964) | Changed by the state (1965) | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hainan, the landlord Nan Batian, the commissar in disguise, the women's company, the two songs | Story compressed to dance scenes; dialogue to mime and leitmotif; a Western form made "national" with Li folk song | Heroine's name; hero's centrality; dances and music; romance erased | 1964: to prove ballet could carry Chinese proletarian heroes; 1965–70: to fit the "three prominences" (三突出) rule that the main hero stand above all [argued from Roberts 2010; three prominences is a documented model-work rule, probable here] |

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].**
- Premise: a girl's liberation as the nation's.
- Perimeter: Hainan, 1930s, one company; the world is a map of red base vs. manor.
- Rules: every pose upward; women on pointe as soldiers; music always tells you who is good.
- Anti-DNA: no fairies, no princes, no romance, no ambiguity.
- Obligations: Zhou's brief (revolutionary subject); the "three transformations" (revolutionise, massify, nationalise).
- Anchor expression: the rifle-drill line on pointe.
- Adjacent translation: film → ballet → opera → film → stamps → TV → theme park: a concept designed to be copied.
- Rights and money: collective state authorship, no individual rights; the credit line itself is ideological.
- Lifecycle: proposed 1963, made 1964, rewritten 1965, frozen 1970 on film, canonised, then reclaimed as "classic" by the post-Mao state.

## 2. *Spartacus* (Bolshoi, Grigorovich, 1968)

«Спартак». Music Aram Khachaturian; this production: choreography and libretto Yuri Grigorovich; design Simon (Soliko) Virsaladze; conductor Gennady Rozhdestvensky. Bolshoi Theatre, Moscow, premiere 9 April 1968 [documented, Bolshoi production credits as relayed by bolshoirussia.com].

**1. Premise.** The slave revolt of 73–71 BCE as a heroic tragedy of freedom against empire, told through four characters: Spartacus and his wife Phrygia, the Roman general Crassus and his courtesan Aegina [documented]. The makers framed it as a heroic epic organised "around the theme of the uprising" [documented, Bolshoi programme text]. Searcy reads the 1968 version as a turn from the "Friendship of Peoples" display of earlier versions to "ethnic homogenization", matching Brezhnev-era unified Soviet identity [documented, Searcy 2016 abstract].

**2. Roots.**
- History: Appian (*Civil Wars*) and Plutarch (*Life of Crassus*), sources also used by Marx, who called Spartacus a hero [probable, Opera Today 2005].
- Novel: Raffaello Giovagnoli, *Spartaco* (1874), credited on the Bolshoi poster [documented].
- Visual: Khachaturian said he was inspired by the Colosseum on a 1950 Soviet delegation visit to Italy [probable, Opera Today].
- Earlier works it answers: (a) Khachaturian's 1954 four-act score and Nikolai Volkov's scenario, which gave Spartacus and Crassus invented lovers, Phrygia and Aegina [probable]; (b) Leonid Yakobson's Kirov version (27 Dec 1956), barefoot or sandalled, in "friezes" of plastique after Greek vase painting, without pointe; Grigorovich danced a gladiator in it [documented, Japan Arts; Wikipedia]; (c) Igor Moiseyev's Bolshoi version (1958), huge corps, folk-ensemble style [probable]; (d) Yakobson's Bolshoi restaging (1962) [documented, Searcy].

**3. Who had it and how it was developed.**
- Originator of the subject: Khachaturian with Volkov (1950–54). Originator of *this* concept: Grigorovich, newly Bolshoi chief choreographer (from 1964), under a theatre commission for "a heroic work" [documented, Bolshoi programme essay].
- Development: Grigorovich wrote a new libretto, cut and reordered Khachaturian's score, and invented danced "monologues" for each of the four principals, alternating action with "self-commentary"; the Bolshoi programme calls this new in ballet [documented]. His conversations with Khachaturian are described as a "battle for the notes" [documented, programme essay quoting Grigorovich]. The company was unenthusiastic at first ("we've already danced that") [documented, same].
- Records: Bolshoi artistic council transcripts, press, and audience surveys, read by Searcy in the theatre archive [documented, Searcy abstract].

**4. The world it built.**
- Structure: three acts in short scenes, each closed by a monologue in front of a dropped curtain: the cinema close-up as dance [argued].
- Movement: virtuosic male dance as the engine: massed lines of legionaries, leaps for Spartacus (Vasiliev), Crassus as a rival virtuoso rather than a mime role; neoclassical lines for women [documented in reviews; Guardian obituary 2025].
- Palette and scene: Virsaladze's spare, "rugged" décor, an empty stage with a few monumental elements, leaving space for mass movement; costumes of tunics and armour that read as Roman shapes in Soviet colours [probable].
- Anti-DNA: no character dances, no folk display of nations (removed), no pantomime [argued, from Searcy].
- Grammar against its day: against drambalet's mime-heavy realism, Grigorovich made "symphonic" dance carry the drama [probable].

**5. The team and the credit.** Credit line: "Libretto by Yuri Grigorovich after the novel by Raffaello Giovagnoli, ideas from the scenario by Nikolai Volkov used" [documented]. Khachaturian keeps composer credit although the score heard is Grigorovich's arrangement; a CD by Capriccio names Grigorovich as "arranger" [documented, Naxos catalogue]. First cast: Vladimir Vasiliev (Spartacus), Ekaterina Maximova (Phrygia), Maris Liepa (Crassus), Nina Timofeyeva (Aegina) [documented]. Dropped: Yakobson and Moiseyev, whose versions the 1968 work replaced.

**6. Production.** Bolshoi Theatre (state theatre), April 1968. Audience: Moscow public and the state (Lenin Prize awarded to Grigorovich, Vasiliev and Virsaladze, 1970 [probable]). Toured to Covent Garden 1969 and London 1974 [probable, Britannica; Guardian]. Filmed 1977 (dir. Vadim Derbenyov and Grigorovich) [documented, Wikipedia]. Khachaturian's score itself won a Lenin Prize, 1954 per Wikipedia or 1959 per Opera Today [contested; the Lenin Prize was re-established in 1956–57, so 1959 is the likelier].

**7. Reception and afterlife.** Became "the" Bolshoi signature work; Grigorovich left the Bolshoi in 1995 and returned to oversee his ballets in the 2010s; died 2025 [documented, Guardian obituary]. Arlene Croce, *New Yorker* 1975 ("Hard Work"), was sceptical [documented title]. The Adagio of Spartacus and Phrygia became a BBC TV theme (*The Onedin Line*, 1971) [probable]: an adjacent translation into popular culture.

**Kept / changed / why.**

| Kept (from Khachaturian/Volkov) | Changed (Grigorovich 1968) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Four principals, Phrygia/Aegina invented by Volkov; the score's big numbers; the uprising plot | Libretto rewritten; score cut and reordered; national dances removed; monologues added; pointe restored; male corps as army | A commission for a heroic work; Grigorovich's "symphonic" dramaturgy; Searcy: the politics of a single Soviet people replacing the "Friendship of Peoples" [documented abstract; argued interpretation] |

**8. Primary documents and open images.** Bolshoi archive (artistic council transcripts) per Searcy; 1977 film; photographs mostly agency-copyright (Getty/Evening Standard, RIA Novosti). No open images found in this pass.

**9. Best sources.**
- Anne Searcy, "The Recomposition of Aram Khachaturian's *Spartacus* at the Bolshoi Theater, 1958–1968", *Journal of Musicology* 33.3 (2016): 362–400 (JSTOR 26414240).
- Anne Searcy, *Ballet in the Cold War: A Soviet-American Exchange* (OUP, 2020) [probable].
- Christina Ezrahi, *Swans of the Kremlin: Ballet and Power in Soviet Russia* (Pittsburgh UP, 2012) [probable on Grigorovich and Yakobson].
- Janice Ross, *Like a Bomb Going Off: Leonid Yakobson and Ballet as Resistance in Soviet Russia* (Yale UP, 2015) [probable; the Yakobson version].

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].** Premise: freedom against empire. Rules: every principal gets a soliloquy; men are the army; the curtain is a camera cut. Anti-DNA: no ethnographic display. Obligations: Soviet heroic-epic genre; a Marx-approved hero. Lifecycle: one score, four choreographic concepts in twelve years (1956, 1958, 1962, 1968): the clearest case in the course of a concept rebuilt by successive makers until one version becomes canonical.

## 3. Cuba: Alicia Alonso's *Giselle* as a national concept

Alicia Alonso (Havana 1920–2019). Her *Giselle*, "after Coralli and Perrot", music Adam; built over 1945–63 and fixed in the film *Giselle* (dir. Enrique Pineda Barnet, ICAIC, 1963) with the Ballet Nacional de Cuba (BNC) [documented, Tomé 2025 postprint].

**1. Premise.** Not a new story but a new claim on an old one: that a Cuban ballerina and a Cuban company could "rescue the essence" of Romantic ballet and give back "a rejuvenated Giselle" to Paris, where it was born in 1841 [documented, Alonso quoted in Tomé 2025]. Alonso framed her method as respect for tradition plus modern theatre: today's dancers and audiences "are different" [documented, quoted in Tomé]. Tomé reads her *Giselle* as "a project in modernizing the piece" through Stanislavskian dramaturgy, and as the vehicle by which a "Cuban school" was asserted [documented, Tomé 2025; Tomé 2013 "Music in the Blood"].

**2. Roots.**
- Nineteenth-century Cuba: *Giselle* was danced in Havana in the Romantic era by touring French companies (the Ravels' troupe; Myrtha by Adélaïde Lehmann, Hilarion by François Ravel) since no Cuban dancers existed [documented, Tomé 2025].
- Her own lineage: learned *Giselle* in Anton Dolin's Ballet Theatre staging (1940s), first as a friend and as Moyna; debut in the title role on 2 November 1943 at the Metropolitan Opera House, New York, replacing an ill Alicia Markova after about seven hours of rehearsal [documented, Tomé; Guardian obit 2019; Granma]. Myth element: she is said to have rehearsed the role in her head and on her fingers while bedridden after eye surgery [probable, Guardian obit].
- Influences she absorbed: Antony Tudor's psychological ballet; partner Igor Youskevitch's version (from Sergeyev, with Lifar elements); Mary Skeaping's "extended" *Giselle* staged in Havana April 1954; the Soviet productions she danced in Riga, Leningrad (7 Jan 1958) and Moscow (17 Jan 1958), from which she took Bertha's mime of the Wili legend [documented, Tomé].
- National discourse: Cubans as an exceptionally musical people; Tomé shows this "Music in the Blood" claim sits in tension with the fact that the style may be Alonso's training passed on [documented, Tomé 2013 abstract].

**3. Who had it and how it was developed.**
- Originator: Alicia Alonso, with Fernando Alonso (husband, teacher, co-architect of the "Cuban school") and Alberto Alonso.
- Sequence [documented, Tomé 2025]: first staging for Havana's Pro-Arte Musical Society, 1945 → Ballet Alicia Alonso founded 1948 (two thirds Ballet Theatre dancers in recess) → company renamed Ballet de Cuba 1955, dissolved 1956 after she repudiated Batista over funding → stagings for Teatro Colón and the Greek Theatre, Los Angeles, 1958 (full-length, "a new paradigm" in the US) → state company after the Revolution (1959; official funding 1960) → ICAIC film 1963.
- Method, in her words: compare productions, keep the "original elements" that recur in all, adopt what works from others, and shape the rest by "our taste, our way of dancing, our way of being" because "everything else depends on the restager" [documented, quoted in Tomé].
- The film as development document: Pineda Barnet, from the Stanislavskian Teatro Estudio, led "table work" with the entire cast; every member of the corps wrote a backstory; the resulting script was so detailed he called it "a book" and dancers kept copies for decades [documented, Tomé interview 2014].

**4. The world it built (what changed).**
- Motivation for everything: Giselle evades Loys at first because she has sewing to do (an inserted hem-stitching gesture), which implies a backstory: she is a seamstress; the Duke saw her delivering needlework and rented the cottage opposite [documented, Alonso "Performing Giselle" (1978) via Tomé]. ABT ballerinas later copied the gesture from the film [documented, Payne via Tomé].
- Class: within the revolutionary climate, Hilarion was softened from jealous brute toward the "exploited classes"; Albrecht "was lies and deceit" (Pineda Barnet) [documented]. Unrealistic mixing of "haughty nobles" and "prancing farmers" amended [documented].
- Mime reduced where dance can say it (Hilarion cuts in on the couple's dance instead of miming "it is me who loves you"); mime kept where only mime will do [documented].
- The corps individualised: Giselle's six friends each with a "dream" and "ideal love"; the peasant pas de deux turned into a dance for the friends; Myrtha's part made harder to "enhance the role" [documented].
- Style: the Cuban school's musicality, speed, balances and the "Cuban arabesque" balanced between Soviet and Western lines [documented, Fernando Alonso via Tomé].
- Anti-DNA [argued]: no mannered "Russian" exoticism (Hurok had sold Ballet Theatre as Russian ballet; Alonso's career is a refusal of that branding), no anonymous corps, no unmotivated gesture.

**5. The team and the credit.** Credit line: "choreography Alicia Alonso after Coralli and Perrot" [documented, NYPL catalogue]. Tomé notes the moment she felt her adaptation "had become distinct enough from Dolin's to merit credit" [documented]. Film team: Pineda Barnet (director), Antonio Rodríguez (camera), Carlos Menéndez (editing), Manuel Duchesne Cuzán (conductor), National Symphony Orchestra; cast Azari Plisetski (Albrecht), Fernando Alonso (Hilarion), Mirta Plá (Myrtha), Loipa Araújo (Bathilde), Josefina Méndez, Margarita de Sáa [documented]. Dropped from the line: Dolin, Skeaping, the Soviet stagers whose elements she adopted; and Fernando Alonso's pedagogical authorship of the "school".

**6. Production.** State-funded BNC and state film institute ICAIC; Havana theatres (Auditorium, García Lorca/Gran Teatro). Audience: after 1959 the company toured fishing villages, factories and schools as part of revolutionary culture [probable, Encyclopedia.com; Tomé 2017 "Swans in Sugarcane Fields" already in scope 01].

**7. Reception and afterlife.** Adopted by Ballet de Camagüey (1970), Paris Opéra (1972), Bellas Artes Mexico (1976), Vienna State Opera (1980), San Carlo Naples (1981), Slovak National Theatre (1989) [documented, Rey Alfonso via Tomé]. Alonso danced the role until 1983 (full) and in excerpts until 1993 [documented]. 2 November, her debut date, is kept as a *Giselle* night in Havana [documented, Granma]. The BNC still tours "Alicia Alonso's *Giselle*" (e.g. Saratoga 2018) [documented, docslib copy of press release].
- A Cuban-themed creation for comparison: Alberto Alonso's *Carmen* (Bizet–Shchedrin, Bolshoi 1967, for Maya Plisetskaya; Cuban version for Alicia Alonso the same year) [probable], and the earlier *La rebambaramba* project (Carpentier and Roldán, 1928) as a vision of "a Cuban ballet" with Afrocubanismo [documented title, Tomé 2013 Korea article].

**Kept / changed / why.**

| Kept | Changed | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Adam score, two-act structure, Coralli–Perrot "original elements" common to all versions; the Wili legend | Motivation inserted (seamstress backstory); Hilarion humanised, Albrecht the deceiver; mime partly turned into dance; corps individualised; Myrtha harder; full-length restored | To make the story legible to a new film and mass audience "not familiar with ballet conventions"; Stanislavskian theatre; revolutionary class reading "applied subtly"; and to define a Cuban style worthy of returning to Paris [documented reasons, Tomé] |

**8. Primary documents and open images.**
- Film *Giselle* (ICAIC 1963), VHS by Kultur (2006) [documented]; NYPL *MGZIA 4-4960* gala video (1981) and BNC souvenir programmes (MGZR) at NYPL Jerome Robbins Dance Division [documented, catalogue].
- Alonso, "Performing Giselle" (1978) and "Apuntes sobre Giselle" [documented as cited].
- Journal *Cuba en el Ballet* (BNC) [documented as cited]. No open images located; BNC/ICAIC rights reserved.

**9. Best sources.**
- Lester Tomé, "Alicia Alonso's *Giselle*: A Cuban Dancer's Journey into Romantic Ballet and Modern Dramaturgy" (Italian publ. in E. Randi ed., *La storia di Giselle*, Kinetès, 2025), **English postprint OA** at Smith ScholarWorks — https://scholarworks.smith.edu/dance_books/1.
- Tomé, "Alicia Alonso: *Giselle* in a Cuban Accent", in M. Kant (ed.), *Cambridge Companion to Ballet* (2007).

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].** Premise: the canon can be owned by the periphery. Rules: every gesture motivated; every dancer a person; the "original elements" are sacred, everything else belongs to the restager. Obligations: to the Romantic text and to a revolutionary audience. Anchor expression: the seamstress gesture, small enough to travel to ABT. Lifecycle: a performer's interpretation (1943) → a staging (1945) → a national school's signature → a film "book" → export back to Paris (1972). Lesson for learners: a remake can be conservative in plot and radical in method.

## 4. Dance Theatre of Harlem: *Creole Giselle* (1984)

Choreography after Coralli and Perrot, staged by Frederic Franklin; reconceived by Arthur Mitchell; scenario by Arthur Mitchell and Carl Michel; designs Carl Michel; music Adam. Premiere London Coliseum, 1984 (DTH's first full-length classic); New York City Center season October 1984 [documented, DTH website; New Yorker 22 Oct 1984].

**1. Premise.** *Giselle* transposed to 1840s Louisiana among free people of colour: the class gap between a village girl and a nobleman becomes a gap within a Black society, where caste was measured by "the number of generations a person could count up from slavery" [documented, Croce 1984 quoting the company's "copious historical notes"]. Mitchell's framing: a ballet created in 1841 that "was not imagined to be performed by" Black dancers, now owned by them [documented, DTH 2023 page]. Casey reads it as "hybridity, stability and subversion": the production keeps the classical text stable so that its Black performers can be "caught dancing" in it, which itself subverts the canon [probable, title and abstract-level reading of Casey 2006].

**2. Roots.**
- The 1841 *Giselle* (Gautier, Saint-Georges, Coralli, Perrot, Adam), via Franklin's own memory of the Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo version he danced [documented, MOBBallet].
- History: the gens de couleur libres of antebellum Louisiana and their colour/freedom hierarchy; the company researched and published notes [documented, Croce].
- Visual: plantation architecture, Spanish moss, bayou [documented, Croce: "a white porch and white barns overhung with plenty of Spanish moss"].
- Earlier work it answers: DTH's *Firebird* (1982, Geoffrey Holder's Caribbean setting, John Taras choreography) as a prior "transposition" model [probable]; and the long exclusion of Black dancers from the *ballet blanc* (scope 01 covers this).

**3. Who had it and how it was developed.**
- Originator: Arthur Mitchell (concept), with designer Carl Michel (co-scenarist) [documented, DTH credit "Scenario by Arthur Mitchell and Carl Michel"].
- Stager: Frederic Franklin, English, ex-Ballet Russe star, who transmitted the steps from memory [documented, MOBBallet; probable for "from memory"].
- Development: the lead, Virginia Johnson, "researched Louisiana in the 1840's, Russian ballerinas who performed *Giselle*, and the original ballet style"; she credited dramatic roles in *Streetcar* and *Fall River Legend* for her mad scene [documented, Dance Enthusiast 2023]. Karel Shook (co-founder) answered the *New York Times* on whether the company was ready [documented].
- Abandoned directions: not found.

**4. The world it built.**
- Rules: steps, music and narrative are kept; names, setting, costume and social logic are changed. Albrecht becomes Albert; Giselle's mother was born a slave; Albert's family is "three rungs" higher [documented, Croce; Wikipedia]. The Wilis become ghosts of girls who loved dancing and died of broken hearts, in a bayou graveyard [probable, Wikipedia].
- Palette and costume: 1840s day dress of free Black Louisianans, white plantation architecture in Act I; Spanish moss and swamp in Act II [documented, Croce; Wikipedia].
- Croce's observation of a grammar shift: the peasant pas de deux now reads as "imported Paris finery", the steps "gentrified", because the society on stage aspires to European manners [documented, Croce 1984]. This is the concept's sharpest effect: the ballet vocabulary becomes a sign of class within the story.
- Anti-DNA [argued]: no plantation slavery on stage, no "folk" Africanism added to the steps, no rewriting of the choreography. The refusal to alter the steps is the point: competence in the canon as the claim.

**5. The team and the credit.** DTH's own credit is precise: "Choreography by Jean Coralli & Jules Perrot / Staged by Frederic Franklin / Scenario by Arthur Mitchell and Carl Michel" [documented, DTH 2023]. Franklin received a Laurence Olivier Award for it [documented, MOBBallet]. Popular accounts often credit only Franklin ("Franklin restaged…", Wikipedia), dropping Mitchell and Michel's scenario. Cast: Virginia Johnson (Giselle), Eddie Shellman (Albert) [documented, photo caption].

**6. Production.** London Coliseum premiere 1984 (date not found), then City Center, New York; filmed in Aarhus, Denmark, August–September 1987 (dir. Thomas Grimm; producers Yanna Kroyt Brandt, Thomas Grimm) for NBC; "the first full-length ballet production broadcast on NBC" (per HistoryMakers) [documented]. No budget found.

**7. Reception and afterlife.** Croce's *New Yorker* review "Giselle, ou La Fille des Bayous" (Oct 1984) [documented]. Wikipedia records a split: some saw it as "inappropriate and inferior", others as "ground-breaking" [probable]. The NBC film inspired the young Misty Copeland [documented, Dance Enthusiast]. Streamed by DTH in 2020 and 2023 [documented]. Toured to the USSR in 1988 (company tour) [documented]. Fed into later debates on skin-tone tights and shoes (scope 03).

**Kept / changed / why.**

| Kept | Changed | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Coralli–Perrot steps (as Franklin knew them), Adam's score, the plot beats, the Wili act | Setting (Rhineland → Louisiana 1840s); names (Albert); class logic becomes a colour/freedom caste among free Black people; costumes and scenery; Wili backstory | To place Black dancers inside the canon without altering the canon's steps; to give a Black company a narrative world in which a classical story is historically plausible; to answer critics who doubted Black dancers' classical credentials [argued from DTH statements and Croce] |

**8. Primary documents and open images.** 1987 Aarhus film (Kultur DVD 2005; DTH streaming) [documented]. Arthur Mitchell papers, Columbia University (scope 01). Smithsonian NMAAHC holds DTH objects (scope 03). No open images confirmed for *Creole Giselle*.

**9. Best sources.**
- Carrie Gaiser Casey, "Caught Dancing: Hybridity, Stability, and Subversion in Dance Theatre of Harlem's *Creole Giselle*", *Theatre Journal* 58.2 (2006): 269–89, JSTOR 25069823; institutional repository record at Saint Mary's College (OA status not confirmed).
- Arlene Croce, "Giselle, ou La Fille des Bayous", *New Yorker*, 22 Oct 1984.
- Zita Allen's DTH exhibition essay, Columbia University Libraries (scope 01).
- Brenda Dixon Gottschild, *The Black Dancing Body* (2003) for context [probable].

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].** Premise: the same steps, a different society. Perimeter: one Louisiana parish, 1840s. Rules: never change a step; change everything around it. Anti-DNA: no exoticising. Anchor expression: the Act I porch hung with moss. Adjacent translation: TV broadcast (1987) → streaming (2020). Lesson: re-conceptualisation by *re-setting*, the cheapest and most legible kind of remake, works when the new setting has its own internal hierarchy that maps onto the old one.

## 5. Mats Ek: *Giselle* (1982) and *Swan Lake* (1987)

Cullberg Ballet (Sweden). Choreography Mats Ek; set and costumes Marie-Louise Ekman; *Giselle* stage premiere 6 July 1982 [documented, Arthaus DVD notes; venue not confirmed]; *Swan Lake* (*Svansjön*) 1987 [documented, Wikipedia works list; premiere date and venue not found].

**1. Premise.**
- *Giselle*: the village outsider whose "hypersensitivity" sets her apart is driven mad by a man from another class; Act II takes place not in a forest of spirits but in a psychiatric asylum [documented, NYT 10 Nov 1982: "reset its second act in an insane asylum"]. Ek's own framing, before the premiere: Giselle is "the focal point where high and low, life and death, meet in a union that tears her to pieces, but creates a human being"; his version is "an attempt to reimagine the fairy tale" [documented, Ek 1982 as quoted by videotanz.ru, translated; under 25 words each].
- *Swan Lake*: a prince's break-up from his mother and his dream of purity, "a development with obstacles, about a break-up that becomes too difficult to be completed"; the prince seeks "an inner exile" back at the lake [documented, Ek paraphrased in videotanz.ru]. Ek starts from Tchaikovsky, an "outsider", and the music's "great sadness" [documented, same].
- Ek insists his reworkings are not "a polemic against the original works" but a search for emotions "in the bound form and period expression of the fairy tale" [documented, same, paraphrase].

**2. Roots.**
- The 1841 and 1877/1895 originals, and the scores, both kept. For *Giselle* he works to a fixed recording (Richard Bonynge, Opéra de Monte-Carlo, Decca) "to retain every small nuance of the rhythmical sketch" [documented, Mariinsky tour page].
- Family and form: son of choreographer Birgit Cullberg and actor Anders Ek (Bergman's films); trained in theatre direction (Marionette Theatre, Royal Dramatic Theatre, 1966–73), Graham technique and the Jooss/Cullberg dance-drama tradition [documented, Opéra de Paris bio; Wikipedia].
- Earlier works it answers: Birgit Cullberg's dance-theatre (*Miss Julie*, 1950); the post-1960s European "Tanztheater" climate (Pina Bausch) [probable]; the Romantic heritage of madness and the Victorian asylum as cultural image [argued].
- For *Swan Lake*: psychoanalytic readings of the Siegfried–Queen relationship; Tchaikovsky's biography [documented as Ek's stated point of departure].

**3. Who had it and how it was developed.** Ek as both author and company director (co-director with his mother 1978–80; sole director from 1985 to 1993) [documented]. Developed with Ana Laguna, for whom Giselle was created, and with designer Marie-Louise Ekman, a painter and filmmaker (later head of the Royal Dramatic Theatre) [documented, Laguna Wikipedia; probable on Ekman's later post]. Development documents not found in this pass beyond Ek's 1982 pre-premiere statement.

**4. The world it built.**
- *Giselle* Act I: a rural landscape painted by Ekman as a body (hills read as breasts) [probable]; Giselle tied by a rope, a village "simple" girl; Albrecht in a white suit, an urban man [probable, from film]. Act II: a white asylum ward; Wilis are patients; Myrtha a matron [probable]; Ekman's set shows dismembered body parts [probable]. Ending: no forgiveness in a forest; Albrecht left naked, Hilarion covers him [probable, from the 1987 film].
- *Swan Lake*: swans of both sexes, bald-headed, barefoot or in soft shoes, with flexed feet, honks and squats; the Queen is a dominant mother; Odile emerges "from under Ekman's abstract lake backdrop" in the final moment [documented for the last image, Segal LA Times 2002; probable for the rest].
- Movement grammar: classical lines broken by flexed feet, grounded pliés, deep second-position squats, everyday gesture, comic animal behaviour; Ek "uses classical as well as modern dance techniques" [documented, Wikipedia].
- Anti-DNA [argued]: no tutus, no pointe as ethereality, no supernatural explanation; the supernatural is relocated to the psyche.

**5. The team and the credit.** Ek (choreography, later TV adaptation); Ekman (sets and costumes); Jorgen Jansson (lighting, on tour); assistants Ana Laguna, Monica Mengarelli [documented, Mariinsky/Lyon credits]. 1987 TV *Giselle*: dir. Måns Reutersvärd; costumes after Marie-Louise De Geer Bergenstråhle by Ulla Mamer [documented, videotanz — note this names a different costume credit for the TV version; contested whether De Geer Bergenstråhle or Ekman designed the original costumes]. Cast: Ana Laguna, Luc Bouy (Albrecht), Yvan Auzely (Hilarion), Lena Wennergren (Myrtha) [documented]. Credit lines keep "music Adolphe Adam" and drop Coralli/Perrot and Gautier: the steps are wholly new.

**6. Production.** Cullberg Ballet was a touring state-funded company (Riksteatern) [probable]. *Giselle* reached New York in Nov 1982 [documented, NYT]. Swedish TV (SVT) filmed *Giselle* 1986/87 and *Svansjön* 1990; one Ek TV ballet won an Emmy (*Carmen*) [documented, Laguna Wikipedia].

**7. Reception and afterlife.** *Giselle* is Cullberg's most-performed work [documented, videotanz]; entered Paris Opéra 1993 and Lyon Opéra Ballet 2009 [documented]. Laguna won the French Video Dance Prize 1983 [documented]. Ek's "reconceived classics" (*Carmen* 1992, *Sleeping Beauty* Hamburg 1996, *Juliet & Romeo* 2013) became a genre and a precedent for Bourne, Masilo and Khan [argued; Masilo explicitly cites Ek? not confirmed].

**Kept / changed / why.**

| Work | Kept | Changed | Why (as Ek gives it) |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Giselle* | Adam score (recorded), two-act structure, characters (Giselle, Albrecht, Hilarion, Bathilde, Myrtha), class contrast, madness | Setting of Act II (asylum), movement language, the supernatural made psychological; no Wilis as ghosts; new choreography | To find in the original's "strong contrasts" (realism vs dream, castle vs hut, individual vs group) a modern human story; to make madness, not ghosts, the subject [documented] |
| *Swan Lake* | Tchaikovsky score; prince, mother, white and black swan | Swans as ungendered, bald, earthy; mother–son drama central; ending open and ironic | The fairy tale is "quite unclear" with different endings; Ek finds in Tchaikovsky's music longing and danger; the prince's horror at a world that threatens "his dream of purity" [documented] |

**8. Primary documents and open images.** Films: Arthaus Musik *The Art of Mats Ek: Giselle* (109280), streaming on Marquee TV and Alexander Street [documented]. Swedish archive (SVT, Dansmuseet/Scenkonstmuseet) [probable]. No open images found.

**9. Best sources.**
- "Ballet: Cullberg *Giselle*", *New York Times*, 10 Nov 1982 (reviewer not seen in this pass).
- Lewis Segal, "Cullberg Ballet brings whimsy, sophistication to *Swan Lake*", *Los Angeles Times*, 21 Oct 2002.
- Lena Hammergren, writings on Swedish dance and Cullberg (e.g. in *The Oxford Handbook of Dance and Politics*) [probable].
- Vida Midgelow, *Reworking the Ballet: Counter-Narratives and Alternative Bodies* (Routledge, 2007): the key scholarly book on Ek, Bourne and others as "reworkings" [probable; strongly recommended].

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].** Premise: the classic's supernatural is a code for the psyche. Rules: keep the score, change the cosmos; the body is grounded. Anti-DNA: tutu, pointe, spirits. Anchor expression: the asylum ward; the bald swan. Lifecycle: made for a small touring company, then adopted by the Paris Opéra, the institution of the original: the counter-version absorbed into the canon.

## 6. Matthew Bourne: *Swan Lake* (1995)

Adventures in Motion Pictures (AMP; now New Adventures). Direction and choreography Matthew Bourne; set and costumes Lez Brotherston; lighting Rick Fisher [probable]; music Tchaikovsky (reordered). World premiere Sadler's Wells Theatre, London, 9 November 1995 (run to 25 November) [documented, New Adventures timeline].

**1. Premise.** A modern prince, trapped by royal duty and starved of affection by a cold mother, meets a Swan (a man) who embodies the freedom and love he cannot have; the pack turns on the Swan and the prince dies [documented, Wikipedia synopsis]. Bourne's stated founding image: "The strength, the beauty, the enormous wingspan of these creatures" suit a male dancer's musculature more than "a ballerina in her white tutu" [documented, quoted in Wikipedia; under 25 words each]. Scholars and critics read it as a gay *Swan Lake* in which the supernatural becomes psychological; Bourne has resisted a single reading [contested: queer-love reading (Wikipedia "Politics"; Midgelow 2007 probable) vs. Bourne's broader story of longing and emotional neglect (probable, interviews)].

**2. Roots.**
- The 1895 Petipa–Ivanov *Swan Lake* and Tchaikovsky's 1877 score (the "outline of the plot") [documented].
- Film: Hitchcock's *The Birds* (1963) [documented, BBC 2024]. Bourne's AMP worked from cinema and musical theatre ("Adventures in Motion Pictures" is a film joke) [argued].
- Contemporary Britain: a royal family in the tabloid 1990s, paparazzi and a prince's unsuitable girlfriend [probable; widely noted].
- Tchaikovsky's homosexuality as a background resonance [documented as reading, Wikipedia].
- Earlier reworkings: his own *Nutcracker!* (1992) and *Highland Fling* (1994, after *La Sylphide*) [documented]; Ek's 1987 *Swan Lake* with male and female bald swans as precedent [argued; not documented as a direct influence].

**3. Who had it and how it was developed.** Originator: Bourne. AMP had six dancers in 1995 and had to grow to 32 to make the show [documented, Wikipedia]. Bourne recruited Royal Ballet principals Adam Cooper (Swan/Stranger) and Fiona Chadwick (Queen) [documented]. Swan look (black triangle on the forehead, cropped hair, bare torsos and feet, white silk-chiffon "feathered" breeches) "created by Bourne and British designer Lez Brotherston" [documented, BBC 2024]. Development notes and Bourne's working process are recorded in Alastair Macaulay (ed.), *Matthew Bourne and His Adventures in Motion Pictures* (Faber, 1999; rev. 2011), a book-length interview [documented title, New Adventures timeline].

**4. The world it built.**
- Setting: a contemporary European court (palace, nightclub, a "Swank Bar", a royal ball, a park lake, a psychiatric clinic) [probable, synopsis].
- Rules: swans are a male pack, wild and aggressive, hissing, flapping, crouching; no pointe; the corps is a predator, not a sisterhood [documented, Wikipedia: "fierce, bird-like choreography"].
- Doubling: the Swan returns in Act III as the Stranger in black leather, seducing everyone including the Queen (the Odile function given to the same dancer) [documented].
- Humour: a ballet-within-the-ballet parodying Romantic ballet in Act I [probable].
- Score: reordered and cut: No. 5 moved from Act I to Act III after the national dances; most of No. 19 and the following pas de deux removed [documented, Wikipedia "Music"].
- Anti-DNA [argued]: no tutus, no pointe, no magician (Rothbart removed), no redemptive love-death.
- Grammar: dance theatre/musical theatre ("Best Director of a Musical" Tony), commercial long runs rather than repertory.

**5. The team and the credit.** Bourne (director and choreographer), Lez Brotherston (sets and costumes; Tony for Costume Design 1999), producer Katharine Doré, musical director David Frame, Scott Ambler (Prince) [documented, New Adventures timeline awards list]. Credit lines omit Petipa and Ivanov entirely; "Matthew Bourne's *Swan Lake*" places the author's name in the title, a branding choice [argued]. Note: the V&A catalogue text says Brotherston "was not Bourne's first choice" of designer [documented but garbled in source; treat as contested].

**6. Production.** Commercial and subsidised mix: Sadler's Wells premiere (2.5 weeks), UK tour Feb–Apr 1996, West End at the Piccadilly Theatre from 11 Sept 1996 (120 performances, a West End record for a full-length dance classic), Ahmanson LA 1997, Broadway Neil Simon Theatre 8 Oct 1998 [documented, New Adventures timeline]. Audience: a theatre and musical audience rather than a ballet subscription base [argued]. No budget figure found.

**7. Reception and afterlife.** Olivier Award 1996; three Tony Awards 1999 (Direction of a Musical, Choreography, Costume Design); 30+ awards [documented, V&A; New Adventures]. Filmed 1996/98 (dir. Peter Mumford; PBS *Great Performances*), 2012 (3D) and 2019 (cinema) [documented]. *Billy Elliot* (film 2000) ends with Adam Cooper about to enter as the Swan: the work becomes a cultural sign for male dancing in Britain [documented]. 30th-anniversary tour "The Next Generation" 2024–25 [documented].

**Kept / changed / why.**

| Kept | Changed | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Tchaikovsky score (reordered); the prince, mother, swan, black-swan double; the lakeside Act II and ball Act III structure | Swans male; setting contemporary royal court; Rothbart cut; the double is the Swan's own dancer as "the Stranger"; score cut; ending in the prince's bedroom with swans breaking through | Bourne: the swan's power and wingspan suit men; the plot moves from "magical to psychological" (Wikipedia); to tell a story about emotional repression and royal duty legible to a 1990s audience [documented quote + probable] |

**8. Primary documents and open images.** V&A holds Lez Brotherston costume designs for revivals (e.g. Prince's uniform, watercolour/ink/pencil) — https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O1625573/swan-lake-costume-design-brotherston-lez [seen; V&A images usually non-commercial use]. Programmes (e.g. Sadler's Wells 2006–07). Hugo Glendinning photographs (copyright). Films (DVD).

**9. Best sources.**
- Alastair Macaulay (ed.), *Matthew Bourne and His Adventures in Motion Pictures* (Faber, 1999; 2nd ed. 2011).
- Vida L. Midgelow, *Reworking the Ballet: Counter-Narratives and Alternative Bodies* (Routledge, 2007) [probable].
- Ramsay Burt, *The Male Dancer: Bodies, Spectacle, Sexualities* (Routledge, 1995; rev. 2007) [probable, on gender and the male swan].
- Jennifer Fisher and Anthony Shay (eds), *When Men Dance* (OUP, 2009) [probable].
- BBC Culture, "'It was a shock to many': Matthew Bourne on his *Swan Lake*", 27 Nov 2024.

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].** Premise: the swan as the prince's desire. Rules: male flock; no pointe; the same body plays Swan and Stranger. Anti-DNA: tutus, magician, rescue. Anchor expression: the feathered breeches and forehead triangle (an image so strong it became a brand and a film ending). Rights and money: an author-titled commercial property touring for 30 years. Adjacent translation: *Billy Elliot*; anniversary merchandising; cinema releases.

## 7. Dada Masilo: *Swan Lake* (2010) and *Giselle* (2017)

Dada Masilo (Soweto 1985 – Johannesburg, 29 December 2024 [documented, NYT 31 Dec 2024; South African Dept of Sport, Arts and Culture statement]); company Dada Masilo/The Dance Factory, Johannesburg.
- *Swan Lake*: commissioned by and premiered at the National Arts Festival, Grahamstown (now Makhanda), July 2010; c. 60 minutes, 14–15 dancers [documented, NAC Ottawa press release 2016].
- *Giselle*: world premiere Dansens Hus, Oslo, 4 May 2017; SA preview UJ Theatre 24–25 June; SA premiere National Arts Festival 29 June–1 July 2017 [documented, Talk of the Town; Romaeuropa; Dansens Hus].

**1. Premise.**
- *Swan Lake*: Odette (Masilo) is married off to Siegfried, but Siegfried loves Odile, a male swan; society disapproves and "no one lives happily ever after" [documented, NYT 2016]. Masilo: "I wanted my version to be more realistic and not a fairy tale"; "homophobia is real and rife" [documented, NAC 2016]. The final trio began as a solo to Saint-Saëns' "Dying Swan", "a requiem for my aunt who died of AIDS" whose real killer was stigma [documented, Pittsburgh City Paper 2016].
- *Giselle*: "a rough and violent version"; a poor village girl (named Mbali, "flower", in some accounts) betrayed by a man from another world; the Wilis as ancestral spirits who take revenge; "At the end, there is no forgiveness" [documented, Gilbert/Dance Consortium 2020; Talk of the Town 2017]. Demerson reads it as "a decolonial love story" that "indigenizes the ballet" through ancestorism, gender fluidity, signifyin(g) and Tswana movement [documented, abstract, *DRJ* 2023].

**2. Roots.**
- The originals (1841, 1895) and their music; Masilo trained in classical ballet and contemporary dance at Johannesburg's National School of the Arts, then Jazzart (Cape Town) and P.A.R.T.S. (Brussels), where she was "made to" create [documented, NAC; Gilbert].
- A childhood ambition: "I remember thinking … I would create my own Swan Lake one day" [documented, NAC].
- South African social facts: homophobia, HIV/AIDS stigma, bride-price (lobola) negotiations [documented for homophobia/AIDS; lobola probable from reviews].
- Movement sources: South African township and Tswana dance (North West Province), stomping, hip-shaking, ululation [documented, NYT; Gilbert].
- Precedents: Bourne's and Ek's reworkings are named by critics alongside hers [documented, City Paper]; her earlier *Romeo and Juliet* (2008) and *Carmen* (2009), also National Arts Festival commissions [documented, NAC].

**3. Who had it and how it was developed.** Masilo was originator, choreographer, co-costume designer and lead dancer. Three successive National Arts Festival commissions (2008, 2009, 2010) built the method of "retakes on the classics" [documented, NAC]. *Swan Lake*'s European touring came via Interarts Lausanne after her 2011 Brest solo [documented]. *Giselle* was a co-commission of a global network: Joyce Theater (Weinroth Fund), Hopkins Center (Dartmouth), Biennale de la danse de Lyon 2018, Sadler's Wells; also La Bâtie Geneva; SAMRO Foundation support [documented, Romaeuropa credits]. Why *Giselle*: it "had never been Masilo's favourite ballet", but she was intrigued by the Wilis, whose viciousness she felt tulle and delicate steps "played down" [documented, Gilbert]. She denies #MeToo influence: the piece predated it by 18 months; "I was just following the narrative!" [documented, Gilbert].

**4. The world it built.**
- *Swan Lake*: barefoot ballet with African dance; men in tutus and on pointe; bare-chested, bare-breasted corps; spoken narration that mocks ballet conventions ("surplus girls in the moonlight"); score of Tchaikovsky plus René Avenant, Saint-Saëns, Arvo Pärt, Steve Reich; androgynous characters [documented, NAC; NYT]. Headdress of white feathers on Masilo's shaved head [documented, Gilbert].
- *Giselle*: Act I village; Act II the spirit world; Myrtha becomes a sangoma (traditional healer) danced by a man (Llewellyn Mnguni); Wilis of both sexes; Giselle stripped twice (by her mother, then in her madness) instead of the hair-loosening mad scene; ancestors resurrect her and the prince is haunted to death [documented, Gilbert; Talk of the Town]. Movement: "earthy downward vigour" of Tswana dance, "the exact opposite of the 1841 ballet's airy grace" [documented, Gilbert]. Music: new score by Philip Miller, live voices and strings with African percussion; drawings by William Kentridge as scenery [documented, Romaeuropa].
- Masilo's rule: "I don't use a single dance language because I don't want to regurgitate" [documented, Gilbert].
- Audience rule: "I want … these classical works [to] speak to someone from a township in Soweto or from a rural area" [documented, Gilbert, condensed].
- Anti-DNA [argued]: no fairy tale, no forgiveness, no abstract shapes without story, no fixed technique, no hair (the shaved head as personal signature).

**5. The team and the credit.**
- *Swan Lake*: choreography Masilo; costumes Masilo and Suzette le Sueur; costume construction Ann and Kirsten Bailes; headdresses Karabo Legoabe; original production and lighting le Sueur [documented, NAC].
- *Giselle*: music Philip Miller; drawings William Kentridge; assistant director David April; lighting Suzette le Sueur; costumes David Hutt (Donker Nag Helder Dag, Act I), Songezo Mcilizeli and Nonofo Olekeng (Those Two Lifestyle, Act II); cast Masilo, Kyle Rossouw (Albrecht), Tshepo Zasekhaya (Hilarion), Llewellyn Mnguni (Myrtha), Liyabuya Gongo (Bathilde), Khaya Ndlovu (mother) [documented, Dansens Hus; Romaeuropa]. Credit lines name the fashion labels, an unusual and deliberate link to Johannesburg design [argued].

**6. Production.** Small company (12–15 dancers), festival-commissioned in South Africa, financed for touring by European and US presenters; *Swan Lake* toured France, Italy, Switzerland, Sweden, Germany, Canada, US (Joyce, Feb 2016) [documented]. Running times short (60 min / 110 min). Budget not found.

**7. Reception and afterlife.** NYT (2016) found its politics "simple — against homophobia" and its satire "too gentle" [documented]. Guardian praised "wit and seriousness" [documented, via City Paper]. Sadler's Wells and Dance Consortium UK tour 2019–20 of *Giselle* [documented]. Standard Bank Young Artist Award 2008 [documented]. Her final work *The Sacrifice* (2022, after *Rite of Spring*) [probable].

**Kept / changed / why.**

| Work | Kept | Changed | Why (Masilo) |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Swan Lake* | Characters, Tchaikovsky excerpts, white/black swan pairing, tragic end | Siegfried loves a male Odile; Odette is the abandoned bride; music collaged; narration; African dance; men on pointe | Realism, not fairy tale; homophobia and AIDS stigma in South Africa; a requiem for her aunt [documented] |
| *Giselle* | "Almost all of its elements": betrayal, madness, death, Wilis, Myrtha, Albrecht's survival into Act II | No forgiveness; Myrtha a male sangoma; mixed-sex Wilis as ancestors; Tswana dance; new score; Giselle stripped | Wilis' revenge "played down" by tulle; to make the story legible to Soweto and rural audiences; ancestral cosmology replaces Christian forgiveness [documented + Demerson's argued reading] |

**8. Primary documents and open images.** Programmes (NAC 2016; Dansens Hus 2017). Photographs by John Hogg and Suzanne Gerber (copyright). National Arts Festival archive, Makhanda [probable]. No open images found.

**9. Best sources.**
- Rainy Demerson, "Dada Masilo's *Giselle*: A Decolonial Love Story", *Dance Research Journal* (CUP, 2023).
- Jenny Gilbert, "No Forgiveness", Dance Consortium feature (2020), long interview.
- "Review: Dada Masilo's *Swan Lake* Offers Ill-Fated Love With a Twist", *New York Times*, 3 Feb 2016 (reviewer not seen).
- Lyndsey Winship, "Dada Masilo obituary", *Guardian*, 10 Jan 2025.
- Scholarship on South African contemporary dance more broadly not searched in this pass (gap).

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].** Premise: the classic told so a township audience recognises itself. Rules: change the dance language every project; the choreographer dances the lead; shout the counts. Anti-DNA: forgiveness, fairy tale, abstraction. Obligations: to a South African audience first, to festival co-commissioners second. Anchor expression: the shaved head with a white-feather crown; the male sangoma Myrtha. Rights and money: an international co-commissioning consortium funds a South African remake for global touring: a new economic model for re-conceptualising the canon.

## 8. Akram Khan: *Giselle* (English National Ballet, 2016)

English National Ballet (ENB). World premiere 27 September 2016, Palace Theatre, Manchester [documented, Akram Khan Company page].

**1. Premise.** *Giselle* among a community of migrant garment workers, locked out of a closed factory and now entertaining the "Landlords" who own it; in Act II the ghosts of dead factory workers take revenge [documented, britishballetnowandthen 2019; ENB copy: "a divided world of migrant Outcasts and exploitative Landlords"]. Khan's framing: the core of *Giselle* is "love, betrayal and forgiveness … a universal trilogy"; "'classical' is not about being in a specific time, but … about timelessness" [documented, Chicago Tribune 2019 via AKC]. Tamara Rojo's framing: the ballet lives "in both the real and spiritual world", which Khan's work also represents [documented, Tim Yip studio page].

**2. Roots.**
- The 1841 ballet's dualisms (village/forest, rich/poor, flesh/spirit), extracted as themes by Khan and dramaturg Ruth Little [documented, Tribune].
- Politics: the Rana Plaza collapse, Bangladesh, April 2013 (over 1,100 dead), Bangladesh being Khan's parents' country; and xenophobia around migration into Europe [documented, Tribune].
- Khan's own forms: kathak and contemporary dance; he had seen only one *Giselle*, "as a curious outsider" [documented, Guardian 2016 (Mackrell)].
- Previous ENB collaboration: *Dust* (2014), in ENB's First World War programme *Lest We Forget* [documented].

**3. Who had it and how it was developed.**
- Originator of the commission: Tamara Rojo, ENB artistic director from 2012, who "decided I wanted to bring *Giselle* … into the 21st Century" and chose Khan for his knowledge of tradition and "the spirituality of the theme" [documented, danzaballet 2016; britishballetnowandthen].
- Originator of the concept: Khan with Ruth Little (dramaturg). Process recorded: theme extraction from the original; relocation "into a recognizable time and place, without losing the heart of what people love" [documented, Tribune].
- Rojo on the effect of making it: "we felt better than ever going back to the traditional because we knew it better, and because we'd challenged it" [documented, Tribune; condensed].
- NYT called it "a courageous, expensive gamble" for Rojo and Khan's first full-length work for a ballet company [documented, Sulcas 2016].

**4. The world it built.**
- Setting: a ghost industrial zone; a 20-ft wall designed by Tim Yip that "rises and revolves, like a giant domino on a spit", dividing migrants from Landlords; the wall reads as capitalism, migration barrier and the promise on the other side [documented, Tim Yip studio quoting press; Tribune].
- Palette and costume: frayed, earth-toned clothes for the workers; extravagant, "pointless" luxury for the Landlords (Yip) [documented, press quoted on Yip studio page].
- Movement: classical ballet, kathak and contemporary fused; the opening image is the corps pushing against the wall [documented, ENB; britishballetnowandthen]. The Wilis remain on pointe but as vengeful factory ghosts; Myrtha wields a stick [probable, from reviews/film].
- Sound: Vincenzo Lamagna's composition "after the original score by Adolphe Adam", layering Adam's themes with industrial soundscape; orchestration Gavin Sutherland; Khan says a connoisseur "will recognize [it] most of the way through" [documented, AKC credits; Tribune].
- Hilarion as a middleman between Outcasts and Landlords [probable].
- Anti-DNA [argued]: no idyllic village, no harvest festival, no tutu romance, no forgiveness as pure Christian redemption (contested: Khan keeps forgiveness in his "trilogy" but the ending separates Giselle and Albrecht by the wall).

**5. The team and the credit.** Direction and choreography Akram Khan; visual and costume design Tim Yip (Oscar for *Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon*); composition and sound design Vincenzo Lamagna after Adam; orchestration Gavin Sutherland; lighting Mark Henderson; dramaturgy Ruth Little; assistant choreographer Andrej Petrovič; répétiteur Mavin Khoo; sound design Yvonne Gilbert [documented, AKC credits]. Coralli and Perrot are dropped from the credits; Adam is kept "after"; the title is "Akram Khan's *Giselle*" [documented]. Original Giselles: Alina Cojocaru and Tamara Rojo [documented]; in the 2017 film, Rojo (Giselle), James Streeter (Albrecht), Jeffrey Cirio (Hilarion), Stina Quagebeur (Myrtha) [documented, Official London Theatre].

**6. Production.** ENB (a touring national company) with live ENB Philharmonic; premiere in Manchester then UK tour (Southampton, Bristol, London Sadler's Wells) Sept–Nov 2016 [documented, danzaballet]. US exclusive at the Harris Theater Chicago 2019 [documented]. Revived London Coliseum January 2026 [documented, ENB site]. Budget not disclosed; "expensive" per NYT.

**7. Reception and afterlife.** Nominated for the 2017 Olivier for Best New Dance Production; ENB won the Olivier for Outstanding Achievement in Dance "for expanding the variety of their repertoire with *Giselle* and *She Said*"; also the Manchester Theatre Awards and the South Bank Sky Arts Award [documented, BBC 2017; ENB blog]. Filmed live at the Liverpool Empire, October 2017, directed for screen by Ross MacGibbon, commissioned by The Space; ENB's first cinema release, 25 April 2018; Royal Ballet of Flanders hired the production in 2018 [documented, Official London Theatre; ENB blog]. Rojo staged a traditional *Giselle* the same season [documented, Tribune]: the remake and the original sold side by side.

**Kept / changed / why.**

| Kept | Changed | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The "trilogy" of love, betrayal, forgiveness; the characters; the two-world structure; Adam's themes (re-composed); pointe for Wilis; Giselle's death and the ghost revenge | Peasants become migrant garment workers; aristocrats become Landlords; the forest becomes a ruined factory; the wall replaces the cottage; score re-composed with soundscape; kathak in the vocabulary; the credit line | Rojo's wish to make the classical repertoire "continue to mean something"; Khan's heritage and Rana Plaza; European migration politics; to convince classical audiences that the classical is "timelessness" [documented] |

**8. Primary documents and open images.** ENB archive and programmes; Laurent Liotardo photographs (ENB copyright) [documented credit, NYT]; Tim Yip's design studio pages [documented]. Streaming of the film via AKC/ENB [documented]. No open images.

**9. Best sources.**
- Judith Mackrell, "Akram Khan's refugee *Giselle*", *Guardian*, 27 Sept 2016.
- Roslyn Sulcas, "Review: *Giselle*, a Winning Gamble for English National Ballet", *NYT*, 28 Sept 2016.
- Kay Harlow, *Chicago Tribune*, 2019 (PDF on AKC site).
- "Giselle Productions Now & Then", British Ballet Now and Then blog (2019), comparative analysis.
- Royona Mitra, *Akram Khan: Dancing New Interculturalism* (Palgrave, 2015) — on Khan's method before *Giselle* [probable].

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].** Premise: Giselle as a migrant worker. Perimeter: one factory, one wall. Rules: the wall governs space; the classical vocabulary belongs to the ghosts; workers' bodies push, the Landlords' bodies display. Obligations: to the 1841 text's emotional core; to a national ballet company's corps on pointe. Anchor expression: the wall. Adjacent translation: cinema release, streaming. Lifecycle: an institution commissions the remake to keep its own canon alive, then programmes both versions in one season.

## 9. Alexei Ratmansky: *The Sleeping Beauty* (2015) and reconstruction from notation

American Ballet Theatre (ABT), co-production with Teatro alla Scala. "Choreography by Marius Petipa; staging and additional choreography by Alexei Ratmansky, assisted by Tatiana Ratmansky"; scenery and costumes Richard Hudson "inspired by Léon Bakst"; lighting James F. Ingalls. World premiere 3 March 2015, Segerstrom Center for the Arts, Costa Mesa, California (Vishneva, Gomes); New York 29 May 2015, Metropolitan Opera House; Milan, September 2015 [documented, ABT production page; DanceTabs]. (Scope file 05 already covers the notation's limits and the "221 pages" quote; this entry treats reconstruction as a concept.)

**1. Premise.** Remaking by subtraction: "to wash away the layers of tradition and habit" and see the 1890 ballet afresh [documented, Harss, DanceTabs 2015]. Ratmansky's question, raised by Vikharev's 1999 Mariinsky reconstruction: "How different is the 'traditional' from the 'original'?" [documented, Ratmansky Q&A 2015]. Fullington frames it as restoring Petipa's balance of mime and dance, the *pas d'action* at the centre of each act [documented, Fullington, *Oxford Handbook* chapter, 2017]. ABT's director Kevin McKenzie wanted "an iconic new" *Beauty* for the company's 75th season [documented, CriticalDance].

**2. Roots.**
- The 1890 St Petersburg production (Vsevolozhsky libretto, Petipa, Tchaikovsky) — covered in W1.
- Stepanov notation of *Beauty* made c. 1903, Harvard Theatre Collection, bMS Thr 245 (204), with piano reductions bMS Thr 245 (205) [documented, Fullington].
- Other sources: Pavel Gerdt's drawings of the Act III adagio; Lopukhov's and Gusev's descriptions of the Prologue pas de six; Cyril Beaumont's accounts of the 1921 and 1946 London productions; pre-war Ballets Russes films (from Australia) and old Royal Ballet films; Imperial-era photographs and reviews [documented, Fullington; Ratmansky Q&A].
- Visual: Bakst's designs for Diaghilev's *The Sleeping Princess* (London, 1921) [documented].
- Earlier reconstructions: Sergeyev's Vic-Wells staging (1939), Vikharev's Mariinsky reconstruction (1999), Ratmansky's own *Paquita* (Bavarian State Ballet, 2014, with Fullington) [documented].

**3. Who had it and how it was developed.** Originator: Ratmansky, who learned to read Stepanov notation in spring 2014 "to find out what Petipa's original text is and to connect/compare it to Vaganova, Balanchine, Ashton" [documented]. Team: Tatiana Ratmansky (assistant; they spent months decoding together) [documented, Guardian 2015]; Doug Fullington (notation consultant on *Paquita*; scholar of the *Beauty* production) [documented]; Richard Hudson (who had designed Ratmansky's 2010 ABT *Nutcracker*; agreed to "riff on, rather than slavishly copy" Bakst); dance historian Caroline Hamilton located surviving Bakst costumes in museums worldwide [documented, NYT 28 May 2015]. The idea of using Bakst first arose when Ratmansky planned a *Beauty* for the reopening of the Bolshoi's historic stage (2011), a project that did not happen in that form [documented, Q&A].

**4. The world it built (the rules of a reconstruction).**
- Steps: "follow the notation as literally as possible: the extension of the leg, the position of the feet, the degree the leg is bent" (Fullington) [documented]. Result: lower extensions, lower passés, smaller jumps, more épaulement and footwork, softer pointe work; "deceptively understated" [documented, CriticalDance; probable on specifics].
- Mime restored throughout ("all the mime, although shortened") [documented].
- Gaps filled by Ratmansky: Prologue cavaliers and Carabosse's attendants, the Awakening, Red Riding Hood, the Apotheosis [documented].
- Admitted departures: fewer dancers than 1890; Bakst not 1890 designs; some 1921 characters in the Act III procession; historically important later additions kept (the Rose Adagio balances with arms *en couronne*, "not done that way until Margot Fonteyn"; the fish dives, possibly Vladimirov's 1921 invention); score cuts to fit three hours under "union rules" [documented, Q&A].
- Design: Hudson's economical sets frame Bakst-derived costumes in which, Ratmansky notes, Bakst made the 17th and 18th centuries "all look like Louis XIV" [documented].
- Anti-DNA [argued]: modern hyperextension, the Soviet 1952 Konstantin Sergeyev redaction's cuts of mime and pages, the idea that "tradition" equals "original".

**5. The team and the credit.** The credit line puts Petipa first and Ratmansky as "staging and additional choreography", a careful authorship statement [documented, ABT]. Hudson's credit "inspired by Léon Bakst" is a second layered credit. Stepanov, the notators (Sergeyev and others), and the 1890s dancers whose names appear in the notation (Kschessinska, Gerdt, Marie Petipa, Pavlova, Karsavina, Vaganova, Legat) are invisible in the programme but named by Ratmansky as proof of authenticity [documented, Q&A].

**6. Production.** ABT 75th anniversary; co-produced with La Scala; supported by the Fedora prize in 2015 [documented, Fedora platform]. Premiere at Costa Mesa (Segerstrom co-commissioned, probable). Budget not disclosed.

**7. Reception and afterlife.** Critics hailed a ballet "the world hasn't seen … since 1890" [documented, CriticalDance], while the dancers had to "go against their habits" [documented, La Scala/Fedora]. Debate (also in scope 02/05): reconstruction is "necessarily interpretative" (Fullington) and Croce's "magic empathy" is needed [documented, quoted by Harss]. Ratmansky went on to reconstruct *Swan Lake* (Zurich 2016), *Harlequinade* (ABT 2018), *Giselle* (Bolshoi 2019) [documented in scope 02; probable for Zurich].

**Kept / changed / why.**

| Kept | Changed | Why |
|---|---|---|
| All notated choreography; Petipa's mime (shortened); Tchaikovsky's order; the *pas d'action* structure | Designs after Bakst 1921 not 1890; reduced cast; some 1921 characters; later "historically important" additions retained; score cut to 3 hours; gaps assembled from films and descriptions | To recover Petipa's style beneath accretion; to compare "original" and "traditional"; practical limits (budget, cast size, union time rules); Bakst's designs "as much part of the *Sleeping Beauty* myth as any other component" [documented] |

**8. Primary documents and open images.** Harvard Theatre Collection, Houghton Library, Sergeyev Collection, bMS Thr 245 (204–205) — digitised, viewing online [probable; scope 02 cites MS Thr 245]. Bakst designs for *The Sleeping Princess* (1921): many in museums; Bakst died 1924, so his designs are public domain in most jurisdictions (life+70), though museum photographs may carry terms [argued; check each holder: V&A, Wadsworth Atheneum, Musée des Arts Décoratifs].

**9. Best sources.**
- Doug Fullington, "Finding the Balance: Pantomime and Dance in Ratmansky's New/Old *Sleeping Beauty*", *Oxford Handbook* (online 2017).
- Marina Harss, "Alexei Ratmansky – Simple and Wise", DanceTabs, Aug 2015 (Q&A); and "Doug Fullington on Stepanov Notation", DanceTabs, June 2015.
- Roland John Wiley, *Dances from Russia: An Introduction to the Sergejev Collection* (Harvard, 1976); *Tchaikovsky's Ballets* (OUP, 1985).
- Maureen Gupta, *Nicolai Serguéeff and The Sleeping Beauty* (UBC Press) (scope 05).
- Tim Scholl, *Sleeping Beauty: A Legend in Progress* (Yale UP, 2004) — on the 1999 Vikharev reconstruction [probable].

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].** Premise: the original is a lost world to be re-entered. Rules: the notation outranks habit; every deviation must be declared. Sources field: the strongest of any work here, with call numbers. Obligations: to Petipa first, to today's bodies second, to union rules third. Lifecycle: a work remade not by adding a new concept but by removing the accreted ones: remaking as archaeology. Lesson for learners: a "faithful" version is also a design decision, with its own anti-DNA.

## 10. An Arab or Gulf original work: *El-Leila El-Kebira* (Cairo Opera Ballet, 2001/02)

الليلة الكبيرة, "The Grand Night". Ballet version directed and choreographed (and designed) by Abdel-Moneim Kamel for the Cairo Opera Ballet Company; music by Sayed Mekawy (سيد مكاوي), re-orchestrated by Gamal Salama; verse by Salah Jahin (صلاح جاهين) [documented, Al Bawaba 3 Jan 2002; Elaph 2 Jan 2002; Ahram Online].

**Why this work.** The search found no resident ballet company in the Gulf; Gulf houses present touring ballet and commission opera or dance-theatre (see "Gulf context" below; also scope 01 §3.5). Cairo is the Arab world's main ballet institution (scope 01). Among Cairo's Arab-themed originals (*Osiris*, *Hassan and Naïma*, *Egyptian Night*, *El Nil*, *Cleopatra* 2019 [documented, Cairo Opera company page; Ahram 2019]), *El-Leila El-Kebira* is the one built on an Arabic literary source and the one the Cairo press itself framed as a possible "Arabic ballet".

**1. Premise.** A night at a village *moulid* (festival of the Prophet's birthday): food-sellers, a fortune teller, a shooting gallery, circus jugglers, clowns, a lion tamer, a belly dancer, a lost child, all in a carnival of popular Egyptian life [documented, Wikipedia; Egypt Independent 2020]. The ballet's concept: "puppets turn into live dancers" [documented, Ahram Online]. Elaph's reviewer framed the result as "a kind of Arabic ballet", combining ballet's "geometric and calculated" steps with the spontaneity of *raqs sharqi* [documented, Elaph 2002, Arabic, paraphrased].

**2. Roots.**
- Literary source: Salah Jahin's colloquial Egyptian verse operetta, written at Sayed Mekawy's prompting, who "drew his attention to the life of the working class" [documented, Al Bawaba/Elaph, contested on whether mid-1960s or 1961].
- The original: puppet operetta, premiered 1 May 1961 (Wikipedia) or "in the 1960s" (Cairo Opera); c. 40 minutes; theatre director Salah El-Sakka; art direction/puppets Nagy Shaker; décor Mostafa Kamel; voices including Sayed Mekawy, Salah Jahin, Mohamed Roshdy, Houria Hassan; televised, colourised in the early 1980s; called "the Arab world's most iconic puppet show" [documented, Wikipedia; Egypt Independent] [contested date: 1960 (Egypt Independent) vs 1961 (Wikipedia)].
- Folk source: the *moulid* around the mosques of al-Hussein, Sayyida Zaynab, Aisha and Nafisa in Cairo [documented, Elaph].
- Ballet source: the Soviet-trained Cairo company (1966, Lavrovsky's *Fountain of Bakhchisarai*) and Kamel's European career [documented, Cairo Opera page].

**3. Who had it and how it was developed.** Originator of the ballet: Abdel-Moneim Kamel (dancer trained at the Cairo Higher Institute of Ballet, later head of the Cairo Opera House) [documented as director; probable on biography]. Development choices recorded in the press: because Egyptian and Arab audiences had linked the work with marionette movement "for more than 37 years", some dances, especially the belly dances, were designed to imitate the sharp broken movements of puppets [documented, Al Bawaba; Elaph]. Premiere attended by First Lady Suzanne Mubarak and ministers for a children's celebration, with a children's choir under Selim Sahab and the Opera orchestra under Taha Nagi [documented, Elaph]. Kamel's company page dates production to 2001; Elaph reports the performance on Wednesday 2 January 2002 [contested: late 2001 vs Jan 2002 premiere].

**4. The world it built.**
- Setting: a moving décor carrying *moulid* symbols: circus, the chickpea seller, the shooting gallery, games of strength [documented, Elaph].
- Palette: "bright green to blue and red", the colours of popular festivity and childhood [documented, Elaph].
- Movement rules: three vocabularies blended: classical ballet, Egyptian *raqs sharqi*, and puppet-like angular breaks that quote the marionettes [documented, Elaph; Al Bawaba].
- Music: Mekawy's tunes and Jahin's sung verse kept, re-orchestrated for symphony orchestra [documented].
- Anti-DNA [argued]: no European fairy tale, no Pharaonic exoticism (contrast *Osiris*, *Cleopatra*), no tragedy; the subject is the urban/rural popular class, not royalty.

**5. The team and the credit.** Kamel (direction, choreography, design); Mekawy (music); Jahin (verse); Gamal Salama (orchestration); Selim Sahab (children's choir); Taha Nagi (conductor) [documented]. The 1961 puppet-makers (Nagy Shaker, Salah El-Sakka) are not in the ballet's credit line though the ballet quotes their puppets' movement [argued from absence in reports].

**6. Production.** State production of the Cairo Opera House (Ministry of Culture), main hall; usually programmed as a short work paired with another (e.g. *Oriental Steps* to Attiya Sharara's 2nd Violin Concerto) [documented, Ahram Online]. Audience: families and children; national occasions. Erminia Gambarelli Kamel (artistic director from 2004) prepared it with *Zorba* for the company's first big tour to the Royal Opera House Muscat in 2020; the tour was halted by the pandemic [documented for preparation, Metwaly 2020; probable for cancellation]. The ballet was streamed on the Ministry of Culture YouTube channel, March 2020 [documented].

**7. Reception and afterlife.** In the standard repertory, revived regularly (e.g. May 2014 photo gallery; 2020 stream) [documented]. The puppet original continues in parallel at the Cairo Puppet Theatre (e.g. Damanhour Opera House, Nov 2023) [documented]: two versions of one concept co-exist, in two media.

**Kept / changed / why.**

| Kept | Changed | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Jahin's verse and Mekawy's songs; the *moulid* characters and sequence; the colours of the folk fair | Puppets → dancers; score orchestrated; belly dance and ballet joined; puppet movement quoted as choreographic style | To bring a beloved Egyptian popular work into the national ballet company and make an "Egyptian ballet"; to honour 37 years of audience memory of the marionettes [documented] |

**Gulf context (for the course).** The Royal Opera House Muscat (opened 2011) commissions Arab stage works but not ballets with a resident company: *Ibn Battuta: The Winged Traveller* (30–31 May 2015, with Arabesque International; dir./choreographer Walid Aouni; composer Hisham Gabr; also Expo Milan 2015) [documented, Arabesque International]; *Sindbad: The Omani Sailor*, an Arabic-language "grand-opéra" with Müpa Budapest (2025–26 season; composer Hisham Gabr; libretto Nader Salah El Din) that relocates Sindbad's voyage to begin and end in Oman [documented, Oman News Agency, 1 Sep 2025]. Walid Aouni's dance-theatre *Scheherazade* (Cairo, 2000; Muscat May 2024) re-sets Rimsky-Korsakov's 1888 score, opens in the 1950s and adds Aladdin [documented, New Arab 2024]. Fikret Amirov's *One Thousand and One Nights* (Baku 1979; Nazirova choreography; libretto Maqsud and Rustam Ibrahimbeyov) is a Soviet-Azerbaijani ballet on the Arabic source now danced by the Cairo company (2024) and seen in Doha (2023) [documented]. A search for a ballet on Naguib Mahfouz or *Majnun Layla* found nothing in this pass.

**8. Primary documents and open images.** Ministry of Culture YouTube stream (2020); Cairo Opera photographs (Ahram photographer Bassam Al Zoghby); 1961 puppet film (Egyptian TV). No open licences found.

**9. Best sources.** Press only: Ahram Online (Ati Metwaly's reporting; atimetwaly.com interview with Erminia Kamel, 11 May 2020); Elaph (2 Jan 2002, Arabic); Al Bawaba (3 Jan 2002). Scholarly context: Barsova & Podyachev (2023) on the Soviet-built Cairo school (scope 01); no scholarship found on the ballet itself: a gap.

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].** Premise: the national popular festival as ballet. Rules: every dance must remind you of the puppets. Anti-DNA: no foreign fairy tale. Adjacent translation runs backwards here: puppet theatre → TV → ballet (cf. *Red Detachment*'s film → ballet). Obligation: audience memory. Lesson: a non-European ballet concept can come from a national *popular* artwork rather than from myth or ancient history.

## 11. A Japanese original work: Matsuyama Ballet, *The White-Haired Girl* (白毛女, Tokyo, 1955)

Matsuyama Ballet (松山バレエ団, founded January 1948 by 清水正夫 Shimizu Masao and 松山樹子 Matsuyama Mikiko). Premiere 12 February 1955, Hibiya Public Hall (日比谷公会堂), Tokyo [documented, CFLAC; Matsuyama Ballet history page gives February 1955]. Chosen because it is a Japanese original that is also the earliest known ballet adaptation of *The White-Haired Girl* and, per Wilcox, laid "the foundation for Chinese revolutionary ballet" [documented, Wilcox 2023 abstract]: it links directly to §1.

**1. Premise.** A peasant girl, Xi'er, sold to a landlord to pay her father's debt, flees to the mountains, lives in a cave until her hair turns white, is taken for a ghost, and is rescued when the revolutionary army arrives [probable, standard synopsis of the Chinese source]. The makers framed it as a "heartfelt gift" (心からの贈り物) from the Japanese people to the Chinese people [documented, Matsuyama Ballet page]. Japanese political reading at the time: the 1954 Japanese script's half-title addressed "Japanese youths! There are white-haired girls in Japan too" [documented, Qi 2023 quoting Yamada]. The couple saw parallels with US-occupied Japan, where farmland was taken for bases, and with women's liberation [documented, Qi 2023]. Wilcox reads it as a "bridge" between the 1950 Chinese film and the 1971 Chinese ballet film, and as revealing different Japanese and Chinese ideas of Sino-Japanese "friendship" [documented, abstract].

**2. Roots.**
- Chinese source chain: the *geju* (song-drama) *The White-Haired Girl*, Yan'an 1945 (lead Wang Kun); the 1950 film (Changchun, lead Tian Hua), shown in Japan in 1952 [documented, CFLAC].
- How it reached the makers: in late 1952 Shimizu attended a private screening of the "contraband" film in Tokyo, then showed it to Matsuyama and the company [documented, Qi 2023]. Matsuyama recalled the 18-year-old actress Tian Hua, "adorable and perseverant" [documented, CFLAC quoting Shimizu's book].
- Texts: Shimizu wrote to the China Theatre Association; in late 1953 its chairman Tian Han (田漢) sent the script, score and stage photographs; Shimada Masao's Japanese translation of script and score was published 1954 by Mirai-sha with the Japan-China Friendship Association [documented, Qi 2023].
- Earlier Matsuyama original: *白狐の湯* (*Byakko no yu*, "The White Fox's Hot Spring", 1953) [documented, company history].
- Soviet ballet model: the company staged *The Fountain of Bakhchisarai* in Japan in 1957 [documented].

**3. Who had it and how it was developed.**
- Originators: Shimizu Masao (producer, husband) and Matsuyama Mikiko (choreographer and lead) [documented].
- Development: "more than two years" of work; the couple sold their house to fund it, under pressure from the right and anti-China forces [documented, CFLAC]. Music by 林光 Hayashi Hikaru, composed from the Shimada translation of the score [documented, Qi 2023]. Direction by 土方与志 Hijikata Yoshi (left-wing theatre director, Tsukiji Little Theatre founder); costumes by 土方梅子 Hijikata Umeko [documented, CFLAC].
- Political context: the Red Purge in occupied Japan; the company "became an outcast in ballet circles in Japan" [documented, Qi 2023 citing Shimizu].

**4. The world it built.**
- A story of Chinese peasants told by Japanese bodies in ballet technique, with Chinese music material re-composed by a Japanese modernist [documented for team; argued for the "rule"].
- Audience rule: performed about 40 times across Japan 1955–58, mainly to students and workers such as miners [documented, Qi 2023 citing Yamada]: ballet as a workers' art, against its "highbrow" image.
- Image: the white-haired girl (white wig) in a mountain cave; the ghost-woman figure echoes both the Romantic *ballet blanc* and Japanese ghost-theatre (yūrei) traditions [argued].
- Anti-DNA [argued]: no European fairy tale, no Japanese self-exoticism; the source is contemporary Chinese revolutionary art.

**5. The team and the credit.** Choreography and lead: Matsuyama Mikiko; producer: Shimizu Masao; director: Hijikata Yoshi; costumes: Hijikata Umeko; music: Hayashi Hikaru; translation: Shimada Masao; source credit owed to Tian Han, the Yan'an collective authors (He Jingzhi and Ding Yi, librettists; Ma Ke and others, composers) and the 1950 film [documented for Japanese team; probable for Chinese authors]. In China's later histories the Matsuyama production is often omitted from the lineage of the 1965 Shanghai ballet [argued, from Wilcox's "first study in English" claim].

**6. Production.** Small private company, self-funded (house sold); Tokyo public hall premiere; 40 performances in three years; first China tour 1958 (*White-Haired Girl* and others), received by Zhou Enlai [probable]; 13 China tours by 2003 [documented, company history]. Second tour 1964 [documented, NYU Shanghai].

**7. Reception and afterlife.** Controversy in Japan; acclaim in China [documented, Qi]. The Shanghai Dance School began its own *White-Haired Girl* ballet in 1964, full-length 1965/66, later one of the eight model works [documented, NYU Shanghai: full-length 1966; Wilcox ch. 4]. Revised three times; new production by 清水哲太郎 Shimizu Tetsutarō (son) in 2011, toured China (13th tour) with a message of "gratitude and apology" to Chinese predecessors [documented, Matsuyama Ballet page]. Morishita Yōko (森下洋子), who joined in 1971, became its later star [documented, NNTT timeline]. A case of "ballet diplomacy" (Qi) across half a century.

**Kept / changed / why.**

| Kept (from Chinese opera/film) | Changed (Japanese ballet) | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Story of Xi'er, landlord, white hair, rescue; Chinese songs as melodic material | Medium: sung drama/film → ballet; music re-composed by Hayashi; Japanese cast; framing as a gift of friendship and as Japan's own story ("white-haired girls in Japan too") | Emotional response to the film; anti-base, anti-occupation politics; women's liberation; to bring ballet to workers; to build Japan–China friendship [documented] |

**Contrast case (one line).** Maurice Béjart's *The Kabuki* (Tokyo Ballet, April 1986; music commissioned from 黛敏郎 Mayuzumi Toshirō) turned the kabuki *Kanadehon Chūshingura* into a two-act ballet with Japanese dance gesture, and toured Covent Garden, La Scala, Paris and Vienna in 1986 [documented, NBS News 325]: a Japanese original concept authored by a European for export, the reverse of the Matsuyama route.

**8. ## 12. Cross-work patterns: how ballet concepts are born, developed and remade

All of this section is **[argued]**, drawn from the eleven entries above.

**12.1 Who originates.** The person who has the idea is often not the choreographer.
- **The state or a patron sets the brief; artists find the subject.** Zhou Enlai asked for "a revolutionary subject" and the troupe chose the film (§1). Grigorovich answered a Bolshoi commission for "a heroic work" (§2). Kevin McKenzie wanted a 75th-anniversary *Beauty* (§9). Tamara Rojo chose *Giselle* and then chose Khan (§8). Egypt's Ministry of Culture gave Kamel the national stage (§10).
- **The company director as author.** Mitchell (§4), Alonso (§3), Ek (§5), Bourne (§6) and Masilo (§7) all ran the company that made the work. Rojo commissioned *and* danced the lead. Where author and institution are one, the concept can be radical, because no one else has to approve it.
- **The performer as originator.** Alonso's *Giselle* grew out of a role she had danced (1943), and Masilo always dances her own lead. Matsuyama Mikiko danced the girl she had seen on film. An interpretation hardens into a staging, and the staging into a concept.
- **The couple or pair.** Shimizu and Matsuyama, Alicia and Fernando Alonso, Alexei and Tatiana Ratmansky, Ek and Laguna, Mitchell and Franklin. The concept is often a two-person invention that the credit line gives to one name.

**12.2 Where the sources come from.** There are five kinds:
1. **A canonical ballet** to be reread: *Giselle* five times (§§3, 4, 5, 7, 8), *Swan Lake* three times (§§5, 6, 7), *Sleeping Beauty* once (§9).
2. **A film**: *Red Detachment* (1961 film) and *White-Haired Girl* (1950 film), and Bourne's debt to Hitchcock. Twentieth-century ballet concepts often begin in the cinema.
3. **A news event or social fact**: Rana Plaza (§8), AIDS stigma and homophobia (§7), European migration (§8), the occupation of Japan (§11), the royal tabloids (§6).
4. **History researched for the occasion**: free people of colour in 1840s Louisiana (§4), Hainan's women soldiers (§1), Appian and Plutarch (§2), Stepanov notation (§9).
5. **A national popular artwork**: Jahin's puppet operetta (§10), Hainan folk song (§1), Tswana dance (§7).

Borrowing across cultures is a two-way route here. Japan borrowed from China (§11), and China then borrowed back from Japan's adaptation. Cuba took *Giselle* and "gave it back" to Paris (§3). A Bangladeshi-British choreographer and a Hong Kong designer remade a French ballet for an English company (§8).

**12.3 How teams form.** Three recurring roles stand beside the choreographer:
- **A dramaturg or story-analyst**: Ruth Little (§8), Pineda Barnet's "table work" (§3), Mitchell and Michel's scenario with its "historical notes" (§4), Ratmansky as his own notation scholar with Fullington (§9).
- **A designer whose single image carries the concept**: Yip's wall, Brotherston's feathered breeches, Ekman's asylum and bald swans, Bakst via Hudson, Kentridge's drawings. In nearly every case the anchor expression is a design object, not a step.
- **A composer who keeps the old score recognisable while changing it**: Lamagna "after Adam" (§8), Grigorovich's "battle for the notes" (§2), Bourne's reordering (§6), Masilo's collage (§7), Hayashi's re-composition (§11), Gamal Salama's orchestration (§10).

Committees appear under the state: a forum, then a team appointed by a vice-minister (§1), then a party leader's revisions. They also appear in global co-commissioning, where five presenters on three continents funded *Giselle* in §7. Both are ways of spreading risk; they differ in who may change the work afterwards.

**12.4 How worlds get rules.** Every remake keeps a "sacred" layer and opens a "free" layer. The line between them is the concept.
- **Keep the steps, change the world**: *Creole Giselle* (§4) and Alonso (§3; she kept the "original elements" common to all versions).
- **Keep the score, change the steps and the world**: Ek, Bourne, Khan.
- **Keep the story, change the music and the dance language**: Masilo; *El-Leila El-Kebira* (§10, kept the songs and changed the medium).
- **Keep the documents, remove the accretions**: Ratmansky (§9).
- **Keep the history, impose a doctrine**: *Red Detachment*'s 1965 revision (§1).

The rules nearly always come in pairs of opposites, inherited from *Giselle*'s own dualisms: village and forest, flesh and spirit, poor and rich. Remakers re-map these pairs onto a new axis: freed and enslaved (§4), sane and mad (§5), migrant and landlord (§8), living and ancestor (§7), worker and landlord (§1).

Anti-DNA is usually explicit and usually concerns the same three things: the tutu or pointe as a sign of ethereality, the fairy-tale ending (forgiveness, rescue), and the aristocrat as hero.

**12.5 How works are remade.** The entries show five modes of re-conceptualisation, which can serve as a teaching taxonomy:

| Mode | Example | Question the remaker asks |
|---|---|---|
| Re-setting | *Creole Giselle*; Khan | "Where else does this hierarchy exist?" |
| Re-psychologising | Ek; Bourne | "What if the supernatural is inside the hero?" |
| Re-politicising | *Red Detachment* 1965; Masilo; Matsuyama | "Whose story should this be, and for whom?" |
| Re-motivating | Alonso | "Why does each character do what they do?" |
| Re-excavating | Ratmansky; Vikharev | "What was there before habit?" |

Remakes also feed back into the original. ABT ballerinas copied Alonso's seamstress gesture (§3). Rojo said ENB danced the traditional *Giselle* "better than ever" after the Khan version (§8). The Paris Opéra took in both Alonso's (1972) and Ek's (1993) *Giselle*. Remaking is part of how the canon survives.

**12.6 Credit and authorship.** The credit line is itself a design decision that can be read:
- collective under Mao ("China Ballet Troupe");
- "after Coralli and Perrot" (Alonso, Franklin);
- "Choreography by Petipa; staging and additional choreography by Ratmansky";
- the author's name in the title ("Matthew Bourne's *Swan Lake*", "Akram Khan's *Giselle*").

The name that drops out is usually the source-maker: Liang Xin and Xie Jin, the puppeteers of 1961, the Yan'an authors, Dolin and Skeaping, Stepanov's notators. Teach learners to write the full credit chain of their own concept.

**12.7 Media chains (adjacent translation).** Several concepts travel *into* ballet from other media: film into ballet (§§1, 11), puppet theatre into ballet (§10). Almost all travel *out* again to film or television: the 1970 model-work film, Alonso's 1963 ICAIC film, the 1987 NBC *Creole Giselle*, SVT's Ek, Bourne's cinema releases and *Billy Elliot*, ENB's cinema *Giselle*, the 2020 streams. Since the 1960s the filmed version often becomes "the" version (§1, §3).
