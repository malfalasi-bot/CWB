# W4. Theories of the concept and the creative process in ballet

Scope: how ballet's makers and scholars have theorised what a ballet is, how it should be conceived, and how a team turns an idea into a finished world. Read as a sequence of theories of "the concept", then the scholarship on collaboration, authorship and borrowing, then the working documents, then how other design and world-building teaching uses performance, then a mapping to the program's Concept DNA.

## Part 1. Theories of what a ballet should be, in sequence

**Overview [argued].** Read as a sequence, ballet's theories of the concept swing between two poles. One pole says the concept is a *subject* (a theme, fable or story) to which every element is subordinated: court-ballet "unity of design", Noverre's *ballet d'action*, the Romantic livret, Fokine, *drambalet*. The other pole says the concept is a *structure* or *system*, and the subject either disappears or is generated by the system: Gautier's half-serious "the dance itself", Balanchine's music-first plotless ballet, Cunningham and Cage's independent elements, Forsythe's choreographic objects, McGregor's thinking tools. Between them sits the question of *who* holds the concept: the inventor-courtier, the ballet master, the librettist, the impresario's committee, the composer, the choreographer as sole author, the state, or a research team. Each theory below answers both questions: what the concept is, and who owns it.

### 1.1 Court ballet: allegory, emblem, the "invention" (Beaujoyeulx 1582; Saint-Hubert 1641; Ménestrier 1682)

**The documents.**
- Balthasar de Beaujoyeulx, *Balet comique de la Royne* (Paris: Le Roy, Ballard & Patisson, 1582), the published livret-plus-account of the 15 October 1581 performance in the Louvre for the Joyeuse–Vaudemont wedding. Open copies: Gallica (BnF ark bpt6k1110737, cited in two scholarly articles seen) and Internet Archive, Getty Research Institute copy (archive.org/details/baletcomiqvedela00beau) **[documented]**. The Getty record credits Beaujoyeulx as having "conceived, directed and choreographed" it, with music by Lambert de Beaulieu and Jacques Salmon, verses by La Chesnaye, décor and plates by Jacques Patin **[documented, catalogue note]**. Note the date discrepancy: performance 1581, publication 1582.
- François de Lauze's *Apologie de la danse* (1623) and **Monsieur de Saint-Hubert, *La Manière de composer et faire réussir les ballets* (1641)**, the earliest known how-to manual for conceiving a ballet **[probable]**.
- Claude-François Ménestrier SJ, *Des ballets anciens et modernes selon les règles du théâtre* (Paris: René Guignard, 1682). Open: Library of Congress full PDF (loc.gov/item/10018134), with the note that the 1679 privilege is for Ménestrier's *Philosophie des images*, "of which this forms a part" **[documented]**. Also Acting Archives PDF and Google Books (Bayerische Staatsbibliothek) **[documented, catalogue]**. Facsimile Minkoff 1972. Ménestrier's earlier *Remarques pour la conduite des ballets* (1658, preface to his Lyon ballet *L'Autel de Lyon*) and *Des représentations en musique anciennes et modernes* (1681) belong to the same project **[documented, catalogue]**.

**The theory.**
1. *Union of the arts as an antique revival.* Beaujoyeulx's preface proposes to merge ballet and comedy (hence the title) and to amalgamate music and poetry; this follows the programme of Baïf's Académie de Poésie et de Musique (1570/71), which wanted to recover the union of the arts attributed to antiquity **[probable: Couto 2022 reads the preface this way; Encyclopedia.com/International Dictionary of Ballet links Beaujoyeulx to Baïf]**. A dedicatory poem by Billard calls him "Geometre, inventif": the maker is praised as an *inventor* of figures, not a storyteller **[probable]**.
2. *The parts of a ballet.* Saint-Hubert lists what is necessary: the Subject, the Melodies, the Dance, the Costumes, the Machines and the order **[probable, quoted by Anna Leon, *Expanded Choreographies*, 2022, ch. 1]**. The subject determines how many *entrées* a ballet has; he gives standard numbers (thirty for a grand royal ballet, twenty for a lesser one) **[probable]**. Steps are "subjected" to music and entrées, so the dance is not the master element **[probable]**. Novelty was a rule: to be beautiful, a subject must never have been done before (Saint-Hubert, as translated by Clara Rodrigues Couto 2020/2022) **[probable]**. Anna Leon shows that Saint-Hubert's author figure is not the dancing master (one master was needed for every three or four entrées, so several worked on one ballet) but a *maître d'ordre*, a "master of order" holding artistic and practical responsibility, whom she calls an "expanded choreographer" **[documented, Leon 2022 ch. 1]**.
3. *Unity of design, not unity of action.* Ménestrier, applying the "rules of the theatre", argues that ballet does not need the Aristotelian unities of action, time and place that tragedy needs, only a **unity of *dessein*** so that the different entrées relate to one Subject **[documented as quoted in Leon 2022 and Couto 2022, two independent studies; Ménestrier 1682 pp. 54 and 114 of the 1972 facsimile]**. His examples: a ballet on the world divided into sky and earth; a ballet on time divided into seasons **[probable]**. This is a *taxonomic* or *collection* model of the concept: a theme is divided into parts, and each entrée is one instance of it.
4. *Imitation through movement.* Ménestrier defines ballet as an imitation like the other arts, but one that expresses movement, which painting and sculpture cannot, and thereby the "nature of things" and the passions of the soul **[documented in Perugini's 1915 English paraphrase on Project Gutenberg; probable for the exact French wording]**.
5. *Allegory as living emblem.* Ménestrier was also the leading theorist of emblems, devices and heraldry (*La Philosophie des images*, 1682), and the ballet treatise was printed as part of that larger project **[documented, LoC note]**. Scholars read court-ballet figures as "symbolic figures, instantaneous signs" for spectators to decode, which depended on costume, mask, décor and text rather than on expressive dancing **[documented, Leon 2022 ch. 1; see also McGowan 1963 and Franko]**. The scenic apparatus makes "allegories as living emblems" **[documented, Couto 2022]**.

**Who holds the concept.** The *inventeur* or *ordonnateur* (often a courtier, poet or Jesuit), with the patron; the livret was printed and distributed so the audience could decode the emblems. The concept is political: the *Balet comique* ends with gifts of emblematic medals among the audience **[documented, Getty catalogue note]**.

**Scholarship.** Margaret M. McGowan, *L'Art du ballet de cour en France 1581–1643* (CNRS, 1963) and *Le Balet comique de Balthazar de Beaujoyeulx, 1581* (facsimile ed., 1982); Mark Franko, *Dance as Text: Ideologies of the Baroque Body* (Cambridge UP, 1993; rev. Oxford 2015); Clara Rodrigues Couto, "Ballet in writing: precepts and rules of composition for court ballets in France under the Ancien Régime (1581–1682)", *Revista Brasileira de Estudos da Presença* 12.1 (2022), **open access** (journals.openedition.org/rbep/770); Couto also published a commented translation of Saint-Hubert (2020); Anna Leon, *Expanded Choreographies – Choreographic Histories: Trans-Historical Perspectives Beyond Dance and Human Bodies in Motion* (transcript, TanzScripte 63, 2022), **open access** on OAPEN and inlibra (doi 10.14361/9783839461051), ch. 1 "Monsieur de Saint-Hubert's expanded choreographic poietics" **[documented]**. Leon's conclusion reads Saint-Hubert, Picabia's *Relâche* and others as "choreography as assemblage" **[documented]**. Marcel Paquot (1957) proposed reading court-ballet rules through the librettos rather than the theorists **[probable, via Couto]**. Franko's line that dance theory "does not come 'after' choreography; it is constitutive of choreography" (2005) is quoted in Couto 2022 **[documented as quoted]**.

### 1.2 Noverre's *Lettres sur la danse* (1760) and the *ballet d'action*

**The documents.** Jean-Georges Noverre, *Lettres sur la danse, et sur les ballets* (Stuttgart and Lyon: Aimé Delaroche, 1760). Open: Library of Congress (loc.gov/item/08017354, with IIIF manifest) and Internet Archive (York University copy, archive.org/details/lettressurladans00noveuoft) **[documented]**. The enlarged St Petersburg edition *Lettres sur la danse, sur les ballets et les arts* (1803–04, 4 vols), vol. 3 subtitled *Observations sur la construction d'une salle d'opéra et programmes de ballets* **[documented, archive.org]**. English: Cyril W. Beaumont's translation (1930; Dance Horizons reprints, 1966/1975; archive.org lending copy, access-restricted) **[documented]**. LoC also holds Noverre's *Programme des grands ballets historiques, héroïques, nationaux, moraux et allégoriques* with costume designs by Louis-René Boquet (1791) **[documented, LoC "more like this"]**, a rare surviving set of a choreographer's own concept documents.

**The theory.**
1. *Ballet as drama in movement.* Ballet must represent characters and express their passions; dance without dramatic or expressive content is meaningless **[probable, Britannica; standard reading]**.
2. *Plot discipline.* A logical, intelligible plot integrated with movement; superfluous solos and irrelevant virtuosity removed; music suited to the dramatic development; costumes, décor and lighting compatible with the introduction, plot and climax of each act **[probable, summary of Noverre's points in standard references]**. This is the earliest well-known statement that *every department of a production answers to one dramatic concept*.
3. *Against masks and conventions.* The ninth letter attacks masks; destroy them and "we shall gain a soul" **[probable, Beaumont translation as quoted]**. Costume should be lightened to show the body and suit the character. These are explicit **anti-DNA** clauses: what the new ballet refuses.
4. *The ballet master's education.* Noverre asks the ballet master to know painting, anatomy, music, geometry, history and poetry, i.e. to be a polymath director **[probable, widely paraphrased; exact wording not verified in this pass]**.
5. *The programme.* Noverre published programmes (printed scenarios) of his ballets. Edward Nye argues that in the 1760s the word *chorégraphie* shifted from meaning Feuillet notation to meaning the dramaturgical structure of the work, possibly the written plot summary itself; so ballet programmes "are not peripheral to performance" but carry what contemporaries understood as choreographic information **[documented, Nye abstract, "'Choreography' Is Narrative: The Programmes of the Eighteenth-Century *Ballet d'Action*", *Dance Research* 26.1 (2008), JSTOR 40263986]**. This is the strongest scholarly support for treating the written scenario as the concept document of a ballet.

**Contested.** Noverre was not alone and perhaps not earliest: Gasparo Angiolini and Franz Hilverding in Vienna developed danced pantomime at the same time, and Angiolini publicly disputed Noverre's priority (the "Noverre–Angiolini quarrel", 1773–75) **[probable]**. Use "earliest well-known theorist", not "inventor". Lucian's *On the Dance* (2nd century CE) is the antique authority the reformers invoked **[probable, Vallejos 2013 abstract]**.

**Scholarship.** Judith Chazin-Bennahum, "Jean-Georges Noverre: dance and reform", *Cambridge Companion to Ballet* (2007) ch. 8; Edward Nye, *Mime, Music and Drama on the Eighteenth-Century Stage: The Ballet d'Action* (Cambridge UP, 2011); Nye 2008 (above); Ingrid Brainard and Kathleen Kuzmick Hansell on Angiolini; Arianne Fabbricatore et al. on Noverre's programmes **[probable]**.

### 1.3 Blasis: the codifier's theory of composition

**The documents.** Carlo Blasis, *Traité élémentaire, théorique et pratique de l'art de la danse* (Milan: Beati & Tenenti, 1820), digitised by NYPL Digital Collections (Jerome Robbins Dance Division) **[documented]**; *The Code of Terpsichore* (London: Bulcock, 1828; trans. R. Barton "under the author's immediate inspection"; French *Manuel complet de la danse*, 1830) **[documented, NYPL catalogue note]**; full text on Internet Archive (codeterpsichore00blasgoog, Google scan, public domain) **[documented]**; *Notes upon Dancing* (London, 1847), archive.org PDF **[documented]**.

**The theory.** Blasis is remembered for codifying technique, but the *Code* is also a composition manual: it has sections on the composition of ballets and pantomime, and its preface aims to show that ballets "should not be mere divertissements or dancing spectacles" but drama **[documented, archive.org OCR of preface]**. He continues Noverre (via his teacher Dauberval and Viganò's *coreodramma* at La Scala) **[probable]**. His second contribution is the idea that classical vocabulary is derived from an ideal geometry and from antique sculpture: he is credited with modelling the *attitude* on Giambologna's *Mercury* **[probable, Britannica]**. Gabriele Brandstetter reads the *Code* as "mechanics as the matrix of grace" **[documented title, *Topoi* 24 (2005), doi 10.1007/s11245-004-4162-x]**.

**Why it matters for a concept course [argued].** Blasis separates two layers the course needs to name: the *grammar* (a shared, codified vocabulary that any ballet can use) and the *concept* (what this ballet does with it). Fokine would later attack exactly this layer of "ready-made" steps.

### 1.4 Gautier as critic-librettist; the Romantic livret as a form

**Gautier's account of conception.** In an 1841 open letter to Heinrich Heine in *La Presse*, Théophile Gautier tells how he read Heine's passage on the Wilis in *De l'Allemagne*, exclaimed that it would make a pretty ballet, then the same evening met Jules-Henri Vernoy de Saint-Georges backstage at the Opéra; "three days later the ballet of Giselle was written and accepted" **[documented as quoted by Marian Smith and Doug Fullington, Pacific Northwest Ballet essay; Smith and Fullington note Gautier overstates the speed: about two months]**. Victor Hugo's "Fantômes" (*Les Orientales*) is the second literary source **[probable]**. Britannica adds the production logic: the Opéra wanted a vehicle for Carlotta Grisi, so the concept met a *casting obligation* **[probable]**.

**The critic as theorist.** Gautier's reviews (1837–48, trans. Beaumont, *The Romantic Ballet as Seen by Théophile Gautier*, 1932/1947) contain his double theory: ballet as visual, sensual spectacle, and the much-quoted line that "the real, unique and eternal theme for a ballet is the dance itself" **[documented as quoted in Smith 2000, JSTOR preview]**. The Paris Opéra programme also uses the line "Ballets are the dreams of poets taken seriously" attributed to Gautier **[documented as used; original source not verified]**.

**The livret as a form.** Marian Smith, *Ballet and Opera in the Age of "Giselle"* (Princeton UP, 2000) is the key study. Her argument: in the 1830s–40s the Paris *ballet-pantomime* and opera shared structure, librettists, staging conventions and a musical language of encoded gesture; the "divorce" came gradually **[documented, publisher description]**. The livret was written by a professional dramatist (Scribe, Saint-Georges), printed and sold, laid out the plot scene by scene, with characters, locale and staging; composers wrote "air parlant" music that quoted known songs so the audience could read the mime **[probable, Smith; Fullington review]**. Smith's appendix gives the *Giselle* libretto in French with her translation, and a list of all ballet-pantomimes and operas at the Opéra 1825–50 **[documented, Fullington review]**. Smith also shows the livret was routinely co-signed: the 1841 *Giselle* livret credits Saint-Georges, Gautier and the choreographer Coralli **[documented, Actes Sud 1991 edition title in NYPL catalogue]**.

**Why it matters [argued].** The Romantic livret is the closest historical analogue to the program's *premise + perimeter + rules* sheet: a short printed document that fixes setting, characters, world-rules (the Wilis dance men to death between midnight and dawn), the two-world structure (daylight village vs. moonlit forest), and the obligatory star vehicle. Smith's point that the "silent language" was legible because it borrowed opera's conventions is a lesson in *adjacent translation*.

### 1.5 Wagner's *Gesamtkunstwerk* and its uptake in ballet

**Wagner.** In *Das Kunstwerk der Zukunft* (Leipzig: Wigand, 1850 [1849]), Wagner names the three "purely human" arts as Dance (*Tanzkunst*), Tone and Poetry, once united in Greek drama, now split; the artwork of the future reunites them, with architecture, sculpture and painting serving the whole **[documented, chapter list in de.wikipedia; first edition on archive.org (Public Domain Mark) and Deutsches Textarchiv]**. Wagner saw this as a collective "fellowship of all artists" but in practice subordinated the sister arts to drama under one author **[probable]**. Adolphe Appia later criticised Bayreuth's realisation **[probable]**, and Appia's own theory of rhythmic space fed Dalcroze and modern dance.

**Uptake.** The *Mir iskusstva* circle (Diaghilev, Benois, Bakst) were Wagnerians, and the Ballets Russes is routinely described as realising the *Gesamtkunstwerk* through ballet **[probable, Routledge Encyclopedia of Modernism abstract]**. The sharper scholarly reading: unlike Symbolist, Secession or Bauhaus versions, Diaghilev put real dancing bodies at the centre **[probable, Lupishko on Bellow 2013]**. Marina Lupishko, "A Post-Wagnerian Gesamtkunstwerk? Stravinsky, Diaghilev, Benois, and the Russian Painters of the Ballets Russes" (Olms, 2022) tests the claim **[documented title]**. **Contested:** Stravinsky and later Balanchine rejected Wagner (Stravinsky's *Poetics* attacks music drama), and Fokine's fifth principle demands *equality* of the arts rather than Wagner's subordination to drama, so "Gesamtkunstwerk" is better read as an aspiration to integration than as a doctrine the company held **[argued]**.

### 1.6 Fokine's five principles (1914)

**The document.** "The New Russian Ballet: Conventions in Dancing: M. Fokine's Principles and Aims", letter to *The Times* (London), 6 July 1914 **[documented, NYPL finding aid for Fokine papers, which holds a copy]**. Fokine had sent a reform memo to the Imperial Theatres directorate in 1904 (on a *Daphnis and Chloë* scenario) that anticipates the principles **[probable]**. Text reproduced on American Ballet Theatre's Fokine page **[documented]**.

**The five principles (paraphrased; short quotes only).**
1. Create "in each case a new form of movement" matching the subject, period and character of the music, not combinations of "ready-made and established steps".
2. Dance and mime have no meaning unless they express dramatic action.
3. Conventional gesture only where the ballet's style requires it; otherwise the whole body expresses.
4. The group is not ornament: expressiveness extends from individual to ensemble and crowd.
5. The alliance of dance with the other arts "only on the condition of complete equality": the ballet is neither slave of music nor of décor.

**Reading [argued].** Principle 1 is the most important for a world-building course: *each concept generates its own movement vocabulary*. That is the program's "grammars" field stated as a rule. Principle 5 is a theory of the team: equal partners, not a hierarchy. Principles 2–3 are anti-DNA statements against the Petipa-era conventions. The Boston Globe critic's remark that Fokine's credo is "the antithesis of the Balanchinian delight in manipulating" established steps marks the next swing **[probable]**.

### 1.7 Benois and Diaghilev: the "committee" and the impresario as curator

**The model.** The Ballets Russes grew from the *Mir iskusstva* (World of Art) circle, which Diaghilev, Benois and Bakst co-founded as a journal in 1898 **[documented, LoC timeline]**. Its earliest productions were conceived in conversation among non-dancers: painters (Benois, Bakst, Roerich), a music critic (Walter Nouvel, later company secretary), balletomane critics (Valerian Svetlov, General Bezobrazov), the choreographer (Fokine) and the régisseur (Serge Grigoriev, engaged 1909 as business manager and rehearsal director) **[probable: names from LoC timeline; the informal "committee" meeting in Diaghilev's St Petersburg flat in 1909–10 is described in Grigoriev, *The Diaghilev Ballet 1909–1929* (1953), Benois's *Reminiscences of the Russian Ballet* (trans. Mary Britnieva, Putnam 1941/1947) and Fokine's memoirs; this pass did not see the passages at source]**. Credit lines record the collective: *Schéhérazade* (1910) libretto "by Léon Bakst, Alexandre Benois, and Michel Fokine" after the first tale of the *Thousand and One Nights*; *Petrushka* (1911) libretto by Stravinsky and Benois **[documented, LoC online exhibition captions]**. Benois later publicly protested that *Schéhérazade*'s programme credited the libretto to Bakst alone, an early authorship dispute inside the "committee" **[probable; see `../scope/05`]**.

**Two theories inside the model.**
- **Benois's** (the designer-scenarist as originator): he presents the "new ballet" as the work of a circle of artists and connoisseurs, with the painter-scenarist conceiving the whole and the choreographer realising it **[probable]**. Arlene Croce calls Benois "the coauthor of *Petrushka*" and notes Scheijen's view of him as "the group's moral conscience" **[documented, NYRB 2011]**.
- **Diaghilev's** (the impresario as curator and editor): he chose subjects, paired composer, painter and choreographer, commissioned, cut and reassigned (he asked Bakst to redesign three of Golovin's *Firebird* costumes **[documented, LoC timeline]**), and assembled each season as a mixed bill. Scholars stress that his own predilections placed "a higher value on the music, the scenery, and the story than on the choreography" (Croce reading Scheijen) **[documented]**. After 1917 he moved from Russian *Mir iskusstva* artists to the Paris avant-garde (Picasso, Matisse, Derain, Braque, Gris, Cocteau, Satie, Poulenc, Prokofiev) **[documented, Polish Music Library entry; LoC]**: the curator changes the collection, not just the works.

**Scholarly frame.** Lynn Garafola, *Diaghilev's Ballets Russes* (Oxford UP, 1989; archive.org lending copy) is the key study of the company as an enterprise that moved ballet "from state patronage into the world of commercial sponsors" (NYT on Scheijen) and of the collaborative model **[documented title; probable reading]**. Sjeng Scheijen, *Diaghilev: A Life* (Profile/OUP, 2009/2010) uses Russian archives on the early years **[documented, reviews]**. Garafola and Scheijen both read the repertoire in terms of "progressive" (*Parade*, *Les Noces*, *Le Pas d'acier*) versus "conservative" (*Schéhérazade*, *Firebird*, eighteenth-century pastiche) works; Croce contests that politicised split **[documented, NYRB]**. **Contested:** the Mamontov circle at Abramtsevo and Mamontov's Private Opera (1880s–90s) had already fused painters, designers and performers; Garafola treats it as a precursor whose results lacked cohesion **[probable, via Dickinson course blog citing Garafola p. 151]**.

**Reading [argued].** For the course, Diaghilev is the clearest historical figure of the *concept holder who makes nothing himself*: he holds the perimeter (Russian exoticism for Paris, then modernism), the collection map (the season, the mixed bill), the casting and commissioning, and the money. Benois represents the *originator-scenarist*. Fokine represents the *realiser who wants equal status*. The tension among them is the authorship problem of Part 2.

### 1.8 Stravinsky and Balanchine: music first, plotless ballet

**Statements (short quotes; sources vary in reliability).**
- Balanchine on *Apollon musagète* (1928): it was "the turning point" of his life; the score "seemed to tell me that I could dare not to use everything, that I, too, could eliminate"; he learned that gestures, like tones and shades, "have certain family relations" **[documented as quoted by the BSO programme note and the Balanchine Trust page; the source is Balanchine's "The Dance Element in Strawinsky's Music", *Dance Index* 6 (1947)]**. This is a theory of **anti-DNA as method**: making by elimination.
- "See the music, hear the dance"; "You cannot dance a story"; "A man and a woman on stage is already a story"; "There are no mothers-in-law in ballet" **[probable: widely quoted, collected in *By George Balanchine* (San Marco, 1984); exact first sources not verified]**.
- Balanchine on Stravinsky: music is time, and "I am not a creator of time myself" **[probable, quoted by Taper via Encyclopedia.com]**.
- Stravinsky: choreography "must realize its own form, one independent of the musical form", not duplicating "the line and beat of the music" **[probable, quoted by Chicago Symphony essay; source likely Stravinsky–Craft conversation books]**.
- Stravinsky's *Autobiography* (*Chroniques de ma vie*, 1935–36) contains the famous claim that music is by its nature powerless to express anything at all **[probable; widely quoted]**; his *Poetics of Music* (Harvard Norton Lectures 1939–40, published 1942) attacks Wagnerian music drama and praises order and limitation **[probable]**.
- Balanchine's own theoretical writings: "Notes on Choreography", *Dance Index* 4:2–3 (Feb–Mar 1945); "Marginal Notes on the Dance", in Walter Sorell (ed.), *The Dance Has Many Faces* (1951; rev. 1966) **[documented, Balanchine Foundation bibliography]**.

**The theory [argued from the above].** The concept is located in the *score*. The choreographer reads the music as structure and makes visible its time; décor and costume are reduced (practice clothes in *The Four Temperaments*, *Agon*), story is reduced to relation. This is a theory where the **grammar** (the classical vocabulary, which Balanchine treated as a liberation, not a constraint) and the **anchor** (the score) carry the concept, and the narrative premise is deliberately left out. It inverts Fokine's first principle: not a new vocabulary per subject, but new arrangements of an inherited vocabulary per score.

**Scholarship.** Stephanie Jordan, *Stravinsky Dances: Re-Visions across a Century* (Dance Books, 2007); Charles M. Joseph, *Stravinsky and Balanchine: A Journey of Invention* (Yale UP, 2002); Paul Hodgins, *Relationships between Score and Choreography in Twentieth-Century Dance* (1992); Baird Hastings, *Choreographer and Composer* (1983) **[documented, Balanchine Foundation bibliography; Joseph probable]**.

### 1.9 Cunningham and Cage: independence of elements as the counter-model

**The theory.** Cage's essay "Goal: New Music, New Dance" (1939) argued that dance should be freed from dependence on musical structure **[probable, n+1 2013]**. Cunningham and Cage then proposed that music and dance "occur in the same time and space yet be created independently" **[documented, NYPL finding aid for the Merce Cunningham Dance Foundation records]**. The decisive early structure was a shared *time* frame: dance and music met at the boundaries of time units and were independent in between; Cunningham told Calvin Tomkins (1962) that "the thing everybody missed" was that the structure "was based on time" **[documented as quoted, Walker Art Center essay, citing Kostelanetz ed., *Merce Cunningham: Dancing in Space and Time*, 1992, p. 139]**. Décor (Rauschenberg, Johns, Warhol, Stella) was commissioned independently too; the Trust describes the method as each element "created independently and brought together for the premiere performance" **[documented, Merce Cunningham Trust]**. Chance operations (coin tosses, dice, playing cards in *Canfield*, 1969; I Ching) decided sequence, space and timing **[documented as described by the Trust and standard references]**. Cunningham's essay "Space, Time and Dance" (*trans/formation* 1, 1952) is the core text **[probable]**. Equal value of all stage positions, no single front **[documented, Trust]**.

**Why it is the counter-model [argued].** Every theory from Ménestrier to Fokine assumed the elements must be *unified* by a concept (theme, drama, music). Cunningham and Cage keep only a container (time span, stage) and a rule set (chance procedures) and let elements coexist without integration. The Walker essay puts it as "precarious proximity that nevertheless refuses to ever integrate" **[documented]**. For a world-building course this is the *rules-and-perimeter without premise* model: the world is defined by its operating rules, not by a story. It also shows a different *handover*: the Dance Capsules (below, Part 3).

### 1.10 Soviet *drambalet* and socialist realism: a state theory of the concept

**Context.** Socialist realism was declared the official method of Soviet literature and art from 1932–34 **[documented, Britannica]**. In ballet it produced *drambalet* (dramatic ballet, also "choreodrama"), c. 1932–1956.

**The theory (as practised and written up).**
1. *A literary source of canonical status* (Pushkin, Shakespeare, Balzac, Lope de Vega, Hugo) adapted into a full dramatic scenario by a professional dramatist. *The Fountain of Bakhchisarai* (Kirov, 28 Sept 1934): Zakharov choreography, Asafiev music, Nikolai Volkov libretto after Pushkin's 1823 poem **[documented, standard reference]**. *Romeo and Juliet*: subject proposed to Prokofiev in 1934 by the dramaturge Adrian Piotrovsky, then reworked by Piotrovsky and Sergei Radlov **[probable]**.
2. *Stanislavsky method in the studio*: Zakharov spent months analysing Pushkin's poem with the dancers to build motivated characters **[probable, Lebere 2019 thesis citing secondary sources]**; he is described as "at the origins" of ballet drama, combining dance imagery with acting **[probable]**.
3. *Realism, legibility, historical specificity, and the positive hero*; mime and dance must be motivated by the drama; "formalism" (abstraction, experiment, Western modernism) is condemned.
4. *State correction of the concept.* The *Pravda* editorial "Balletic Falsehood" (6 February 1936) condemned Shostakovich and Lopukhov's *The Limpid Stream* (libretto Piotrovsky and Lopukhov) for unreal folk-kitsch collective-farm life; the work was withdrawn and Piotrovsky was arrested in 1937 and shot **[documented as reported, Wikipedia entries for Piotrovsky and *The Limpid Stream*; a student thesis dates the article 1935 and calls it "Ballet Fraud" — date discrepancy noted, 1936 is the standard date]**.
5. Codified afterwards in Zakharov's *Iskusstvo baletmeistera* (*The Art of the Choreographer*, 1954) and his later *Composition of a Dance* (1983) used at GITIS **[probable]**.

**The counter-current.** Fyodor Lopukhov's *Velichie mirozdaniya* (*The Magnificence of the Universe*, 1923, a "dance symphony" to Beethoven 4) and his theory of "symphonism" (*Paths of a Ballet Master*, 1925) offered a plotless, music-structured alternative inside Soviet ballet; Tim Scholl's chapter "Achieving Symphonism (The Soviet Ballet in Theory)" treats the debate **[probable; chapter title documented, *Sleeping Beauty, a Legend in Progress*, Yale UP 2004]**. Balanchine danced in the 1923 Lopukhov piece **[probable]**.

**Scholarship.** Christina Ezrahi, *Swans of the Kremlin: Ballet and Power in Soviet Russia* (Pittsburgh UP, 2012), argues that producers and performers "quietly but effectively resisted" Soviet cultural hegemony **[documented, publisher blurb]**; Järvinen reviewed it in *Dance Research Journal* 46.3 (2014) **[documented]**; Tim Scholl, *From Petipa to Balanchine* (Routledge, 1994) and *Sleeping Beauty, a Legend in Progress* (2004) **[documented]**; Elizabeth Souritz, *Soviet Choreographers in the 1920s* (Duke UP, 1990) **[probable]**; Janice Ross, *Like a Bomb Going Off: Leonid Yakobson and Ballet as Resistance in Soviet Russia* (Yale UP, 2015) **[probable]**.

**Reading [argued].** *Drambalet* is the clearest case of an **external authority writing the obligations and anti-DNA**: the state defined which sources were legitimate, which styles were forbidden ("formalism"), what the audience must understand, and enforced it after premiere. It is also, technically, a sophisticated *adjacent translation* practice (great literature into danced drama) whose method (dramaturge + composer + choreographer + Stanislavskian rehearsal) produced works still in repertory.

### 1.11 Forsythe's "Choreographic Objects"; McGregor, deLahunta and choreographic thinking tools

**Forsythe.** William Forsythe's short essay "Choreographic Objects" (c. 2008; on williamforsythe.com and in Neri and Respini (eds), *William Forsythe: Choreographic Objects*, Prestel 2018) asks whether choreography can generate "autonomous, accessible expressions of its principles" without the body **[documented, essay page]**. The term is offered "as a categorizing tool" to identify sites for understanding "potential organization and instigation of action-based knowledge" **[documented]**. A choreographic object "is not a substitute for the body, but rather an alternative site" **[probable, quoted on a dance blog and paraphrased by the Synchronous Objects team]**. Projects that embody the theory:
- *Improvisation Technologies* (CD-ROM, ZKM 1994/1999): Forsythe demonstrating the geometric operations he and his dancers used to generate movement **[probable; cited PMC 2014]**.
- *Synchronous Objects for One Flat Thing, reproduced* (Ohio State ACCAD and The Forsythe Company, launched 1 April 2009; creative directors Forsythe, Maria Palazzi, Norah Zuniga Shaw): the dance (2000) translated into data and then into visualisations; Forsythe: "choreography is an organizational practice that employs fundamental creative strategies relevant to many other domains" **[documented, OSU media release]**. The project asks "what else might physical thinking look like?" **[documented, archive site]**. It was the first phase of **Motion Bank** (2010–13 and after) **[documented]**.
- Installations (*The Fact of Matter*, *Nowhere and Everywhere at the Same Time*, *City of Abstracts*), exhibited 2018 ICA Boston onward, 2026 Voorlinden **[probable]**.

**McGregor and the cognitive scientists.** Studio Wayne McGregor's research programme (facilitated by Scott deLahunta as Research Director) has run since 2002–03 **[documented, Studio R+D page]**:
- *Choreography and Cognition* (2003–04): five scientists in residence with Random Dance; studies of movement control, phrase segmentation and the "cognitive dimension of notations" **[documented, deLahunta CLA page]**; reported in McCarthy, Blackwell, deLahunta et al., "Bodies Meet Minds: Choreography and Cognition", *Leonardo* 39.5 (2006) **[documented, OpenAlex]**.
- *Distributed choreographic cognition* with David Kirsh (UC San Diego), c. 2009, on how thought is distributed between choreographer and dancers **[documented]**.
- *Choreographic Language Agent* (2004–08 onward) → *Becoming* (with OpenEndedGroup's Marc Downie and Nick Rothwell, AHRC-funded), used in *Atomos* (2013) as an AI "eleventh dancer" **[documented]**; *AISOMA* (2018) **[documented]**.
- **Choreographic Thinking Tools** (Philip Barnard, MRC Cognition and Brain Sciences Unit, Cambridge): a decade of research yielding tasks that use "embodied mental imagery": working with stimuli, noticing and selecting features, translating them into movement; first used in *FAR* (2010) **[documented, Studio page]**. Published as deLahunta, Clarke and Barnard, "A conversation about choreographic thinking tools", *Journal of Dance & Somatic Practices* 3.1–2 (2012) 243–59 **[documented]**; Barnard and deLahunta, "Mapping the audit traces of interdisciplinary collaboration", *Interdisciplinary Science Reviews* (2017), **open access on PMC** (PMC5743006), which describes the PACT (Process and Concept Tracking) method **[documented]**; May, Calvo-Merino, deLahunta et al., "Points in Mental Space", *Dance Research* 29 (2011) **[documented, OpenAlex]**.
- **Mind and Movement** (2013; McGregor, Barnard, deLahunta, Jasmine Wilson, Ellie Douglas-Allan; Paul Hamlyn Foundation): a boxed teaching resource with twelve principles, five lesson plans, "Discover" reflection cards and "What Next?" development cards for GCSE/BTEC/A-level **[documented, Studio page]**. *Thinking with the Body*, Wellcome Collection exhibition, 2013 **[documented]**.
- deLahunta and Zuniga Shaw's "choreographic resources" concept: "Constructing Memories: Creation of the choreographic resource" (*Performance Research* 2006) and "Choreographic Resources: Agents, Archives, Scores and Installations" (*Performance Research* 13.1, 2008) **[documented titles]**.

**Reading [argued].** Forsythe and McGregor treat the concept not as a subject but as a *transferable organisational system* that can be externalised, visualised, taught and recombined. That is the program's **grammars** field and **handover** field made explicit; *Mind and Movement* is the nearest existing thing to a ballet-derived teaching kit for concept development. Forsythe's claim that choreographic strategies are "relevant to many other domains" is the warrant for using ballet in a world-building course at all.

### 1.12 Siobhan Davies RePlay: the archive as a record of process

**What it is.** *Siobhan Davies RePlay*, a "born digital" archive of British choreographer Siobhan Davies's work, led by Sarah Whatley (Coventry University) with Siobhan Davies Dance; AHRC-funded (Coventry lists £521,258.75); idea 2005, work from 2007, launched June 2009 after 30 months **[documented, Coventry project page and Pure record]**. It held "thousands" of searchable records (moving image, still image, audio, text) and "a comprehensive collection of materials that document the creative dance making process": notebooks, sketches, scores, rehearsal footage, many lent from collaborators' personal collections and seen publicly for the first time **[documented, Coventry; Digital Meets Culture]**. Users could compile **"kinetic scrapbooks"**, annotate video and search by colour or posture **[documented, Whatley 2013; Surrey NRCD Digital Dance Archives project]**. The team's work on IP, copyright and licensing for "collectively 'authored'" material is cited as a model **[documented, Coventry impact statement]**. Follow-on: *The Library of Processes* (Coventry with Siobhan Davies Dance) **[documented, Whatley 2013]**.

**Status (important for the course).** Hosted by Coventry 2009–2021; Siobhan Davies Studios now says it aims "to have a version of RePlay back online in a new format in the future" **[documented, siobhandavies.com, 2022]**. So the archive is **offline**; the original URL should not be given to learners as live. Whatley notes that Davies and a former dancer took part in deciding what rehearsal footage to keep: the record of process is itself curated by the artist **[probable, via Brazilian 2024 article citing Whatley, *Dance Research* 31.2 (2013)]**.

**Scholarship.** Whatley, "Siobhan Davies RePlay: (Re)visiting the digital archive", *International Journal of Performance Arts and Digital Media* 9.1 (2013) 83–98, **open pre-print** on Coventry's CURVE repository; Whatley, "Recovering and reanimating 'lost' traces: the digital archiving of the rehearsal process in Siobhan Davies RePlay", *Dance Research* 31.2 (2013) 144–56; Whatley, "Archiving the Dance: Making Siobhan Davies Replay", in Maaike Bleeker (ed.), *Transmission in Motion* (Routledge, 2016) **[documented]**.

**Reading [argued].** RePlay is the clearest example of a choreographer treating the *process* as part of the work's public life (a **lifecycle** and **handover** decision), and of a team confronting **rights and money** for collectively made material. Its disappearance after 12 years is itself a lifecycle lesson: digital process archives need an owner and a budget after the grant ends.

**Synthesis [argued].** Three findings recur. (1) *The originator is often not the choreographer*: Gautier (*Giselle*), Vsevolozhsky (*Sleeping Beauty*), Benois (*Petrushka*), Roerich (*Rite*), Piotrovsky (*Romeo and Juliet*), Diaghilev (repertory). (2) *Makers rewrite their own origin stories*, usually towards autonomy and genius (Stravinsky's denial of folk sources; Gautier's "three days"). (3) *Credit lines drop people*: scene painters, costumiers, répétiteurs, notators, dancers whose bodies generated material (now argued for McGregor and Forsythe companies, whose dancers are co-authors of movement). See `../scope/05_economics_labour_authorship.md` for labour and money.

**The appropriation debate [contested, both readings].**
- *Reading A (generative):* Phil Chan argues Orientalism was historically "the greatest driver of creativity and innovation in the western performing arts" (large corps numbers, new ways for ballerinas to move, eroticism) **[documented as reported, UCSC 2025]**; Taruskin shows folk appropriation produced Stravinsky's modernist language **[documented]**. On this reading borrowing is how ballet's grammars grew.
- *Reading B (harmful and still active):* Chan and Georgina Pazcoguin's **Final Bow for Yellowface** pledge (2017–18) commits signatory companies to eliminating "outdated and offensive stereotypes of Asians (Yellowface) on our stages" **[documented, quoted in Harvard Crimson 2020]**; Järvinen shows blackface roles persisting in revivals, museum exhibitions and pedagogy **[documented]**. The Guardian (2020) lists *La Bayadère*, *Le Corsaire*, *Schéhérazade*, Nutcracker variations and the Moor in *Petrushka* as the main cases **[documented]**.
- *The practical middle:* Chan's position is explicitly "the opposite of cancel culture": re-imagine rather than drop (e.g. a company turning the "Tea" dancer into a cricket, a Chinese symbol of good fortune) **[documented, Fjord Review; UCSC]**. This is the concept-DNA lesson: **a world's sources carry obligations**, and a remake can keep the anchor (choreography, story) while revising the borrowed surface.

### 3.2 Notes on key documents

- **Petipa to Tchaikovsky.** The plans gave "extremely detailed instructions for the composition of each number, down to the tempo and number of bars" **[documented, standard references; Wiley translations]**. Petipa also asked for additions in rehearsal (Tchaikovsky wrote the 10-bar *Entrée de chats* for the Puss-in-Boots number at his request) **[documented, Wikipedia number list]**. Read as a concept document, this is an *obligations* specification: the commissioning choreographer fixes the time structure the composer must fill. Tchaikovsky's contract required the violin répétiteur by mid-August and the full score by November **[documented, Tchaikovsky Research]**. For *Swan Lake* (Moscow 1877) no such instructions survive **[documented, Petipa Society]**.
- **Notation as handover and as evidence.** The Sergeyev scores let Sergeyev stage *The Sleeping Princess* for Diaghilev (1921), *Giselle* for the Paris Opéra (1924), and the Vic-Wells classics in the 1930s; they lay unread for decades because no one could read Stepanov; they were sold to Harvard in 1967 (*Swan Lake*) and 1969 (the rest) **[documented, Petipa Society]**. They underpin the Kirov's 1999 *Sleeping Beauty* reconstruction (Vikharev) and Ratmansky's reconstructions (e.g. Bolshoi *Giselle* 2019, combining Sergeyev and Justamant) **[documented, Scholl blurb; Petipa Society]**. Scholl's book shows a historically informed revival can be *denounced* by the tradition that claims the work: the "real" work is contested **[documented]**.
- **What usually does not survive openly.** Commercial production bibles of living companies, lighting cue sheets, budgets, and contracts are normally held privately (ROH Collections; company archives) and are not online; design drawings by artists still in copyright are viewable but not reusable: in life+70 jurisdictions Goncharova (d. 1962) enters the public domain in 2033 and Picasso (d. 1973) in 2044, while Bakst (d. 1924), Golovin (d. 1930) and Matisse (d. 1954, from 1 January 2025) are now public domain; Benois (d. 1960) from 2031 **[argued from death dates; check US terms and per-image museum photo rights, see `../scope/06`]**. Pre-1900 livrets, programmes, notation and LoC/Gallica scans are generally public domain **[probable]**.

## Part 4. Performance and ballet as a model in design and world-building teaching

**Summary finding [argued].** Design education has used *ballet specifically* as a teaching model in one clear historical case (the Bauhaus stage workshop and Schlemmer's *Triadic Ballet*) and in one living one (McGregor's *Mind and Movement*, Forsythe's Synchronous Objects). Scenography theory uses *theatre and opera* far more than ballet. World-building scholarship from media studies barely discusses live performance at all. That gap is an opening for the course.

### 4.1 The Bauhaus stage: ballet as a design curriculum (1921–29)

- The Bauhaus stage workshop was set up in summer 1921 (first under Lothar Schreyer), and Oskar Schlemmer ran it from 1923/1925 to 1929 on a purpose-built stage in Dessau; Klee's 1922 diagram of the curriculum put *Bau und Bühne* (building and stage) at the centre **[probable, Nikolić 2014, *SAJ* 6, doi 10.5937/saj1401043n, open access]**. Gropius, in his preface to *The Theater of the Bauhaus*, says the stage shop "attracted students from all departments and workshops" **[documented, Gropius ed., *The Theater of the Bauhaus*, trans. Wensinger (Wesleyan 1961); English translation of *Die Bühne im Bauhaus*, Bauhausbücher 4, 1925; PDF circulates on monoskop]**.
- Schlemmer's *Triadisches Ballett* (begun 1912, premiered Stuttgart 1922, shown at the 1923 Bauhaus exhibition): three acts, three performers, twelve dances, eighteen costumes, each act a different colour and mood **[probable, Nikolić quoting secondary source]**. Schlemmer named its three bases as coloured three-dimensional costumes, the figure as an environment of basic mathematical shapes, and the corresponding movement in space **[probable]**. His essay "Mensch und Kunstfigur" ("Man and Art Figure", 1925) treats costume as the means of turning the dancer into an abstract figure **[documented title]**. Schlemmer: the elements of the stage are SPACE, FORM, COLOUR and LIGHT, "we can convert and transfigure space through form, color, and light" **[documented as quoted in Nikolić 2014, from *Theater of the Bauhaus*]**.
- **Why it matters.** This is the earliest well-known case of a design school teaching *concept development across disciplines through a ballet*: a fixed rule set (three, geometry, colour per act), a costume-as-architecture grammar, and a collective making process over ten years with students **[argued]**. A 2021 ACM study (Ting, Lin and Lin) reconstructs the *Triadic Ballet* as a cross-field design exercise **[documented abstract]**.

**Hann is the bridge text for this program [argued].** She explicitly adopts "worlding" (after Haraway) as a process, refuses to say scenographics "create worlds", and says they "score orders of world" through "world narratives (what belongs) and materialities (what materializes)" **[documented, Hann 2020, open access, doi 10.22456/2236-3254.103570]**. That pair maps directly onto *premise/rules* (what belongs) and *anchor expression/collection map* (what materialises).

### 4.3 Production design and "world building" as a design discipline

- **Alex McDowell** (production designer of *Fight Club*, *Minority Report*; William Cameron Menzies Chair of Production Design at USC; director of the USC World Building Media Lab and World Building Institute) is credited with originating "world building" as a narrative-design practice c. 2000; on *Minority Report* he was hired the same day as the writer, so the world was designed in parallel with the script **[documented, USC bios; McDowell, "Storytelling Shapes the Future", *Journal of Futures Studies* 2019, PDF]**. He has also designed for theatre and opera, including the MIT Media Lab robot opera *Death and the Powers* (Tod Machover) **[documented, World Building Institute bio]**. His "World Building Mandala" (2018) is the closest industry diagram to a Concept DNA **[documented title]**.
- **The parallel [argued].** McDowell's "design the world before the story" is what Gautier and Saint-Georges did with the Wili legend (a world-rule first, then a plot), and what Diaghilev's committee did with a season.

### 4.4 Experience design and immersive theatre

- B. Joseph Pine II and James H. Gilmore, *The Experience Economy: Work Is Theatre and Every Business a Stage* (Harvard Business School Press, 1999; rev. 2011, 2019/2020 with new subtitle *Competing for Customer Time, Attention, and Money*). Chapter titles are theatre metaphors ("Setting the Stage", "The Show Must Go On", "Work Is Theatre", "Now Act Your Part", "Encore: Exit, Stage Right") **[documented, TOC]**. Theatre is the explicit model; ballet is not **[probable]**.
- Immersive theatre literature: Josephine Machon, *(Syn)aesthetics* (2009) and *Immersive Theatres: Intimacy and Immediacy in Contemporary Performance* (Palgrave, 2013); Gareth White, "On Immersive Theatre", *Theatre Research International* 37.3 (2012); W. B. Worthen on *Sleep No More* (*Theatre Journal* 64.1, 2012); Adam Alston, "Audience Participation and Neoliberal Value" (*Performance Research* 18.2, 2013) and "The Promise of Experience: Immersive Theatre in the Experience Economy" (2016), which compares Punchdrunk with Walt Disney World **[documented, Exa/DOI records]**. Punchdrunk's world-pieces are largely *danced*: co-director and choreographer Maxine Doyle's movement carries the narrative in *Sleep No More* **[probable]**, which makes immersive theatre the most direct contemporary heir of ballet's world-making.
- Design-agency literature: Tinker Imagineers, *Worlds of Wonder* (on immersive storytelling where "places and spaces" are the narrative medium) **[documented mention]**.
- **Critique to teach [argued from Alston].** Experience design idealises the audience's experience and can turn it into a commodity; Alston's warning parallels Hann's worry about "scenic" distance and Kraut's about who owns the made thing.

### 4.5 World-building scholarship and performance

- **Mark J. P. Wolf, *Building Imaginary Worlds: The Theory and History of Subcreation* (Routledge, 2012).** The publisher lists the media treated as "literature, comics, film, radio, television, board games, video games, the Internet, and more"; theatre and dance are not named **[documented]**. The history chapter includes a section on "movies and theater" and treats Baum's Oz as an early transmedial world whose stories began as books, musicals, comic strips or plays **[documented, two reviews]**. Its structural categories (space, time, characters; "infrastructures" such as maps, timelines, genealogies, languages; transmedial "windows": description, visualisation, auralisation, interactivation, deinteractivation; "concentric circles of authorship"; canonicity) **[documented, reviews]** are directly usable for ballet, but **ballet and opera are, so far as seen, not discussed** **[probable: not checked against the full text or the 1,400-world appendix]**. Wolf (ed.), *Revisiting Imaginary Worlds* (2016), *The Routledge Companion to Imaginary Worlds* (2017) and *Exploring Imaginary Worlds* (2020) were not checked chapter by chapter **[gap]**.
- **Henry Jenkins**, *Convergence Culture* (2006) on transmedia "world-making" **[probable]**; Lisbeth Klastrup and Susana Tosca on "transmedial worlds" (2004) **[probable]**. Neither treats ballet.
- **The gap and the claim [argued].** Media-studies world-building theory begins from literature and screen and treats live performance as marginal; scenography theory (Hann) has begun to adopt "worlding"; dance studies (Leon, Forsythe, deLahunta) has developed "expanded choreography" and "choreographic objects" as organisational systems. Nobody seen in this pass has put ballet's *concept documents* (livret, composer's plan, design, notation, revival licence) into the world-building frame. That is the course's distinctive contribution.

## Part 5. Mapping to the Concept DNA fields [argued]

Everything in this part is our interpretation. Each row gives the theory that best *names* the field, the historical document that best *embodies* it, and a teaching prompt.

| Concept DNA field | Ballet theory that names it | Document that embodies it | What the history teaches | Prompt for learners |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Premise** | Ménestrier's single *Sujet*; Noverre's dramatic subject; Gautier's "pretty ballet" idea; Balanchine's counter-claim that the score can be the premise | Vsevolozhsky's 1888 letter; Gautier's 1841 letter to Heine | A premise can be a theme (court), a story (Noverre to *drambalet*), a piece of music (Balanchine) or a rule set (Cunningham). Name which kind yours is. | Write your premise three ways: as theme, as story, as system. Which one generates the most? |
| **Perimeter** | Ménestrier's "unity of *dessein*": everything must relate to the one subject; Fokine's "new form for each subject, period and character of the music" | The livret's setting line ("Rhineland, Middle Ages") | The perimeter is what lets many entrées belong to one world without one plot. | List ten things that could appear in your world; cut those that do not relate to the *dessein*. |
| **Rules** | Saint-Hubert's parts and numbers of entrées; the Romantic two-world rule (village by day, Wilis by night); Cunningham's chance procedures and common time; Forsythe's organisational algorithms; Schlemmer's three-ness | Livret world-rules; Cunningham Dance Capsule production notes; Synchronous Objects data | Rules can be narrative (the Wilis dance men to death until dawn), structural (time units), or generative (chance, algorithms). Generative rules make worlds that can be remade. | State three rules of your world: one narrative, one structural, one generative. |
| **Anti-DNA** | Noverre against masks and irrelevant virtuosity; Fokine against "ready-made steps" and conventional mime; Balanchine's "dare not to use everything… eliminate"; Cunningham against integration; *drambalet* against "formalism"; Final Bow for Yellowface against stereotype | Fokine's *Times* letter; *Pravda* "Balletic Falsehood" (as a negative example of anti-DNA imposed from outside) | Every reform theory in ballet is defined as much by its refusals as by its aims. Refusals imposed by an outside authority are a different thing from refusals chosen by makers. | Write five refusals. Mark which you chose and which a client, market or law imposes. |
| **Obligations** | Petipa's bar-count plan to Tchaikovsky; Saint-Hubert's dancing masters adapting to noble performers; the star vehicle (Grisi for *Giselle*); the court occasion; state requirements (socialist realism) | Petipa's instructions (Wiley 1985, appendices); Tchaikovsky's contract deadlines | Obligations are not the enemy of the concept; Tchaikovsky wrote masterpieces inside bar counts. Record them explicitly. | List your fixed constraints (time, budget, venue, star, client, occasion) as Petipa would: number, duration, character. |
| **Collection map** | Ménestrier's division of a theme into entrées (sky/earth; four seasons); the Petipa *divertissement*; Diaghilev's season and mixed bill; Cunningham's *Events* (excerpts recombined) | Ballets Russes season programmes; *Sleeping Beauty* Act 3 number list | Ballet has always been a collection of numbers held by one world; the impresario's season is a collection of works held by one sensibility. | Draw your world as a set of "entrées": which pieces, in what order, which are optional? |
| **Anchor expression** | Fokine: the movement vocabulary is the work; Balanchine: the score is the anchor; Gautier: "the dance itself"; Croce/Scheijen: the works that survive are those "with the strongest dance content" | Notation scores (Sergeyev, Justamant); the score; the iconic design (Bakst's *Schéhérazade*) | Decide which single expression must survive any remake. In ballet history, choreography and music outlived décor and story. | Name your anchor. If everything else were redesigned, what must stay? |
| **Adjacent translation** | Marian Smith: ballet-pantomime borrowed opera's gesture-music so audiences could read it; *drambalet*'s literature-to-dance method; Bellow: painting translated into "duration and dimension"; Hann: scenographics in gardening, marketing, interiors; McDowell's film-to-urban/biology world building | Ballets Russes designs in fashion (Bakst and Poiret), posters; Synchronous Objects (dance to data to objects) | Translation goes both ways: ballet took from literature, opera, folk art, painting; and gave to fashion, film, design. The *mechanism* (Wolf's "windows") matters more than the medium. | Translate one element of your world into a medium that has no narrative (a textile, a scent, a garden, a data visualisation). |
| **Rights and money** | Kraut on choreographic copyright (race and gender); Garafola on the impresario's market; the Balanchine Trust and Cunningham Trust licensing; RePlay's licences for "collectively 'authored'" material | Dance Capsules licensing tier; Dance Notation Bureau scores as "copyrightable publication quality"; credit lines (e.g. *Schéhérazade* libretto dispute) | Who is credited and who owns the steps were contested from Noverre vs Angiolini to Balanchine's estate. Decide early, write it down. | Draft the credit line and the licence for your world before you make it. Who is dropped? |
| **Handover** | Notation as handover (Stepanov to Sergeyev to Diaghilev, Vic-Wells, Ratmansky); Cunningham Dance Capsules; Forsythe's choreographic objects; McGregor's *Mind and Movement* | Harvard Sergeyev collection (digitised 2024); Capsules; *Mind and Movement* box | A world that cannot be handed over dies with its makers (most Ballets Russes collaborations went "to museum heaven"). Handover documents are a design task. | Make the "capsule" for your world: what would a stranger need to remake it? |
| **Lifecycle** | Scholl's "legend in progress": a classic is re-made, contested and re-authenticated; revivals vs reconstructions; RePlay online 2009–21 then offline; Chan's "re-imagine, not cancel" | Kirov 1999 *Sleeping Beauty* reconstruction; PNB 2011 *Giselle* with Smith and Fullington; Final Bow pledge | Ballets have long afterlives in many versions; which is "the" work is a social decision. Digital archives need owners after the grant. | Plan three futures: preserved, re-imagined, retired. What triggers each? |
| **Grammars** | Blasis's codified vocabulary; Fokine's per-work vocabulary; Balanchine's classical vocabulary as liberation; Forsythe's Improvisation Technologies; McGregor's Choreographic Thinking Tools; Schlemmer's space/form/colour/light; Hann's scenographics | *Code of Terpsichore* plates; Stepanov alphabet; *Improvisation Technologies*; *Mind and Movement* principles poster | Ballet separates a shared grammar (the classical vocabulary) from each work's dialect. Strong worlds either invent a grammar or bend an inherited one visibly. | Write your world's grammar as twelve principles (cf. *Mind and Movement*). Which are inherited, which invented? |
| **Sources** (program field) | Taruskin's "neonationalism"; Smith on originality; Chan and Järvinen on Orientalism and blackface | Heine, Perrault, Afanasyev, Juszkiewicz, Pushkin; Roerich's ethnography | Makers hide sources (Stravinsky) or overstate speed (Gautier). Borrowed cultures carry obligations to the people they come from. | List sources with how you used each (quoted, adapted, abstracted, invented around) and who could object. |

**A teaching sequence drawn from the history [argued].**
1. *Court ballet* → premise as theme and perimeter as "unity of design" (Ménestrier).
2. *Noverre and the Romantic livret* → premise as story; the written scenario as the concept document (Nye; Smith).
3. *Petipa–Tchaikovsky* → obligations as a specification.
4. *Ballets Russes* → team, curator, credit, borrowing (Garafola, Taruskin, Bellow, Järvinen).
5. *Balanchine / Cunningham* → the anchor and the rule set as alternatives to story.
6. *Drambalet* → anti-DNA and obligations imposed by an authority.
7. *Forsythe / McGregor / Davies* → grammar, handover and process as public artefacts.
8. *Hann / McDowell / immersive theatre* → transfer to world-building beyond the stage.
