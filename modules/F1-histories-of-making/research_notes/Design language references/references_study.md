# Reference study: a design language for F1

Twenty references supplied on 20 October 2026, read for what each does, what the course can take from it, and what it should leave. Then a synthesis: the principles, the tokens that change, a catalogue of components mapped to the course's surfaces, and what each existing surface would become. The references are saved as `refs/01…20`.

The course already has a finish the lead rates highest (v4: washi ground, sumi line, indigo for trade, seal red for the state, Newsreader and IBM Plex). The study does not replace it; it tells it where to grow. Every "take" below is checked against the project's rules: open licences, no invented placement, confidence words, quotes of 25 words or fewer, no points or badges, the Yoshiwara read not played.

## 1. The references, one by one

### Group A. Editorial restraint (how a page breathes)

**01 · Wall of Art shop.** A two-column editorial grid: full-bleed photographs of a studio, then four product frames on white with one-line captions, then two more photographs, then a quiet footer. One typeface, one size for almost everything, captions under images not over them, a top bar with five words. *Take:* the captioning rule (the caption sits under the image, never on it, in the smallest size); the rhythm of a page as alternating "scene / objects / scene"; the "New arrival" tag as the only decoration. This is what the course's chapter openers and object galleries should feel like: a gallery wall, not a dashboard. *Leave:* the commerce chrome (prices, cart).

**02 · Paris editorial (illustrated travel stories).** Each story owns one colour (ochre, violet, rose, green, oxblood) and one illustrated motif (a tree, a cup, a croissant, a stamp). Titles mix a roman and an italic in the same line ("City *Unplugged*: Insider Tips…"), set in a bookish serif with a small-caps deck beneath. The phone frames show the same system stacking cleanly. *Take:* one colour and one motif per lecture or unit, carried everywhere that lecture appears (its card in the Atlas, its threshold beat, its tick on the timeline); the roman/italic title pairing for thresholds; an illustrated glyph per story as its index mark. *Leave:* the full-bleed illustration style (the course's pictures are the objects themselves, CC0 prints, not drawings), and the warm pastel palette, which would fight the washi ground.

**07 · "Traditional art forms around the world" (card grid).** Eight tall cards, each a cropped artwork with a bold name, a country and a flag; a row of four icon-words beneath ("Creativity · Tradition · Culture · Heritage"). It is tidy and it is generic: the flags reduce a tradition to a state, the icons say nothing, and the crops are interchangeable. *Take:* the card proportion (tall, image two-thirds, two lines of type) for the Atlas's unit cards and the composer's option cards. *Leave:* the flags (a tradition is a place and a people, drawn as a wash on the globe, never a state symbol), the icon row, the slogan, and the AI-generated look: every card in the course carries a real object with its holder and licence.

### Group B. Objects explained (how a thing is taken apart and annotated)

**03 · National Geographic, Tutankhamun's nested shrines.** One exploded vertical: four shrines, a pall, a sarcophagus lid, three coffin lids, the body, the bed, the base, each pulled apart on the vertical axis and labelled at the right with a bold name and one sentence. Black ground, nothing else on the page. *Take:* the exploded view as the course's demonstration format: the block stack of Print the Wave (key block, colour blocks, paper, baren) and the Wave's five depth planes become one exploded vertical, labelled the same way, reader-stepped. The label pattern, bold name + one plain sentence, is the course's annotation voice. *Leave:* the black ground (the course's demonstrations sit on paper); the gold render (ours are photographs and reconstructions, labelled as such).

**04 · Petrie's predynastic pottery plate (sequence dating).** Nine classes in rows, each a shelf of drawn vessels with a type number and a sequence-date range under each. The whole chronology of a culture's pottery is one page, readable at a glance as "shape against time". *Take:* the typology plate: a row per class, a shelf of objects per row, a date range under each object. For Edo: seals by regime (none / kiwame / nanushi / aratame) with their years; print formats (ōban, chūban, hosoban, triptych) with their prices; the Tōkaidō's print stations. For the whole course: a plate per unit's material (vessels, textiles, tablets). It is also the honest form for a date range: the number under the object is a span, not a point. *Leave:* nothing; this is the strongest reference for the course's "object as evidence" argument.

**08 · DK, *Art: The Definitive Visual Guide*, Fayum portraits and Hepworth / Giacometti spreads.** Each object page has: a title and one-line deck in italic; the object large with leader-line annotations ending in small bold labels ("Divine gold", "Brush strokes"); a tiny location map and a scale figure (a human silhouette against the object); a running header band with the era and page range in a colour tab; a boxed sidebar ("Artist's template", the facial-proportion diagram); a pull-quote in a larger size with two bold words. *Take:* nearly all of it. The annotated object page is the course's missing surface: every object the unit shows (the Wave, the key-block proof, the Tenpō order, the Hundred Views sheets, the 1859 map) gets leaders, a scale figure, a location thumb, a date and holder line, and one pull-fact. The era band in the header is what the act bar already is. *Leave:* the DK density (five images and four boxes per spread); the course takes one object per page and lets it breathe, as reference 01 does.

**09 · DK, Mamluk glass bottle and *The Kiss*.** The same system applied to a vessel (leaders to "central medallion", "warriors", "gilded background") and to a painting; a "Creating enamelled glass" four-step process box with small drawings; a "Five types of weapon are shown" fact in large type; a Byzantine mosaics comparison box. *Take:* the process box as a step-strip (the printing sequence, the seal-reading sequence); the comparison box ("the influence of manuscript illustration") as the pattern for the unit's comparisons (three Waves; the Wave and its Van Gogh copy); the large-type fact with the number in bold. *Leave:* as above.

### Group C. Time made visible (how a span, a sequence or a density reads)

**05 · Art history basic timeline (Fractalion).** A span list: one row per period, the start year at the left, the end year at the right, a rule between them and the name in the middle; dark oxblood ground, serif, small caps for BC/AD. Thirty rows read in ten seconds. *Take:* the span list as the compact timeline for eras, regimes and acts, where the lane chart is too much: the six eras, the four censor regimes, the five acts, the 41 nengō. Each row is a bar with two dates, exactly the "range is a bar, never a point" rule from the editorial process. *Leave:* the ground colour (the course has one dark surface, the lecture room, and it is sumi, not oxblood); rows for things with no end date.

**06 · History of Red (Art Lounge).** Six circles, each a material sample (ochre, cinnabar, cadmium), joined by a dotted line, each with a period and two sentences. *Take:* the material biography: a colour or a material told as a chain of samples with dates. Prussian blue is this exactly (Berlin 1704 → Nagasaki 1824 → the fan 1829 → the Wave 1831), and so are indigo, safflower pink, Echizen paper and cherry wood. One row of samples, each a circle cut from a real object, each with its date and confidence. *Leave:* the drop-shadowed sticker look and the hashtag; the course's samples are flat circles on paper.

**10 · Works by year (a print-maker's catalogue as a wall over a time axis).** Thumbnails stacked in columns above the years they belong to; under the axis, bubbles sized by the count per year; a cursor at one year. The density of a life's work is visible before any thumbnail is read. *Take:* the works wall: the Hundred Views (118 sheets, 1856–58), Hokusai's late series, Tsutaya's publications, stacked over the lane chart's axis, with the run bubbles the chart already has. It is the timeline focus for a *run* of objects. *Leave:* the black ground; the stacking goes on paper above the lane chart.

**11 · Phase board (form finding → technique → configuration → … → refinement).** Images above and below one horizontal line, divided into seven named phases by vertical rules. *Take:* the phase strip for processes that have stages rather than dates: the workshop (drawing → key block → proofs → colour blocks → registration → printing → the sheet), the life of a print (design → censor → carving → printing → sale → afterlife), a learner's own progress through a unit. Above the line the artefacts, below the line the tools or the people. *Leave:* the mood-board scale (seventy images); the course uses four to eight per phase.

### Group D. Place made visible (how a globe, a map and a route are read)

**12 · In-flight map (London → Hong Kong).** A dark globe horizon with the route arcing over it; under it one bar: departed / arriving, with icons at the moments along the flight (meals, shopping) and the plane at "now". "Explore map" is a single button. *Take:* the journey bar: every route scene gets one bar under the map with the stops as marks, the dated events as icons, the "now" glyph at the cursor, and the two ends labelled with their dates. It is the scene's clock and its table of contents at once, and it is what the "crawl" mode should look like. *Leave:* the space-black ground, the photoreal Earth; the course's globe is paper and ink.

**13 · Surveillance Watch (globe, list, panel).** Three columns: a country list with counts at the left, the globe in the middle with arcs from the selected entity, a panel at the right with the entity's name, a paragraph, "used on / provided to / affiliations / type" and a sources list with publication names. *Take:* the three-column Atlas: list (chapters, lectures, with counts) · globe · panel (the selected thing, its paragraph, its relations as linked lists, its sources). The sources block at the bottom of every panel is the course's honesty line made structural. The arcs drawn only for the selected entity is "dim, don't hide". *Leave:* the surveillance aesthetic (monospace labels, acid green); the course's panel is set in Newsreader on paper.

**14 · AR driving display.** Place labels anchored in space with a leader and an ETA ("Lighthouse · 15 min"); a chevron for the turn; the rest of the view untouched. *Take:* the anchored label with a leader line and a secondary line (distance, date, count) under the name: "Nagasaki · Dejima / 1824". And the discipline: three labels in a whole landscape. *Leave:* everything else; it is a car.

**15 · Tactical contour map (animated).** Contour lines, a single glowing point, one red route, labels in boxes with tick corners, grid faint behind. *Take:* the contour drawn as pencil that turns to ink around the focus (the Atlas already has pencil contours); the single glow at the current place; a route in one accent. *Leave:* the whole aesthetic (sci-fi, black, scan lines) and the animation that never stops; the course has no ambient motion inside a scene.

**16 · Rotterdam energy map.** A dark city with yellow heat-glows sized by power, red heat backbones, white road web, a legend at the bottom with sized circles explained. *Take:* the legend as a drawn key with sized samples ("Power > 300 MW" as a circle of that size); the glow as the cohort-density layer the Atlas planned (anonymous, opt-in, aggregated); the backbone line for a route that carries volume (Hayashi's 218 shipments as a thicker line, not 218 arcs). *Leave:* the dark ground and the colour (yellow and red on black); on paper, density is a darker wash, as the Atlas already draws it.

**17 · Topographic route with elevation labels.** A dark relief, a single orange road, contour lines, elevation callouts in boxes along the road, place names set in small caps spaced wide. *Take:* the route with callouts at its stations: the Tōkaidō with Hakone's pass as the one elevation callout the content supports (the climb is why the station matters), each station a box with the station number and the print's title. *Leave:* the ground; the callout boxes with corner ticks become paper cards with a hairline.

**18 · Weather globe with a side panel and a stepper.** A globe with marks and alert glyphs; a right panel with the selected storm's name, coordinates, a 3D terrain tile, two figures (force, wind speed), and "previous storm / next storm" at the bottom; a bottom row of counts by kind ("5 items · tornado, storm…"). *Take:* the previous/next stepper in the panel (the scene stepper; the Atlas's "next lecture in this chapter"); the panel's two big figures (the scene's date and number); the counts-by-kind row as the Atlas's legend of highlight kinds (journeys · incidents · lectures · objects). *Leave:* the black ground.

**19 · Statskog Millionen (Norway in one green).** A whole country as one ink wash on one dark green, white dots as projects, a season list at the left ("Høst 2020, Vår 2020, …") as the time navigation, a single card rising from a selected dot with a picture, a line and a figure. Nothing else. *Take:* the one-colour map: for a chapter or unit, the territory in a single wash and the marks in one ink; the season list as a vertical time index (the acts; the eras); the one card that rises from the selected mark. This is the closest reference to the Atlas's own look and shows how far restraint can go. *Leave:* nothing; invert it to paper.

**20 · Shipments dashboard (glass cards over a pale map).** A pale terrain map with routes; translucent cards with counters ("1,780 active shipments"), small bar charts, and a row of ship cards with per-shipment progress bars between two dates. *Take:* the per-item progress bar between two dates (a shipment, a print's journey, a lecture's span) and the counter card ("218 shipments · 166,000 prints"); the glass panel as the way a card can sit over a map without hiding it (frosted paper rather than frosted glass). *Leave:* the dashboard density (nine panels), the sans everywhere, the lime accent.

## 2. What the references agree on

Read together, the twenty say five things.

1. **The object is the page.** DK (08, 09), the Tut exploded view (03), Petrie's plate (04) and the WOA gallery (01) all put one thing large and explain it with the smallest possible apparatus: a leader, a bold label, one sentence. The course's pages have been built around the stage and the story; the object page is missing. It should be the most common page in the course.
2. **Time is a bar, a shelf or a wall, not a ruler with a play button.** The span list (05), the material chain (06), the works wall (10), the phase strip (11) and the journey bar (12) each give time a shape the content has: a period is a bar, a material is a chain, a life's work is a wall, a process is a strip, a route is a bar with stops. The lane chart stays as the instrument; these are the five views it can take.
3. **One colour and one mark per thing.** Paris (02) gives each story a colour and a glyph; Norway (19) gives a whole country one colour; Rotterdam (16) one glow. The course's highlight vocabulary (six kinds) and glyph library (fourteen) are the same idea; the references add: a colour and a motif *per lecture*, carried everywhere that lecture appears.
4. **The panel beside the map carries the sources.** Surveillance Watch (13) and the weather globe (18) both end their panel with sources and a previous/next. The Atlas panel should be built this way: name, paragraph, relations as links, two figures, sources, previous/next.
5. **Labels are few, anchored and two-line.** The AR display (14), the topo route (17) and the HUD map (15) label three or four things in a whole landscape, each with a leader and a second line (distance, elevation, date). This is the course's label budget, confirmed, with the second line added: the date or the count under the name.

And one thing they warn against: eight of the twenty are dark, glowing, sci-fi or dashboard surfaces (13–18, 20). Their *mechanics* (list–globe–panel, stepper, journey bar, sized legend, anchored labels, glass cards) transfer; their *looks* do not. The course's answer to a glowing point on black is a seal-red pulse on washi.

## 5. What each existing surface becomes

- **The Atlas** becomes list · globe · panel (13, 18) on paper, with the one-colour unit tier (19), sized legends (16), the journey bar under any playing scene (12), and a chapter list that carries each lecture's colour and motif (02). The panel ends with sources and previous/next.
- **The unit's opening** becomes a gallery (01): the act bar, then a scene photograph or the act's object, then the act's objects as framed cards with one-line captions, then the first scene sheet.
- **Scenes** keep the framework (study 2) and gain the journey bar (12), anchored two-line labels (14, 17), route callouts for stations (17), and the counter card as a frosted panel (20).
- **Objects** gain the page they never had: the annotated object page (08) for every image the unit shows, the exploded view (03) for the two demonstrations, and the typology plate (04) for seals, formats and stations.
- **The timeline** keeps the lane chart and gains four views of the same data: the span list (05), the works wall (10), the material chain (06) and the phase strip (11), chosen by what is being shown.
- **The lectures** gain an identity each (02) and the dark room (05): sumi ground, washi text, the lecture's colour as the only accent.
- **People** keep their three layers; the character sheet takes the DK pattern (portrait, dates, one paragraph, "appears in", sources).

## 6. What the references do not solve

- None of them is accessible by default; the course's rules (keyboard, reduced motion, text twins, 4.5:1 contrast on paper) are added on top of every take.
- None of them handles confidence; the course's words (documented, probable, contested, argued) and the range-as-bar rule go into every component that shows a date.
- The illustrated references (02, 07) draw their subjects; the course shows the objects themselves, so illustration is confined to the lecture motifs and the fourteen glyphs.
- Reference 07 (traditional art forms with flags) is the one to study as a warning: a clean card grid can still be generic. The course's cards carry a real object, a place as a wash, a date, and never a flag.
