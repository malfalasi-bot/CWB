# How serious research, curation and content production is done elsewhere, and what F1 should take from it

Written 20 October 2026 by Claude Fable 5.1 from the web, for the F1 Histories of making project. Every statement below that describes another organisation's practice carries the URL it was read at. Where a practice could not be found on the web it says "not found" rather than guessing. Pages that could not be read are named at the end.

The project this is written for: AI agents research a subject from the web into a dossier (events, people, objects, counts, arguments, gaps; now being widened to concepts, techniques, materials and supply, works, institutions and economics, bodies and labour); every date and number goes into `claims.yaml` with a confidence word (documented, probable, contested, argued), sources, alternates, a display string and an editor note; `lint_claims.py` checks pages against the register; objects are open-licence only; a six-level protocol governs sensitive content; AI agents build pages that a script grades; the register is frozen and tagged before any page is authored. No human specialist has yet reviewed anything.

## 1. Editorial and fact-checking processes of reference and educational publishers

### Smarthistory

- Contributors must hold "a Ph.D. in a relevant discipline and have significant teaching experience" (or an equivalent for knowledge-bearers and practitioners outside the academy); specialised areas are coordinated by a contributing editor; the site lists 50 editors and more than 800 contributors. https://smarthistory.org/contribute-an-essay and https://smarthistory.org/about/
- Essays are 1,000–1,500 words on a single work, begin with a hook, define every specialist term in the text or in a one-to-two-sentence pop-up, and "assume readers know nothing about the topic". They are explicitly "not original research". https://smarthistory.org/contribute-an-essay
- Review is open, not blind. The example given: essays on the art of Africa "are reviewed by two to three Ph.D. art historians, at least one with expertise in that area". https://smarthistory.org/contribute-an-essay
- Images: Smarthistory finds openly licensed images or supplies its own photographs; for works in copyright it decides with the contributor, who "must not act unilaterally on images". Citation is a modified Chicago note style. https://smarthistory.org/contribute-an-essay
- Not found: a published description of copy-editing rounds, revision dates on essays, or a corrections policy.

### The Met's Heilbrunn Timeline of Art History

- More than 1,000 essays and 300 chronologies, "authored by The Met's experts, curators, conservators, scientists, and educators", "regularly updated and enriched". https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/timeline-of-art-history and https://www.metmuseum.org/met-publications/heilbrunn-timeline-of-art-history
- Each essay carries a named contributor with department, or the department alone where no individual is named ("Department of Greek and Roman Art"), the month and year of publication, a Further Reading list, and a fixed citation block, e.g. `Dabrowski, Magdalena. "Geometric Abstraction." In Heilbrunn Timeline of Art History. New York: The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 2000–. http://www.metmuseum.org/toah/hd/geab/hd_geab.htm (October 2004)`. https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/geometric-abstraction and https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-roman-republic
- Revision is stamped per essay in the form "(originally published October 2000, last revised September 2014)". Example in the related-essays list at https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/hd/teot2/hd_teot2.htm
- Not found: any public account of the internal review steps between a curator's draft and publication.

### Oxford Dictionary of National Biography

- Structure: three permanent Research Editors "undertake research which precedes the choosing of subjects", commission "hundreds of articles each year", "edit contributions for factual accuracy, balance, and accessibility" and "where required ... extend texts with further primary and secondary research". "Each article is peer-reviewed by external specialists who work with the Faculty editors." Above them: 14 Consultant Editors for large chronological and thematic sections, over 350 Associate Editors for themes and periods, and more than 450 Advisors in 43 subject panels who advise on who is included. https://impact.ref.ac.uk/casestudies/CaseStudy.aspx?Id=3705
- The founding editor "built a pyramidal structure of contributors and editors that passed commissions down the line from Oxford and took finished articles up the line to a cadre of Research Editors". More than 10,000 authors wrote the 2004 edition. https://archives.history.ac.uk/makinghistory/resources/articles/ODNB.html
- Update cadence: monthly, each update numbered (the 133rd added 229 lives), with a published editorial introduction; the January update is recent deaths, the others thematic; "Updates also revise existing biographies in response to new research." https://www.history.ox.ac.uk/oxford-dictionary-national-biography , https://www.oxforddnb.com/news and https://www.oxforddnb.com/page/about
- Lives are added "some three or four years after death" so that they can be assessed with some distance. https://archives.history.ac.uk/makinghistory/resources/articles/ODNB.html
- A "Brief Notes for Contributors" PDF exists behind https://www.oxforddnb.com/page/contributors ; its word-length rules were not read (not found on the open web).

### Grove Art and Grove Music (Oxford University Press)

- "Every article, whether new or revised, receives peer review before publication." Suggestions for new entries go to area experts and the editorial board; guest editors run major updates project by project; commissioning is managed by in-house editors. https://www.oxfordartonline.com/page/grove-editorial-and-art-image-policies/grove-art-editorial-and-image-policies
- Revision is credited and the earlier text kept: "When an article is revised by a new author, we archive the original entry, so that readers can consult the original and the updated entry side by side ... The name of the reviser is added to the credit line: 'Jane Smith, updated by Brian Jones.'" A newer policy was "introduced to document all updates and authors involved". Same URL.
- Monthly "What's new" posts publish counts of three kinds of change: new articles, revisions, and emendations "as part of our regular corrections process" (January 2026: 4 new, 13 revisions, 279 emendations). https://oxfordartonline.com/page/news
- Grove Music's author page treats an article as "a living document": minor corrections ("fixing a date or adding a citation") at any time; major revisions "subject to review by the editorial board and copy editors"; a reader who notices a problem in someone else's article has it "passed along to the author anonymously". https://www.oxfordmusiconline.com/page/author-resources
- Images: Grove is "not an image provider"; it accepts only images "for which the contributor has cleared all permissions for worldwide, perpetual online use"; its 40,000 outbound image links "are reviewed regularly by the editorial staff; broken links ... must be corrected manually". https://www.oxfordartonline.com/page/grove-editorial-and-art-image-policies/grove-art-editorial-and-image-policies

### Wikipedia

- Featured Article criteria (verbatim headings): 1a well-written; 1b comprehensive ("neglects no major facts or details and places the subject in context"); 1c well-researched ("a thorough and representative survey of the relevant literature; claims are verifiable against high-quality reliable sources and are supported by inline citations where appropriate"); 1d neutral; 1e stable ("its content does not change significantly from day to day"); 1f compliant with copyright and free of plagiarism or close paraphrasing; 2a a concise lead; 2b appropriate structure; 2c consistent citations; 3 media with succinct captions and acceptable copyright status; 4 length and summary style. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Featured_article_criteria
- Verifiability: "People must be able to check that facts and claims in Wikipedia articles are not just made up." "The burden to demonstrate verifiability lies with the editor who adds or restores material", met by "one inline citation to a reliable source that directly supports the contribution"; quotations and anything "challenged or likely to be challenged" need an inline citation; "any material that needs an inline citation but does not have one may be removed". The old formula "the threshold for inclusion is verifiability, not truth" is now an essay, not the policy's wording. A source has three parts that can each be judged: the work, the creator, the publisher. "The best sources have a professional structure for checking or analyzing facts." "Verifiability does not guarantee inclusion." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability
- Reliable sources guideline: "Context matters"; "widespread citation without comment for facts is evidence of a source's reputation"; prefer secondary sources; "Wikipedia employs no systematic mechanism for fact-checking or accuracy", so "Wikipedia articles (and Wikipedia mirrors) in themselves are not reliable sources for any purpose"; self-published sources may be used only about themselves under five conditions; sponsored content is questionable; disputes go to the Reliable Sources Noticeboard. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Reliable_sources
- Source reviewing at FAC: reliability is "a minimal requirement"; the FA bar is "high quality"; the standing question to a nominator is "What makes this source reliable?"; reviewers "carry out spot checks to ensure ... that the sources do indeed support the text, and that the article contains no plagiarism, including close paraphrasing"; "It is unreasonable to expect a reviewer to test each cited statement"; coordinators "usually require spot-checking for first-time nominations"; all links are checked with a tool. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Guidance_on_source_reviewing_at_FAC

### Khan Academy

- Five content principles: learners at the centre; accuracy ("we staff our team with subject matter experts who use a wide range of source materials as inputs. In addition, we put our content through rigorous reviews and monitor feedback regularly"); diversity and inclusion; appropriate context ("Some topics are potentially uncomfortable for learners (for example, historical injustices such as slavery) ... We include these topics ... with supporting context"); editorial integrity (full editorial control even for sponsored content). https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/360054562832-What-are-Khan-Academy-s-Content-Principles
- Use of LLMs (page updated August 2026): LLMs are used for "brainstorming sample questions, generating accessible diagrams, and proofreading content"; "Every piece of AI-assisted content is carefully reviewed, edited, and approved by our content team before publication. We verify factual accuracy, ensure alignment with our content principles and quality standards"; "All published course content ... undergoes human review before it reaches learners." An earlier version of the page said the AI "helps write first drafts of articles" and the team "often edits it significantly". https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/14004618952717-How-does-the-Khan-Academy-content-team-use-large-language-models
- Not found: a public content-standards document with word counts, reading levels or a review checklist.

### The Open University's course-team model

- Modules are "developed by multi-disciplinary module teams" of academics, educational technologists, media specialists, "respected academics from other universities" and external examiners; production "typically takes place over a 2–3 year time frame" with a curriculum manager, editors, graphic artists, external consultants and external assessors; content is "aligned with the module learning outcomes, with a particular focus on designing for retention"; three to five academics write "and comment on multiple drafts"; "the team ... will act as critical readers for other topics in the modules"; after launch there is an annual Quality Monitoring and Enhancement review and "module reviews and revisions conducted at set points in the lifetime of modules". https://university.open.ac.uk/stem/life-health-chemical-sciences/about-us/our-teaching-model
- The classic course team "is traditionally composed of the subject matter specialist, the educational technologist, the electronic media specialist, the editor, the staff tutor, the course manager and the secretary"; the OU's first vice-chancellor called it the university's most important contribution (Perry 1976). Chung, H.-J. (2001), PhD thesis, https://oro.open.ac.uk/54854/1/340787.pdf
- Not found: a current public OU document on developmental testing with students before launch.

### Our World in Data

- Self-description: "we are effectively a data republisher", which "needs a methodology to process, version, and maintain several datasets all the way from the original source's publication site to our platform". https://docs.owid.io/projects/etl/architecture/
- The ETL repository "allows anyone to rebuild and inspect all steps in our data processing ... so that all of curation choices can be understood, alternatives investigated and problems fixed". Pipeline in four steps: ingest and store a snapshot ("a zip file of all the files ... as they were fetched from the original source", with metadata) → extract into dataframes, adding metadata → harmonise common dimensions and enrich the metadata → export to the chart tool. https://github.com/owid/etl/blob/master/docs/overview.rst
- Provenance is a typed object, `origin`, carried by every indicator: `producer`, `title`, `title_snapshot`, `description`, `description_snapshot`, `citation_full` ("If the producer specified how to cite them, this field should be identical to the producer's text"), `date_published`, `date_accessed` ("Exact day when the producer's data (in its current version) was downloaded"), `url_main`, `url_download`, `version_producer`, `license {name, url}`, `attribution` in the form "producer (year)". Each indicator also has `description_processing` ("Must be used if important editorial decisions have been taken during data processing"), `description_key`, `description_from_producer`, `processing_level`; each dataset a `changelog` and `update_period_days`. "Naming the producers preserves provenance that 'Various sources' would hide." https://docs.owid.io/projects/etl/architecture/metadata/reference/
- How a metric is chosen: it covers the world and a long time; it is reliable; "Its construction is transparent" (description, code, raw data); it is easy to understand, well maintained, accessible and licensed; institutions are preferred to single papers because they keep data up to date; a metric that "captures disagreement across sources" is valued because it "helps us to be appropriately uncertain". https://ourworldindata.org/choosing-our-topics-and-metrics
- Source-comparison articles are a standing genre ("Homicide data: how sources differ and when to use which one"). https://ourworldindata.org/homicide-data-how-sources-differ-and-when-to-use-which-one
- Public correction channel: during COVID, "more than 700 users worldwide contributed to our data repository ... more than 7,000 contributions", and staff "reviewed direct public feedback ... for hours each day". https://ourworldindata.org/owid-covid-history
- Not found: a published fact-check checklist for OWID's prose articles.

### Encyclopaedia Britannica

- "Once an article is written, it is reviewed and revised by a team of editors"; articles are edited to be "fair, making clear not only what is known about a topic but also what may still be in dispute"; "They are fact-checked according to a 14-point checklist"; reader-reported mistakes are investigated; "we make it transparent to readers and users how, why, and when we revise articles. Just click on Article History". https://www.britannica.com/editor/The-Editors-of-Encyclopaedia-Britannica/4419
- Roles: subject editors; fact-checkers ("Only when they give the articles a passing grade can the content go to the next level of review"); copy editors; supervisory editors ("review all new or substantially revised articles"); information architects. https://elearn.eb.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/Editorial-Infographic_Flyer.pdf
- Article History is a public table of Type, Description, Contributor, Date (e.g. "Added a paragraph that describes Britannica's editorial process | Adam Augustyn | Aug 05, 2019"); article pages show "Written by", "Fact-checked by" and "Last updated". https://www.britannica.com/topic/Encyclopaedia-Britannica-general-encyclopaedia/additional-info
- The 14 points of the checklist: not found on the public web.

## 2. Digital-humanities data practice

### PeriodO: periods as assertions

- A "gazetteer of scholarly definitions of historical, art-historical, and archaeological periods" that "helps scholars and students see where period definitions overlap or diverge". https://perio.do/en/
- Every period belongs to an *authority* (the source that defines it, "even if it was informally published"). The original label is recorded verbatim. Spatial coverage is recorded twice: the *description* "taken verbatim from the text of source, in whatever form it may appear: table header, figure caption, etc." and a *coverage area* of linked gazetteer places (which may be inferred). Temporal coverage is recorded as the source's own start and stop labels ("end of the 1st century AD") and then parsed to standardised year ranges ("start of the 5th century" → 0401–0434), which can be overridden by hand. A *locator* field holds the page or figure; a *derived from* link relates a period to the one it modifies in another authority; an *editorial note* is "for explaining decisions made by the author of the PeriodO record". Every permalink resolves to structured data. https://perio.do/guide/

### World Historical Gazetteer and the Linked Places format: attestation with source and certainty

- "Rather than presenting a single 'authoritative' record for each place, WHG captures the rich, contested, and temporally-situated nature of historical place knowledge." An attestation is "a claim that connects a Thing to some information, backed by one or more sources": `[THING] --[RELATION]--> [OBJECT]`, "backed by [SOURCES] with [CERTAINTY] during [TIMESPAN]". This gives provenance, disagreement ("Multiple attestations can make conflicting claims"), uncertainty, temporality and historiography ("who said what when"). Certainty is a score 0.0–1.0 plus a qualifier ("probable", "uncertain", "disputed") plus a `certainty_note` ("Description vague; location reconstructed from travel times"); spatial precision is ±km; temporal bounds are fuzzy (`start_earliest` ≠ `start_latest`) with precision descriptors (century, decade, year). https://docs.whgazetteer.org/content/v4/user-guide/getting-started/concepts.html and https://docs.whgazetteer.org/content/v4/data-model/introduction.html
- Worked example: Troy returns three attestations, Hisarlik at 0.95 from excavation, the Homeric location at 0.3 from the Iliad, medieval claims at 0.1 from pilgrimage accounts; "Results enable critical evaluation, not passive consumption." https://docs.whgazetteer.org/content/v4/data-model/usecases.html
- A *meta-attestation* (an attestation about an attestation, for scholarly debate or correction) is a planned extension. https://docs.whgazetteer.org/content/v4/user-guide/reference/glossary.html
- Linked Places format v1.3 (June 2024): `certainty` values `certain`, `less-certain`, `uncertain` on geometries, relations and (since v1.2.2) on `when`; every name carries `citations` with label, year and identifier; records link out with `closeMatch` / `exactMatch` to Wikidata and the Getty TGN; "Our goal is not to define The One unified data model for gazetteers." https://github.com/LinkedPasts/linked-places-format

### Wikidata: references and ranks

- "The majority of statements on Wikidata should be verifiable." A reference uses `stated in (P248)` for publications or `reference URL (P854)` for web pages, with further properties "so that the source can be tracked down if the URL changes". "It is recommended to not add a second reference which is explicitly based on the same source"; references to community-edited sites or aggregators "should be removed if other sources are already present"; "Statements that are only supported by 'imported from Wikimedia project (P143)' are not considered sourced statements"; Wikipedia pages "are not appropriate as sources". https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Help:Sources
- Ranks are separate from references: "References merely state where a data value comes from; ranks indicate what data value is considered the most correct." *Preferred* is "the most current statement or statements that best represent consensus", ideally with a `reason for preferred rank (P7452)` qualifier; *deprecated* is for statements "known to include errors ... or that represent outdated knowledge", with `reason for deprecated rank (P2241)`; "All statements, including deprecated ones, must be verifiable in the sense that a source makes the claim." Deprecation is not for correct past values, which instead take `start time` / `end time` qualifiers. Queries use "best rank". https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Help:Ranking

### The Getty vocabularies (AAT, TGN, ULAN, CONA, IA)

- Editorial guidelines are public and "subject to frequent modification and addition"; data is released under ODC-By 1.0. https://www.getty.edu/research/tools/vocabularies/guidelines
- Minimum fields for a contribution: AAT "term(s), language of term, source of term, scope note"; TGN "name(s), source of name, broader context, place type, coordinates". Contributions "are subjected to an established review procedure"; the online display is refreshed monthly. http://www.getty.edu/research/tools/vocabularies/contribute.html
- "The preferred term must have three sources. Other terms require one authoritative source." A scope note needs "at least one authoritative source" except when it only disambiguates. https://www.getty.edu/research/tools/vocabularies/brief_vocab_training_manual.pdf
- Every record names its contributors and its sources ("Contributors: VP; BWR  Sources: Maidstone, Hagia Sophia (1988); ..."); "The contributor is cited; however, data from multiple contributors may be merged and the Getty maintains final editorial control." More than 350 contributing institutions and projects. https://www.getty.edu/research/tools/vocabularies/intro_to_contributing.pdf and https://www.getty.edu/publications/vocabularies-editorial-guidelines/aat-guidelines/4_appendices/4.4/

### FAIR and CARE

- FAIR (2016): F1 globally unique persistent identifiers; F2 rich metadata; F3 metadata include the data's identifier; F4 indexed in a searchable resource; A1 retrievable by identifier through an open protocol; A2 "metadata are accessible, even when the data are no longer available"; I1 formal shared language; I2 FAIR vocabularies; I3 "qualified references to other (meta)data"; R1 richly described, with R1.1 "a clear and accessible data usage license", R1.2 "detailed provenance", R1.3 "domain-relevant community standards". https://www.go-fair.org/fair-principles/
- CARE (2019): Collective benefit (C1 inclusive development and innovation; C2 improved governance and citizen engagement; C3 equitable outcomes); Authority to control (A1 recognising rights and interests; A2 data for governance; A3 governance of data); Responsibility (R1 positive relationships; R2 expanding capability and capacity; R3 Indigenous languages and worldviews); Ethics (E1 minimising harm and maximising benefit, "Ethical data are data that do not stigmatize or portray Indigenous Peoples, cultures, or knowledges in terms of deficit"; E2 justice; E3 future use, "Metadata should acknowledge the provenance and purpose and any limitations or obligations in secondary use inclusive of issues of consent"). One-pager: https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5d3799de845604000199cd24/t/5da9f4479ecab221ce848fb2/1571419335217/CARE%2BPrinciples_One%2BPagers%2BFINAL_Oct_17_2019.pdf ; landing page https://www.gida-global.org/care ; paper (Carroll et al. 2020, "implement CARE and FAIR Principles in tandem") https://datascience.codata.org/articles/10.5334/dsj-2020-043

### Local Contexts labels and notices

- TK Labels are applied by communities, not institutions: Provenance labels (TK Attribution, TK Clan, TK Family, TK Multiple Communities, TK Community Voice, TK Creative), Protocol labels, Permission labels (TK Open to Commercialization, TK Non-Commercial, TK Community Use Only, TK Outreach, TK Open to Collaboration). They "identify and clarify community-specific rules and responsibilities regarding access and future use". https://localcontexts.org/labels/traditional-knowledge-labels/
- Notices are what institutions and researchers apply, in three kinds: Engagement (Open to Collaborate), Disclosure (TK Notice, BC Notice, Attribution Incomplete) and Collections Care. "The text for the Notices cannot be changed"; they are generated through the Local Contexts Hub; "Communities may then choose how and when to add their Labels." Example in use: the Wilson Museum's collection page with the Open to Collaborate, Attribution Incomplete and TK Notices. https://localcontexts.org/notices/

### Open-access terms at the Smithsonian and the Met

- Smithsonian: CC0 for 2.8 million items at the February 2020 launch and for new digitisations found copyright-free; excluded are objects not yet digitised, under copyright, under "contractual restrictions from a donor, lender, or artist", "culturally sensitive", not fully owned, unsupported formats, trademarks, and products sold for a fee; non-CC0 assets carry "usage conditions apply"; "no known copyright restriction" means "the Smithsonian is unaware of any copyright restrictions ... based on our best efforts"; attribution is not required but a "minimal" caption of "title, author, source, license, and source URL" is recommended; "culturally sensitive content may not be Open Access now or in the future"; a standard notice says the language of a historical record "may be at odds with contemporary views and terminology". https://www.si.edu/openaccess/faq
- The Met: since 7 February 2017, CC0 for images of works "the Museum believes to be in the public domain" (over 492,000), and CC0 for basic metadata of *every* accessioned work including those under copyright; the full dataset is a CSV on GitHub "updated on a weekly basis"; the collection site distinguishes two classes of image, public domain and "known to be under copyright or other restrictions". https://www.metmuseum.org/policies/image-resources and https://www.metmuseum.org/about-the-met/policies-and-documents/open-access

### rightsstatements.org and Europeana

- Twelve statements in three groups: In Copyright (InC, InC-OW-EU, InC-EDU, InC-NC, InC-RUU); No Copyright with a reason reuse is still limited (NoC-CR contractual restrictions, NoC-NC non-commercial only, NoC-OKLR "other known legal restrictions ... such as cultural heritage or traditional cultural expression protections", for which the institution "should provide a link to a page detailing the legal restrictions", and NoC-US); Other (CNE copyright not evaluated, UND undetermined, NKC no known copyright). The NoC statements "should only be used when it is not possible to use the Public Domain Mark or the CC0 Public Domain Dedication"; NoC-US must not be used "for Items where the organization ... has not undertaken an effort to ascertain the copyright status". https://rightsstatements.org/en/statements/
- They are "high level summaries ... intended to be used in addition to (more detailed) rights information"; the URI is stored in `dc:rights` or `edm:rights` and the statement's name and link are displayed "in close proximity to the object"; the URI content-negotiates to a human or machine version; they are not licences. https://rightsstatements.org/en/documentation/
- Europeana accepts 14 statements (six CC licences, CC0, PDM and six from rightsstatements.org); "A rights statement needs to be provided for every digital object"; rights fields must not contradict one another (edm:rights public domain beside a dc:rights "©" is the named example) and should match what the provider's own site says. https://pro.europeana.eu/page/available-rights-statements
- The Europeana Publishing Framework grades content in four tiers by image size (0.1, 0.42, 0.95 megapixels) and rights statement, from "Europeana as a search engine" to "Europeana as a free reuse platform", and metadata in three tiers; "the more you give the more you get". https://pro.europeana.eu/post/publishing-framework and https://pro.europeana.eu/files/Europeana_Professional/Publications/Publishing_Framework/Europeana%20Publishing%20Framework%20V2.0%20English.pdf

## 3. Research methodology for AI-assisted work

### What the measurements say (2024–2026)

- Citation URLs from 10 commercial models and deep-research agents over 53,090 (DRBench) and 168,021 (ExpertQA) URLs: "3–13% of citation URLs are hallucinated, they have no record in the Wayback Machine and likely never existed, while 5–18% are non-resolving overall"; "Deep research agents generate substantially more citations per query than search-augmented LLMs but hallucinate URLs at higher rates"; the non-resolving rate ranges from 5.4% (business) to 11.4% (theology). Models given a URL-liveness tool reduced non-resolving URLs "by 6–79× to under 1%". Rao, Wong and Callison-Burch, April 2026, with the open tool `urlhealth`. https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03173
- Fourteen models in a deep-research setting: links resolve above 94% and are topically relevant above 80%, "yet achieve only 39–77% factual accuracy" when the cited page is actually read against the claim; "Fact Check accuracy drops by approximately 42% on average across two frontier models as tool calls scale from 2 to 150, demonstrating that more retrieval does not produce more accurate citations." Onweller et al., May 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.06635
- "Citation drift": in multi-turn conversations references "mutate, disappear, or get fabricated"; across 240 conversations the worst model fabricated up to 85.6% of citations; proposed metrics are stability, fabrication rate, drift rate, drift entropy and willingness-to-cite. Ram, WASP 2025. https://aclanthology.org/2025.wasp-main.20
- An audit of 111 million references in 2.5 million papers found "a sharp rise in non-existent references following widespread LLM adoption, with a conservative estimate of 146,932 hallucinated citations in 2025 alone"; prior experiments put fabrication in LLM-generated reference lists at "19.9% to 91.4%". https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.07723
- Fabrication is topic-dependent: 28–29% for less-studied mental-health topics against 6% for a well-studied one, and "even among real citations, 45% contain errors such as invalid DOIs" (JMIR Mental Health 2025, as summarised in https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.03173v1 ; original https://mental.jmir.org/2025/1/e80371 ).
- Tow Center, 1,600 queries to eight AI search tools: wrong "to more than 60 percent of queries" (Perplexity 37%, Grok 3 94%); "Most of the tools we tested presented inaccurate answers with alarming confidence"; ChatGPT was wrong on 134 articles but signalled low confidence 15 times and never declined; tools "fabricated links and cited syndicated and copied versions of articles". https://www.cjr.org/tow_center/we-compared-eight-ai-search-engines-theyre-all-bad-at-citing-news.php
- Vendor benchmarks of citation support: DeepResearch Bench's FACT method extracts statement–URL pairs and judges whether the page supports the statement (Perplexity 90.2% citation accuracy with 31 effective citations; Gemini 2.5 Pro 81.4% with 111; OpenAI 78.0% with 41). https://arxiv.org/html/2506.11763v1 . Its successor notes that support checks "do not necessarily confirm that the information is correct (e.g., false information from unofficial sources)". https://arxiv.org/html/2601.08536v3
- Where errors arise in a multi-agent pipeline: "84.7% of final-report errors in AI-Q originate at the orchestrator, roughly 31% of them hallucinations and the rest citation mistakes"; the study filters hallucinated URLs by comparing citations against the trace log of pages actually retrieved. https://arxiv.org/html/2608.24306

### Calibration and abstention

- OpenAI: models hallucinate "because standard training and evaluation procedures reward guessing over acknowledging uncertainty". On SimpleQA, gpt-5-thinking-mini abstained on 52% and erred on 26%; o4-mini abstained on 1% and erred on 75% with slightly higher accuracy. The remedy proposed is scoring that "discourages guessing": a confident error should cost more than an abstention. https://openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/
- SimpleQA Verified (Google): 1,000 prompts for parametric factuality only; "Enabling tools on SimpleQA Verified results in near perfect performance". https://arxiv.org/html/2509.07968v2
- FACTS Grounding (DeepMind): two-phase judging, first whether the response answers the request, then whether it is "fully grounded in the provided document"; the score aggregates several judge models "to mitigate evaluation bias". https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.03200 and https://deepmind.google/discover/blog/facts-grounding-a-new-benchmark-for-evaluating-the-factuality-of-large-language-models . The December 2025 FACTS suite adds a Search track and scores Coverage and No-Contradiction against human-written rubrics. https://arxiv.org/html/2512.10791v1

### Claim extraction and per-claim verification

- SAFE (DeepMind, 2024): "split a long-form response into individual self-contained facts", "determine whether each individual fact is relevant", then for each fact "issue Google Search queries" and reason whether it is supported; agreement with crowd annotators 72%, and in disagreements the agent is right 76% of the time, at a twentieth of the cost; the F1@K metric balances supported facts (precision) against the number of facts a reader would want (recall). https://deepmind.google/research/publications/85420/ and https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.18802
- VeriScore's correction: extraction must separate verifiable from unverifiable sentences, because FActScore and SAFE "assume that every claim is verifiable". https://preview.aclanthology.org/stats/2024.findings-emnlp.552.pdf

### How vendors build and judge research agents

- Anthropic's Research system: a lead agent plans, parallel subagents search, then "a CitationAgent ... processes the documents and research report to identify specific locations for citations. This ensures all claims are properly attributed to their sources." Effort scales with the question ("Simple fact-finding requires just 1 agent with 3–10 tool calls ... complex research might use more than 10 subagents"); agents are told to "start with short, broad queries, evaluate what's available, then progressively narrow". Evaluation is an LLM judge with a rubric of "factual accuracy (do claims match sources?), citation accuracy (do the cited sources match the claims?), completeness ..., source quality (did it use primary sources over lower-quality secondary sources?), and tool efficiency", plus people: "Human evaluation catches what automation misses ... hallucinated answers on unusual queries, system failures, or subtle source selection biases." https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/built-multi-agent-research-system
- Anthropic's Citations API chunks source documents into sentences and returns citations to exact passages; one customer reports "we reduced source hallucinations and formatting issues from 10% to 0%". https://claude.com/resources/articles/introducing-citations-api

### Editorial and research-integrity policy

- Nature (January 2023): "no LLM tool will be accepted as a credited author on a research paper ... any attribution of authorship carries with it accountability for the work, and AI tools cannot take such responsibility"; "researchers using LLM tools should document this use in the methods or acknowledgements sections". https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-023-00191-1 . Current policy: "AI assisted copy editing" need not be declared; generative-AI images are not permitted; reviewers must not upload manuscripts to generative tools. https://www.nature.com/nature/editorial-policies/ai
- Science (January 2023): "Text generated from AI ... cannot be used in papers published in Science journals ... A violation of this policy constitutes scientific misconduct." Reported at https://www.niehs.nih.gov/news/factor/2023/3/feature/2-artificial-intelligence-ethics (the policy was later relaxed to disclosure; not verified here).
- The European Commission's Living guidelines on the responsible use of generative AI in research, fourth edition, October 2026: researchers "remain ultimately responsible"; they "ensure appropriate oversight and verification of AI contributions"; for agents, "researchers identify and ensure relevant check points throughout the process to ensure that critical decisions (for example methodological) are validated by the researchers"; "The use of AI agents may never be considered a delegation of control or responsibility"; they disclose which tools were used substantially ("carrying out a literature review, identifying research gaps" count as substantial) and "describe meaningful information about oversight and verification strategies (such as measures to identify hallucinations, mitigate bias, etc.)"; they consider non-determinism and decide "whether prompts, logs, model versions, parameters and other information should be retained or shared ... to understand, reproduce or audit the research". https://research-and-innovation.ec.europa.eu/document/download/2b6cf7e5-36ac-41cb-aab5-0d32050143dc_en?filename=ec_rtd_ai-guidelines.pdf
- A university research-office version (Glasgow): "The onus is on you, the user, to ensure that the content they produce is accurate and true"; check every reference and data point; acknowledge the tool, version, company, URL, how it was used and the date; "Keep records of the responses output by AI, even if you do not include these in the submission itself"; treat an AI exchange like private correspondence. https://www.gla.ac.uk/research/strategy/ourpolicies/ai-for-researchers
- A working citation-verification checklist (Editage): search the exact title in quotation marks in two databases; match author spelling and order, year, journal; resolve the DOI through Crossref; "Confirm the source supports the claim" by reading the cited section; check quotations word for word; check "No two sources have been merged into one reference". https://www.editage.com/blog/how-to-verify-ai-generated-citations-references-steps-checklist-examples/
- The Alan Turing Institute on LLM judges: "using LLMs as judges requires great care, and human oversight remains essential. Periodic human audits can identify drift or systematic biases that an automated judge might miss"; trusting an LLM judge needs "meta-audits: statistical frameworks that quantify an LLM's reliability as an evaluator". https://www.turing.ac.uk/blog/making-generative-ai-trustworthy-and-reliable-adoption-scale . David Leslie's framing: generative systems as "computational instruments embedded in warm-blooded research processes". https://www.turing.ac.uk/news/publications/does-sun-rise-chatgpt-scientific-discovery-age-generative-ai
- JISC: its AI-in-research community stresses human oversight and the skill of knowing "how to question their outputs, evaluate reliability, and integrate them ethically"; a formal JISC verification protocol for AI-assisted research was not found. https://nationalcentreforai.jiscinvolve.org/wp/2025/10/29/ai-in-research-newsletter-autumn-2025
- Published red-teaming guidance specific to historical or reference content: not found. The closest are the factuality benchmarks above and Anthropic's note that human testers find "edge cases that evals miss".

## 4. Content and learning-design processes

### Backward design and constructive alignment

- Wiggins and McTighe's three stages: identify desired results; determine acceptable evidence ("How will I know if students have achieved the desired results?"); plan learning experiences and instruction. "Enduring understandings" are what a student should keep "sometime after they've completed the course"; assessments that do not match goals are the named failure. Wiggins, G. and McTighe, J. (1998), *Understanding by Design*, ASCD, pp. 13–34, as summarised at https://ocw.utoronto.ca/backward-design/ (the original ASCD excerpt at https://twut.nd.edu/PDF/backward-design.pdf could not be fetched; the "twin sins" of activity- and coverage-focused design are therefore cited from memory of the book and not verified here).
- Biggs's constructive alignment: teaching activities and assessment tasks are designed to address the intended learning outcomes; Biggs, J. (1996) "Enhancing teaching through constructive alignment", *Higher Education* 32, 347–364, https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00138871 , as summarised at https://competencebasededucation.hull.ac.uk/?p=35792

### Cognitive load, worked examples, dual coding, retrieval practice

- Element interactivity is the variable that decides whether a worked example helps or hinders: the expertise-reversal effect "is a variant of the more general element interactivity effect". Chen, Kalyuga and Sweller (2017), *Educational Psychology Review* 29, 393–405. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-016-9359-1
- Mayer and Moreno's principles from dual-channel theory (a visual and a verbal channel, each of limited capacity): multimedia (words with pictures beat words alone); temporal contiguity (present corresponding words and pictures together); coherence ("exclude extraneous words, sounds, and pictures"); modality (narration beats on-screen text beside animation); redundancy (animation plus narration beats animation plus narration plus the same text on screen). Mayer, R. E. and Moreno, R. (2002), *Learning and Instruction* 12, 107–119. https://www.sfu.ca/~jcnesbit/EDUC220/ThinkPaper/MayerMoreno2002.pdf
- Retrieval practice: of ten study techniques reviewed, practice testing and distributed practice "made the grade" with the highest utility; summarising, highlighting and rereading were rated low. Dunlosky et al. (2013), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1). https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html

### Universal Design for Learning 3.0

- Released 30 July 2024: three principles (Engagement, the "why"; Representation, the "what"; Action & Expression, the "how"), nine guidelines (Perception; Language & Symbols; Building Knowledge; Interaction; Expression & Communication; Strategy Development; Welcoming Interests & Identities; Sustaining Effort & Persistence; Emotional Capacity) and 36 considerations, e.g. 1.2 "Support multiple ways to perceive information", 2.1 "Clarify vocabulary, symbols, and language structures", 2.5 "Illustrate through multiple media", 3.2 "Highlight and explore patterns, critical features, big ideas, and relationships"; the stated goal is "learner agency". https://udlguidelines.cast.org/

### Plain language and readability

- GOV.UK: "we tell people to write on GOV.UK for a 9 year old reading age"; "By using common words we can help all users understand sentences of around 25 words"; "users only read about 20 to 28% of a web page"; "put the most important information first"; "FAQs are strongly discouraged"; "Plain English is mandatory"; technical terms are allowed for expert audiences but "You just need to explain what they mean the first time you use them"; headings start at H2 and never skip a level; active voice. Archived May 2022 text at https://govdiff.njk.onl/update/2022-05-05T11:26:00+01:00/www.gov.uk/guidance/content-design/writing-for-gov-uk ; the live guidance now lives at https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/ (split into pages on user needs, structure, clear language, tone, links, titles, summaries and change notes).
- Hemingway Editor's default target is "a grade 9 reading level, which is the average for adults in the United States", with Accessible and Technical alternatives; it warns that "highlights and scores are only a guide" and that "writers like Ernest Hemingway produced novels for adults that score at a 5th-grade reading level". https://hemingwayapp.com/help/docs/readability

### Museum label writing

- V&A, *Gallery Text at the V&A: A Ten Point Guide* (2013): 1 write for your audience (educated non-specialists; adjust tone, do not write down); 2 stick to the text hierarchy and word count: introductory panels 150–180 words (up to 220), section panels 130–150, object labels 50–60 plus caption, group labels 70, long captions 80–100; 3 organise: panels carry big ideas, labels tie ideas to objects, in a topic–theme–message order; 4 engage with the object: write after looking, and make sure "the text matches what is actually visible"; 5 "Admit uncertainty. Be open about what is not known. Ask questions only when they are genuine"; 6 bring in the human element; 7 sketch in the background; 8 write as you would speak; 9 one idea per sentence, one subject per paragraph, a first sentence under 16 words; 10 Orwell's rules, active voice, explain specialist terms, read aloud. Unexplained specialist words are where visitors stop reading. No reading-age number is given. https://www.bu.edu/haa/files/2018/12/Gallery-Text-at-the-V-and-A-Ten-Point-Guide-Aug-2013.pdf (also at https://thegardenstrust.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/VA_Gallery-Text-Writing-Guidelines_online_Web.pdf )
- Smithsonian Exhibits, *Guide to Exhibit Development*: intro text 150 words maximum; primary and secondary section text 100 maximum each; sidebars 50–100; object labels 50 maximum; captions 25–35; quotes one or two sentences; "people are more likely to read short paragraphs, 25 to 75 words long"; find the right slice ("Exhibits fail when they try to do too much"); make it relevant; "concrete nouns and strong active verbs"; "Let each text stand on its own"; one idea per sentence; "Get a fresh perspective: have someone unfamiliar with the subject act as a test visitor"; read aloud and time it. Mirror copy at https://moehc.moe.edu.sg/files/Guide-to-Exhibit-Development.pdf (the original is published by exhibits.si.edu).
- AAM's *Exhibition* journal (Bartlett 2014): text blocks of 50–75 words and 150–200 words in total (citing Serrell 1996, Dean, Spencer); Bitgood 2014: visitors are more likely to read text broken into segments "of no more than 75 words"; a Flesch–Kincaid target of grade 9–10, with the caveat that scores give "a general sense of a passage's difficulty in comparison to others"; one focus per label (McManus 1989); peer review of drafts between teams. https://www.aam-us.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/10Wellchosenwords_Bartlettexhfall2014-9.pdf
- Museums & Galleries NSW: artwork labels 70–80 words, introductory panels 100–200; type 18–24 pt; "A standing read time of 10 seconds is also the average time given by audiences to any one label"; "once you have written it, test it to know the most important information is included". https://mgnsw.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Exhibition-labelling.pdf

## 5. Quality assurance for content pipelines

### The style guide as single source of truth, enforced by a linter

- Prose linters (Vale, proselint, textlint) run a style guide as code: a `styles/` folder of rule packages (Google and Microsoft style guides ship as packages), a `.vale.ini`, and `vale .` in CI. What they catch is "redundancy, jargon, illogic, clichés, sexism, misspelling, inconsistency, misuse of symbols, malapropisms, oxymorons, security gaffes, hedging, apologizing, pretension"; "a consistent writing style also improves the reading experience". https://passo.uno/posts/first-steps-with-the-vale-prose-linter/
- GOV.UK's review checklist (DfE's published version): subheadings every 3–5 paragraphs; paragraphs under 8 lines; sentences under 25 words; images have alt text; "visual information is also available as text"; videos captioned; link text describes the destination; plain English with technical terms explained; the second-pair-of-eyes check is "the final check" on the final version in context. https://design.education.gov.uk/design-system/reviewing-and-checking-content

### Editorial review and sign-off

- GOV.UK "2i" (second pair of eyes): "2i sees every piece of content before it gets published"; "Only one person will ever 2i a piece of content at any one time"; the reviewer checks Scope (does the change match the request), Sense (does the whole still make sense, including knock-on effects, "the butterfly effect of changing content"), Style (so everything "looks and feels like it's written by one person") and Fit (does it duplicate or contradict other content); small fixes are made directly with notes, large ones go back; a piece "may need to be sent to another government department or subject matter expert for a fact check". https://insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk/2013/07/31/editorial-reviews-and-mainstream-content/
- The publishing tool makes this a workflow: preview in context; an internal note under the History tab saying "who requested the change and why"; "Submit for 2nd eyes"; the reviewer publishes or rejects, and rejected content "goes back to draft status"; a separate Fact checking tab sends a preview link to a named outside checker, who comments. https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/publish-update-retire-content/standard-content-types/send-standard-review/
- The reference publishers above add: a fact-checker stage with a pass/fail gate before supervisory review (Britannica); peer review of every new and revised article (Grove); two to three named reviewers per essay (Smarthistory); external specialist peer review plus three research editors (ODNB); and a module team acting as "critical readers" of one another's drafts (OU).

### Versioning, freeze and release, change logs

- Keep a Changelog: "Changelogs are for humans, not machines"; "There should be an entry for every single version"; "The same types of changes should be grouped"; "Versions and sections should be linkable"; "The latest version comes first"; "The release date of each version is displayed"; change types Added, Changed, Deprecated, Removed, Fixed, Security; an "Unreleased" section at the top; commit-log diffs are "a bad idea: they're full of noise"; dates in ISO 8601. https://keepachangelog.com/en/1.1.0/
- A documentation string freeze is "the point at which we cease creating and modifying strings in Documentation"; exceptions "may be made before release" only for "major glaring typos" or "exceptional circumstances", must be discussed and approved on the list, and must be communicated to translators; after release, fixes follow the stable-release-update procedure. https://wiki.ubuntu.com/DocumentationStringFreeze
- Reader-facing revision history as practised: the Met's "originally published / last revised" stamp; Grove's archived earlier version "side by side" and "updated by" credit line; Britannica's Article History table; OWID's `dataset.changelog` and per-origin `date_accessed`; Wikipedia's "stable" criterion for featured status (all cited in sections 1 and 2).

### Reader testing

- Thinking aloud: "ask test participants to use the system while continuously thinking out loud"; three steps: "Recruit representative users. Give them representative tasks to perform. Shut up and let the users do the talking"; it is cheap, robust even when poorly run, flexible at any stage, and "a handful of users" in about a day gives "the most important insights", above all the users' misconceptions. Nielsen, 2012. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/thinking-aloud-the-1-usability-tool/
- Five-second test: a design is shown for five seconds and removed; participants say what they recalled "most easily or vividly"; it measures "what stands out most about a design or product"; its value "can be compromised when its restrictions are ignored". Doncaster, *The UX Five-Second Rules* (Morgan Kaufmann, 2014). https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/the-ux-five-second/9780128005347/
- The museum equivalents: a "test visitor" unfamiliar with the subject (Smithsonian) and "test it to know the most important information is included" (MGNSW), cited in section 4.

### Accessibility audits

- WCAG-EM, the W3C evaluation methodology, in five steps: define the scope and target level (A, AA, AAA); explore the site for key views, functionality, content types and technologies; select a representative sample (structured plus random); evaluate the sample, recording pass or fail per success criterion; report aggregated findings, evaluation statements and scores, with a report tool and template. https://www.w3.org/WAI/eval/conformance ; WCAG-EM 2.0 Group Note, 23 July 2026, https://www.w3.org/TR/wcag-em-2

## What this project does not do yet

Measured against the practices above and the project's own `claims.md`, `method.md`, `protocol.md` and `checklist.md`:

1. **No human has reviewed anything.** `claims.md` §4 names a writer, a specialist and an editor and says "the specialist signs calendars and names; the editor signs the register", but every role has so far been played by an agent. Every publisher above has at least one named human gate before publication (Britannica's fact-checker pass/fail, Grove's peer review of every article, Smarthistory's two to three PhD reviewers, GOV.UK's 2i, Khan Academy's "human review before it reaches learners"); the EU guidelines say agent use "may never be considered a delegation of control or responsibility".
2. **No spot-check of sources against claims, and no evidence a cited page was ever opened.** The register records `sources`, but there is no record of the date each source was read, no archived copy or Wayback snapshot, no liveness check of URLs, and no per-claim "does the cited passage actually say this" pass. The 2026 measurements show link validity above 94% beside factual support of 39–77%, and 3–13% of agent-cited URLs that never existed; FAC spot-checks and OWID's `date_accessed` are the answers in use.
3. **No reliability grading of sources, and no independence test.** The register treats a canon row, a UNESCO file, a museum page and a teaching page alike. Wikipedia asks "What makes this source reliable?" per source and judges work, author and publisher separately; OWID prefers institutions that maintain data and values sources that disagree; Wikidata refuses a second reference "explicitly based on the same source" and strips aggregator references; the Tow Center found agents citing syndicated copies as if they were independent. Triangulation here means two different URLs, not two independent witnesses.
4. **No disclosure of AI use or of the verification strategy on the page.** Nature requires documenting LLM use; the EU guidelines ask for "meaningful information about oversight and verification strategies (such as measures to identify hallucinations)"; Khan Academy publishes a page on how its team uses LLMs. F1's colophon says who wrote a file, not what was and was not checked.
5. **No audit trail of the research run.** Prompts, model versions, the pages fetched and the pages rejected are not kept alongside `research.md`; the EU guidelines and Glasgow's guidance both ask for retained logs where they are needed "to understand, reproduce or audit the research"; the "Who is the Agent to Blame" study could only detect hallucinated URLs because it had the trace log of what was actually retrieved.
6. **No calibration measurement.** The confidence words are assigned by the agent that wrote the register, and nothing yet measures how often "documented" survives a human read. OpenAI's abstention-versus-error accounting and the FACTS Coverage/No-Contradiction scores are the models; the project's grading script scores design conformance, not factual precision.
7. **No reader-facing revision history, change log or corrections route.** There is a freeze tag (`claims-v1`) but no Keep-a-Changelog file, no "originally published / last revised" stamp on pages, no archived earlier text with an "updated by" credit, and the corrections inbox is still a pending decision (DECISIONS_2026-10-03 §4). Grove publishes monthly counts of emendations; Britannica publishes every edit.
8. **No freeze-exception policy.** The register freezes, but nothing says what may change after the freeze, who approves it and who is told (Ubuntu's string freeze: typos and exceptional circumstances only, approved on the list, translators notified).
9. **No learning objectives and no alignment check.** The unit specs carry a thesis and a threshold concept, but the skill files contain no intended learning outcomes per unit, no "acceptable evidence" stage, and no check that a page's prompts and prediction cards test what the unit claims to teach (Wiggins and McTighe; Biggs; the OU's alignment "with the module learning outcomes").
10. **No readability target or sentence-length rule for the reading.** Lengths exist for beats, captions and quotations (≤ 25 words) and for readings (150–400 words), but no grade level, no 25-word sentence check, no "explain each specialist term on first use" lint (GOV.UK; V&A; Hemingway grade 9).
11. **No reader testing of any kind.** Beats are to be "read cold, out of order, before it ships", but by an agent; no think-aloud, five-second test or "test visitor" has been run, and no learner has seen a page.
12. **No accessibility audit.** The checklist covers keyboard reach, reduced motion and 4.5:1 contrast, but not alt text, captions for video, heading levels, or a WCAG-EM scoped sample with pass/fail per success criterion.
13. **Rights are a project category, not a standard statement.** Objects are "open-licence" by a project rule (`atlas/DATA_LICENCES.md`) rather than carrying the licence URI (CC0, PDM, CC BY) or a rightsstatements.org URI per object, and object captions do not follow the Smithsonian minimal form of title, author, source, licence and source URL.
14. **Period, place and grouping definitions are not linked to outside authorities.** The "as defined by [who, when]" card follows PeriodO's logic but does not record the source's own wording for spatial and temporal coverage separately from the standardised value, does not carry a locator (page or figure), and does not link groupings, periods or places to PeriodO, Wikidata or TGN identifiers (the atlas has a Wikidata matcher for the canon, but the page-level data model does not expose it). Place uncertainty is a "soft blot" without the WHG's precision figure or certainty note.
15. **The sensitive-content protocol is the project's own vocabulary.** Level 1 "Notice" is not the Local Contexts Notice (whose text "cannot be changed" and which invites community Labels), there is no "Attribution Incomplete" or "Open to Collaborate" statement, and CARE's E3 ("Metadata should acknowledge the provenance and purpose and any limitations or obligations in secondary use inclusive of issues of consent") has no field in the object record.
16. **No "what may still be in dispute" sentence as a required element of a page.** Argument cards exist for named arguments, but Britannica's rule that every article makes "clear not only what is known about a topic but also what may still be in dispute" and the V&A's "Admit uncertainty" are not a required line of the reading itself.
17. **Not yet in place, but partly answered by existing design:** the Getty's three-sources rule for a preferred term and one for any other (the register requires sources but not a count); Wikidata's distinction between a deprecated value and a historical one (the `alternates` list holds both without a flag); FAIR's persistent identifiers and "metadata accessible even when the data are no longer available" (record ids are stable once frozen, but there is no identifier scheme beyond the repo).

## Fifteen practices to adopt, in priority order

Each entry: the practice, where it comes from, and what it would change here.

1. **A human gate before any page ships: a named reviewer, a pass/fail fact-check stage and a signed register.** From Britannica's fact-checker gate (https://elearn.eb.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/Editorial-Infographic_Flyer.pdf ), GOV.UK's 2i (https://insidegovuk.blog.gov.uk/2013/07/31/editorial-reviews-and-mainstream-content/ ) and the EU guidelines' check points (https://research-and-innovation.ec.europa.eu/document/download/2b6cf7e5-36ac-41cb-aab5-0d32050143dc_en?filename=ec_rtd_ai-guidelines.pdf ). Change: `claims.md` §4's "the editor signs the register" becomes a real signature by a named person with a date in the file header; a unit's status is "unreviewed" until then and the page says so in its colophon; the grading script refuses to award a pass above "draft" without the signature line.
2. **Spot-check sources against claims before freeze, and record the check.** From FAC source reviewing (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Guidance_on_source_reviewing_at_FAC ) and the FACT / Fact Check methods (https://arxiv.org/html/2506.11763v1 , https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.06635 ). Change: a second agent, then a human, opens a random 20% of records (all records marked documented that drive a tick) and answers three questions per record: link works, page is about this, page says this value; results go in a `spotcheck` field (`{by, date, result}`); a unit with any failed documented record cannot freeze.
3. **Date-of-access, archived copy and liveness check for every source URL.** From OWID's `origin.date_accessed` and snapshot layer (https://docs.owid.io/projects/etl/architecture/metadata/reference/ ), Wikidata's `retrieved` on web references (https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Help:Sources ) and the `urlhealth` tool (https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03173 ). Change: `sources.yaml` gains `date_accessed`, `archive_url` (a Wayback save made at access) and a `last_checked` written by a scheduled liveness run; the lint fails a source with no access date and warns on a dead link; a dead source with no archive demotes its claims to probable until re-read.
4. **Grade every source and test independence before calling a claim triangulated.** From Wikipedia's work / author / publisher test and "What makes this source reliable?" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Verifiability ), OWID's metric criteria (https://ourworldindata.org/choosing-our-topics-and-metrics ) and Wikidata's rule against a second reference from the same source (https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Help:Sources ). Change: each source record carries `kind` (holder record, peer-reviewed, reference work, institutional report, press, teaching page, community statement, aggregator), `checks_facts: yes/no/unknown` and `derived_from` (so a Smarthistory essay citing a Met page is not a second witness); "documented" requires one source that checks facts, or two independent ones; the dossier's "triangulated" column counts independent witnesses, not URLs.
5. **A reader-facing revision history and a change log, with a corrections route.** From the Met's "originally published / last revised" (https://www.metmuseum.org/toah/hd/teot2/hd_teot2.htm ), Grove's archived versions and "updated by" credit (https://www.oxfordartonline.com/page/grove-editorial-and-art-image-policies/grove-art-editorial-and-image-policies ), Britannica's Article History (https://www.britannica.com/topic/Encyclopaedia-Britannica-general-encyclopaedia/additional-info ) and Keep a Changelog (https://keepachangelog.com/en/1.1.0/ ). Change: a `CHANGELOG.md` per unit (Added / Changed / Fixed / Deprecated, ISO dates, Unreleased at top); every page's colophon shows "first published, last revised, reviewed by"; a corrected claim keeps its old value as an alternate with `note: "superseded on <date>, reason"`; the monitored inbox (decision 4) is set up and its address printed on every page.
6. **An AI-use and verification statement on every page and in every dossier.** From Nature's policy (https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-023-00191-1 ), the EU guidelines' "oversight and verification strategies" and Khan Academy's public LLM page (https://support.khanacademy.org/hc/en-us/articles/14004618952717-How-does-the-Khan-Academy-content-team-use-large-language-models ). Change: a fixed colophon block: which agent and model drafted, which checks ran (lint codes, spot-check sample size and result), what a human reviewed and what no human has yet read; the same block in `research.md`.
7. **Keep the research trace as an audit record.** From the EU guidelines ("prompts, logs, model versions, parameters ... to understand, reproduce or audit"), Glasgow ("Keep records of the responses output by AI"; https://www.gla.ac.uk/research/strategy/ourpolicies/ai-for-researchers ) and the trace-log method that catches hallucinated URLs (https://arxiv.org/html/2608.24306 ). Change: each research run writes `trace/<run-id>.jsonl` listing every URL fetched, the status code, a content hash, and whether it was used or rejected and why; a claim may cite only a URL present in the trace; the lint cross-checks `sources` against the trace.
8. **Measure calibration: score abstentions and confident errors separately, and audit the judge.** From OpenAI's accounting (https://openai.com/index/why-language-models-hallucinate/ ), FACTS Grounding's two-phase, multi-judge scoring (https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.03200 ), SAFE's per-fact search verification (https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.18802 ) and the Turing Institute's call for human meta-audits of LLM judges (https://www.turing.ac.uk/blog/making-generative-ai-trustworthy-and-reliable-adoption-scale ). Change: after each human spot-check, the project records per confidence word how many records held, were downgraded or were wrong; the grade script adds a "factual precision" line that counts a wrong documented record as worse than a gap; the register template allows `confidence: unknown` so an agent can abstain instead of guessing; a quarterly human read of a sample of the grader's own verdicts.
9. **Learning outcomes per unit and an alignment table.** From Wiggins and McTighe (https://ocw.utoronto.ca/backward-design/ ), Biggs (https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00138871 ) and the OU's "aligned with the module learning outcomes" (https://university.open.ac.uk/stem/life-health-chemical-sciences/about-us/our-teaching-model ). Change: each unit spec gains three to five intended learning outcomes with a verb, and a table mapping each outcome to the evidence (which prompt, prediction card or argument card shows it) and to the stations that teach it; a page with a prompt that maps to no outcome, or an outcome with no evidence, fails the checklist.
10. **A readability and specialist-term rule in the lint.** From GOV.UK (9-year-old reading age for public pages, sentences around 25 words, explain a technical term on first use; https://govdiff.njk.onl/update/2022-05-05T11:26:00+01:00/www.gov.uk/guidance/content-design/writing-for-gov-uk ), the V&A's first sentence under 16 words and "Admit uncertainty" (https://www.bu.edu/haa/files/2018/12/Gallery-Text-at-the-V-and-A-Ten-Point-Guide-Aug-2013.pdf ) and Hemingway's grade-9 default (https://hemingwayapp.com/help/docs/readability ). Change: a Vale-style prose lint (https://passo.uno/posts/first-steps-with-the-vale-prose-linter/ ) with the project's rules as a style package ("first" → "earliest known", confidence words present, quotes ≤ 25 words, sentences > 25 words flagged, a glossary term used before its definition flagged, Flesch–Kincaid reported per reading with a target of grade 9–11 for readings and lower for captions); the checklist's type and length rules move into it.
11. **Reader testing before G3: think-aloud with five learners and a five-second test per page type.** From Nielsen (https://www.nngroup.com/articles/thinking-aloud-the-1-usability-tool/ ), Doncaster (https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/the-ux-five-second/9780128005347/ ) and the Smithsonian's "test visitor" (https://moehc.moe.edu.sg/files/Guide-to-Exhibit-Development.pdf ). Change: a `research_notes/Reader tests/` folder with a one-page protocol (recruit, tasks, "shut up"), five sessions on the F1.13 build, and a findings log whose items become checklist lines; a five-second test on each of the seven page types asking "what was this page about and what was the date".
12. **Standard rights statements and a minimal caption on every object.** From rightsstatements.org (https://rightsstatements.org/en/statements/ , https://rightsstatements.org/en/documentation/ ), Europeana's mandatory `edm:rights` and no-contradiction rule (https://pro.europeana.eu/page/available-rights-statements ) and the Smithsonian's "title, author, source, license, and source URL" (https://www.si.edu/openaccess/faq ). Change: every object record carries `rights_uri` (a CC, PDM or rightsstatements.org URI as the holder states it), `rights_checked` (date and by whom), and the lint refuses an object whose project licence class contradicts its holder's statement; the caption renders the five Smithsonian fields in that order; a link-out (level 4) object carries the holder's statement too.
13. **Record definitions as the source gives them, with locators and outside identifiers.** From PeriodO (verbatim label, spatial description and coverage area, start/stop labels and standardised years, locator, editorial note; https://perio.do/guide/ ), Linked Places (`closeMatch` to Wikidata and TGN, `certainty` on `when` and geometry; https://github.com/LinkedPasts/linked-places-format ) and WHG's certainty note and precision (https://docs.whgazetteer.org/content/v4/user-guide/getting-started/concepts.html ). Change: the grouping and period cards' "as defined by" gains `label_original`, `coverage_description` (verbatim), `coverage_area` (gazetteer ids), `start_label` / `stop_label` beside the standardised `value`, `locator`, and `same_as` (PeriodO, Wikidata, TGN); places on scenes gain `precision_km` and `certainty_note`; the soft-blot rule draws from these fields.
14. **Local Contexts Notices and a CARE provenance field for Indigenous and community material.** From Local Contexts (https://localcontexts.org/notices/ , https://localcontexts.org/labels/traditional-knowledge-labels/ ), CARE E3 (https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5d3799de845604000199cd24/t/5da9f4479ecab221ce848fb2/1571419335217/CARE%2BPrinciples_One%2BPagers%2BFINAL_Oct_17_2019.pdf ) and the Smithsonian's "culturally sensitive content may not be Open Access now or in the future". Change: protocol level 1 displays the unaltered Local Contexts TK Notice or Attribution Incomplete Notice (generated through the Hub) rather than the project's own wording, with the Open to Collaborate Notice on the course's about page; object records for such material gain `consent` (who, what, when, as `protocol.md` already asks) and `secondary_use_obligations` text; the course never writes a Label of its own.
15. **A freeze-exception rule and numbered updates.** From the Ubuntu documentation string freeze (https://wiki.ubuntu.com/DocumentationStringFreeze ), the ODNB's numbered monthly updates with editorial introductions (https://www.oxforddnb.com/news ) and Grove's monthly counts of new, revised and emended articles (https://oxfordartonline.com/page/news ). Change: after `claims-v1`, only a typo or an error found by review may change a record, each approved by the editor and logged with reason; everything else waits for `claims-v2`; each re-freeze ships as a numbered update whose note lists records added, revised and emended, and the page colophons point to it.
