# F1 "Histories of making": a critique of the learner-facing content

Read 20 October 2026, first as a first-time learner and then as an educator and editor. What was read: the Edo unit's script (`build/units/edo/content/story.yaml`, 71 beats), its cast (`content/people.yaml`), the Edo full case (`research/edo-2026-10-04/EDO_FULL_CASE.md`), the built Edo page at desktop and phone widths (served from `build/units/edo/dist`, screenshots of beats p-1, b1-4, b3-4, b4-3, b5-2, b9-4, b2-4, b4-5); the F1.13 lecture (`build/lectures/src/lectures/f1-13.json`, nine stations, narration and captions, plus the station renders in `build/lectures/F1.13/stations`); the F1.13 scene sheet (`build/units/f1-13-method/text/beats.yaml`, `lectures.yaml`, `lint.md`) and its three built pages (`text`, `lane-chart`, `grouping-indian-ocean`, `shot-1440.png` and `shot-390.png`); the Indian Ocean grouping draft (`grouping-pages/indian_ocean.md`); the ballet brief (`research_notes/Ballet/BRIEF.md`); and two unit specs (`specs/F1.13.md`, `docs/10-unit-specs-batch-1/02-f1-1-three-readings.md`). Reading statistics were computed from the YAML and JSON with a script; the readability figures are Flesch and Flesch-Kincaid on the learner-facing strings only.

## Verdict

The Edo unit is the best thing in the module and is close to a shippable learner experience: it has a thesis a learner can repeat ("a print was never one hand; the law named the designer and the publisher, never the carver or the printer"), it is written in short, concrete, dated sentences at about grade 7–8 reading level, it carries nine guesses, four argued pairs and five act-end rebuilds that are real retrieval practice, and the built page reads well at 1440 and tolerably at 390. Everything else in the set is apparatus wearing the clothes of content. The F1.13 lecture narration, the scene sheet, the Indian Ocean draft and the specs are written for the project (claim ids, register codes, "we write", "drawn after the guess", "as defined by", "held until a reader clears") and a first-time learner cannot tell what they are supposed to learn, in what order, or how they would know they had learned it. No surface states a learning objective; none offers feedback beyond "the answer appears on the map"; none closes with anything the learner makes. The evidence discipline (confidence words, claim ids, two readings) is the module's real intellectual contribution and should be kept, but its current display obstructs reading: the F1.13 narration has a bracketed claim number on 87 sentences, the scene-sheet page prints `claims · f13-e-jar · F1.13-C01 · F1.13-C02 · sources · F1.13-C01 · F1.13-C02` under a 42-word paragraph, and the grouping page opens with a record string and a canon code before its first sentence of history. The fix is not to loosen the rigour but to move it one layer down, behind a tap, and to put objectives, a stated thesis, worked examples, spaced retrieval and a closing task on top.

## Findings by criterion

### 1. Learning objectives: unstated and mostly unassessable

- The Edo unit has an essential question (`story.yaml` line 10: "How did a city turn pictures into a mass market, and who got named when it did?") and a stated plan (p-2: "You will follow five publishers' bets, one to an act, and one sheet of paper from Echizen to New York"). That is a promise of content, not of capability. Nowhere does the learner read "by the end you will be able to date a print from its margin seals", although the unit teaches exactly that (b5-5, b6-4).
- `EDO_FULL_CASE.md` §5 gives each lecture a *thesis* ("the market came before the pictures"; "full colour was invented by amateurs for a party") but no objective. The grep for "objective", "able to", "success criteria" across the Edo case, teaching notes and README returns nothing learner-facing.
- The specs do have **success criteria** (`specs/F1.13.md` §8: "The learner's trace separates four flows for one object; every edge has a source or 'not recorded'"; `02-f1-1-three-readings.md` §8: "The learner writes one sentence per reading about their own object, and tags each one seen, recorded or guessed"). These are good, assessable objectives. They are buried in section 8 of an internal spec and never reach a page.
- The F1.13 lecture has a stated thesis in `lectures.yaml` ("a network is objects, materials, skills and people at once: they share routes, but not terms") but station 1 delivers it as an aside between the jar's accession number and a tap, and the closing (station 9) turns it into a rhetorical question ("Which flow does your own work's material travel on, and on what terms?") with no place to answer.

### 2. Narrative and argument: Edo has a thesis; F1.13 has a taxonomy

- Edo's argument is explicit, recurs, and is paid off: b1-6 states the four trades; b2-5 states who the law named; b4-2 and b4-5 show the printer's unnamed decision in the ink; b8-5 pays it off ("A century of nameless carvers and printers got names only when the state sent the trade abroad"); b9-4 restates it in two sentences. A learner could repeat it. Each act also has a one-line "bet" (`a1.bet`, `a2.bet` ...) that works as a chapter thesis. This is the model.
- F1.13's thesis is a classification ("four flows, shared routes, not shared terms"). It is true and useful, but a learner who has watched eleven minutes could repeat "four flows" and not say *why it matters* or what it changes about how they read an object. The one memorable move, "people are never goods; they get their own mark, never an arrow", is a drawing rule stated as narration ("Look for the people mark beside the silver arc, never on it", s6) rather than an argument the learner is asked to apply.
- The nine stations of F1.13 are nine regions, not nine steps of an argument. Station order is geographic (Iran, Swahili coast, Central Asia, Sahara, Pacific, Japan, Atlantic, Gulf). The scene sheet admits the join is loose: `beats.yaml` b-blue-7's "bridge" leaps from station 2 to station 7 and the `honesty` note says the editor "may swap `ends_on` if the stations are reordered".
- The Indian Ocean draft has a good standfirst ("A wind that reverses twice a year let printers in Gujarat, potters in Zhejiang and builders at Kilwa work for buyers they never met") and then never argues it; the walk is nine unconnected steps and the thesis sentence does not recur.
- The ballet brief is the clearest piece of argument in the set: a thesis in one paragraph, seven claims "worth teaching", three candidate spines with risks, and a recommendation. It is written for the designer, not the learner, but it is the right shape.

### 3. Cognitive load and sequencing

- Edo: the reader always knows where they are. The top bar shows P–V, the year in two calendars, the cast count; each beat has a year and an era name; the act "kicker" names the publisher. Beats are 40–100 words with no sentence over 25 (enforced by `scripts/build-content.mjs`). The overture (nine shots, one sentence each) is a genuine advance organiser.
- Edo problems: (a) **date order is not reading order**. Act I runs 1635 → 1657 → 1660 → 1700 → 1721 → 1672 → 1748 → 1700 → 1765 (b1-1 to b1-7). A first-time reader following the timeline cursor sees it jump back fifty years twice. The reason (thematic grouping) is never said. (b) **Act I is 15 beats before the first publisher appears** (b2-1, Tsutaya), and the act is titled "The quarter's publisher". (c) At phone width the stage occupies the top half and the beat text arrives after a ~350 px blank (m-b4-5.png); the first screen of each beat is mostly timeline. (d) The closing timeline (d-b9-4.png) compresses 1870–2024 to one-fifth scale with act labels overlapping ("III · The ba", "I'", "V").
- F1.13 lecture: six of nine stations open with a content note (s3–s8), each in the same words. By station 5 the note is noise; by station 8, where it matters most, it has been trained out. The station renders (`06-s6.png`) show timeline labels overprinting each other ("Baghdad paper mill, 794–95" over "Catalan Atlas, 1375" over "Xuande wares tested") because every prior station's marks stay on the strip.
- The scene-sheet page (`text/shot-1440.png`) puts a schematic map, a placeholder object frame, a lane strip, a pull-quote, and the beat column on one screen, with monospace build notes ("STAGE · PLACEHOLDER · STATION 1 STATE", "the holder's photograph, placed at build") in the same visual weight as the content. A learner cannot tell apparatus from lesson.
- The lane chart page opens with a paragraph of build rationale ("The Edo chart's lanes for the state, the makers and the afterlife are replaced by the materials, the skills and the people...") before the chart. This is a commit message, not a caption.

### 4. Voice and register: Edo speaks to a reader; everything else speaks to the project

- Edo, good: "Nobody knows who Sharaku was." (b2-6) "The golden age ended in handcuffs twice." (b2-8) "The trade moved at the speed of news." (b6-2). Direct address is used sparingly and well: "Look at the hollow under the claw" (b4-0), "Watch the road draw itself" (b4-8).
- Edo, slips: parenthetical confidence words inside sentences, ten times, e.g. b1-7 "sold the sheets as *nishiki-e*, brocade pictures (probable)"; b6-6 "a decree cancelled the debts of the Yoshiwara's women (probable)"; b4-4 "an inference from wear (argued)". A reader does not know whether "(probable)" qualifies the fact, the date, or the name. Also b7-5 "The museum's licence is non-commercial, so they are linked, not shown" and b9-3 "The note's image is not openly licensed, so it is described here, not shown": rights talk in the lesson.
- F1.13 narration, project voice throughout: "We write 'from Iran'" (s1); "We link to that record and show no image of it" (s3); "We write them as people, in words; the translation's own terms stay in the Deep tier" (s3); "The summary names no weaver" (s4); "We hold both" (s5); "we call that probable" (s6); "so our verb is 'taken'" (s7); "We draw no diagram" (s8); "their words and names stay theirs until their cooperative has been asked" (s9). Nine of nine stations contain editorial policy spoken aloud. A learner hears the editor arguing with the sources rather than being taught.
- Scene sheet: b-blue-2 "On the stage, the jar with blue underglaze decoration that the Cleveland Museum of Art holds as 1962.154"; b-gal-5 the same formula. The accession number is in the learner's sentence; the museum is the subject of the clause. The Met's own essay (see §12) never does this.
- Indian Ocean draft, opening lines of the card: "Network: a set of sea routes, ports and merchant communities, not a state or a people (NET005, the umbrella row; sub-circuits NET006 Arabian Sea, NET007 Bay of Bengal, NET008 South China Sea)". The built page (`grouping-indian-ocean/shot-1440.png`) renders this as the first thing under the title, followed by "AS DEFINED BY historians (K. N. Chaudhuri; Abdul Sheriff) ... (canon NET005; not re-checked here)". "Not re-checked here" is a note to the editor, on the page.
- Specs: written for the production team, correctly; but phrases like "hold until a reader clears" (F1.13 §9) are now echoed in the lecture JSON (`"held": "Paid reader with lived experience of the African diaspora (spec §9); held until a reader clears"`), and from there could leak to screen.

### 5. Evidence handling: the right idea, the wrong layer

- What works: the **argued** form. Edo b3-4 ("When did Prussian blue reach Edo's printers?") gives two readings, three dated pieces of evidence, a verdict and a one-sentence "why". The built card (d-b3-4.png) labels A "probable" and B "documented" and asks the reader to decide before the verdict. b4-5 ("Who made the Great Wave?") and b5-7 ("Did the ban create the landscape print?") are genuine historical questions with real evidence, and the verdict is hedged honestly ("Mostly B"). This is elaborative interrogation done properly. F1.13's argued pairs are weaker because they end "We do not choose between them" (s2) and "We hold both" (s5): a learner is shown two readings and then told the course has no view, which reads as evasion rather than suspended judgement.
- What obstructs: (a) **bracketed claim ids in narration**. The F1.13 narration carries `[2]`, `[2, 3]`, `[6]` on 87 sentences; read aloud they are inaudible, on screen they are footnote marks to a register the learner cannot open. (b) **Confidence words on every caption** (`"confidence": "documented"` on 86 captions). If almost everything is "documented", the word stops meaning anything; it should appear only when the confidence is *not* documented. The scene-sheet lint actually enforces this ("the confidence words live here", in the note beat) and the Edo page shows it correctly as a chip on the argued card; the lecture does not. (c) **"As defined by"** on the grouping page: the question "who named this grouping and when" is a good one, but a 70-word answer with two canon codes and a "not re-checked" is not a learner's answer. One sentence would do: "'The Indian Ocean world' is a historians' name from the 1980s; the people in it had their own names for its seas." (d) **Rights and provenance in the lesson** (Edo b7-5, b9-3; F1.13 s3 "salvaged commercially from 1998. We link to that record and show no image of it"). The reader is being told why they cannot see something, in the place where they expected to see it.
- One evidence leak: Edo b5-2's guess asks "What ceiling did the state set?" (truth 16 mon), but the stage already draws the dashed price line stepping down to 16 and labels it "a bowl of soba (probable)" (d-b5-2.png). The answer is on screen before the guess.

### 6. Balance of apparatus to content

- Edo: per beat, about 70 words of lesson, one collapsed "Sources · n" line, and the stage. Ratio is right. The cast cards (`people.yaml`) carry a 120–220-word story plus three highlights, each with a confidence word: good, and the chip form keeps it out of the prose.
- F1.13 scene sheet page: by measurement of `shot-1440.png`, roughly one line of apparatus (claims/sources row, beat-kind label, station tag) for every three lines of content, plus the instrument description block and the honesty note at the foot. The beats' own comments ("42 words; one date (1300s), one number (four); the scene's thesis in the lecture's words") are in the YAML only, which is correct, but the rendered page has inherited the same register.
- Lecture JSON per station: ~170 words of narration, ~10 captions, ~25 commands. The captions duplicate the narration almost word for word (s1 caption at 35 s is the narration's sentence verbatim). For a learner with sound on, every sentence is read twice; with sound off, it is the narration in 10 fragments.
- Indian Ocean page: of the ~2,600 words, about 900 are the walk a learner would read; the rest is card, members list (a block of 60 canon codes), neighbours by code, candidate objects table with provenance and licence columns, and a 25-item source list. The members paragraph ("This walk lights 10 networks and diasporas, 11 places, 3 people, 5 texts ...") is an inventory, not teaching.

### 7. Retrieval and practice

- Edo has the module's only real practice: nine guesses (slider, tap-the-mark, map tap, route choice, timeline, bet), four argued pairs, five act-end "rebuild" drags (six tiles onto a timeline or a map, each with a hint, and one tile carried forward from an earlier act, e.g. r2-z "The kiwame seal ... from Act I"), and a coda that orders six events across the whole story. The carried-forward tile is spacing done on purpose. The "peel", "margin", "edition", "three waves" tools are worked examples a learner operates. This is the right repertoire.
- Gaps in Edo: all guesses are closed (slider, tap, two options). None asks the learner to *say* anything, even in their head, before the answer; the "argued" card asks "weigh the evidence, then choose" but there is no commit-before-reveal in the built card (d-b3-4.png shows both readings and both confidence chips at once). The rebuild hints give the answer ("Harunobu's year: five before his death" for a tile dated 1765, after b1-7 said "He had five years left to live"), so the drag is recognition, not recall. Nothing is re-asked after a delay longer than one act, except the coda.
- F1.13: four one-tap predictions in 11 minutes, each revealed within four seconds, and six closing questions whose options are timeline band codes ("B07 · 500–1000"). A learner answering "In which band did Kilwa mint coins?" is practising the course's own coding scheme. The spec's Lab ("Route Tracer: trace one object in four layers") and the two Apply tasks (150 words each) are the actual practice, and none of them exists on a page.
- Scene sheet and grouping page: no practice at all. The Indian Ocean "Apply" (design three blocks; write Madmun's order) is good, specific, and unbuilt.
- The ballet brief plans "this unit is read, not played", which is a defensible choice, but then the only "doing" is reading; the one task that would fit (take a tutu apart in making order) is described as a step-through, not something the learner orders.

### 8. Assessment and feedback: none, and what would fit

- There is no feedback beyond the reveal. The guesses say "close" (`close: 2` on log sliders) but the built page does not tell the reader how close they were or what a wrong guess implies. The "say" text is the same whether you were right or wrong (b1-4: "About 1.3 million by the city's own count ... The buyers came before the pictures").
- Given the rule of no points and no badges, what fits is **feedback on reasoning and a visible artefact of learning**: (a) the argued card should take a commitment (A or B) and then show the verdict *with the reader's choice marked*, plus one sentence addressed to the reader who chose the other way ("If you chose A: the earlier arrival is real; the price is what changed"); (b) the act-end rebuild should report which tiles the reader moved on first try, without a score, as "you placed these from memory, these with a hint"; (c) each unit should end with a **closing contribution** the learner writes (the specs already define it: the SRC line in F1.13 §"Closing contribution", the attribution line in F1.1 §8), shown back to them and kept; (d) a "what you can now do" checklist at the end of the unit, written as the objectives, that the reader ticks for themselves. All of this is formative, none of it scores.

- Sentence length and grade level are right for a university first-year reading in English as a second language. The Edo max of 37 words is one sentence (b2-3's contract clause) and acceptable.
- The problem is not the prose but **what surrounds it**: Japanese quotations in the beat (b5-2, b5-3) are given without romanisation; the built page shows them in red; a screen-reader user gets the kanji string. The F1.13 captions use "B07" band codes. The scene-sheet page uses 10–11 px monospace for apparatus. Timeline labels overprint in the station renders. The Edo README promises alt text per image and the overture has it (`alt:` on each shot); the beats' `figure.cap` is a credit line, not a description.
- Content notes: F1.13's are well worded and give a skip; but six identical notes in nine stations is a pattern that trains skipping. Edo's `note: quarter` beats (b1-3, b2-3, b4-6) carry no visible content note in the script; the full case says the note is "spoken here, once" in lecture 1, which the scrolling unit does not have.
- The Edo page is built for 1440 and 390 and both were checked; the F1.13 scene sheet at 390 is a single 3,700 px column in which apparatus and content alternate.

### 10. Inclusion: whose names, which regions

- Edo cast: 41 people across six groups (13 designers, 7 publishers, 6 dealers and collectors, 5 carvers and printers, 5 state, 5 writers and actors). Two women (Katsushika Ōi, Hanaōgi), and the unit is honest about why ("From 1793 the only women prints could name were courtesans", b2-3; "One historian argues her hand was made to vanish", b4-6). Act IV's named cast is Hayashi, Bing, Van Gogh, Fenollosa, Wright, the Spauldings: the "second trade" is told from Paris, Boston and Chicago. Osaka gets one beat (b2-7); Nagoya one (b3-2); Kyoto none beyond the road's end. The paper-makers of Echizen, promised in p-2 ("one sheet of paper from Echizen to New York"), get half a sentence in b9-1.
- F1.13 is built around inclusion and it shows in the right places: the rule that people get their own mark; "enslaved people", never "slaves", in the course's voice; Omar ibn Said taught through his own text; Yi Sam-pyeong named; the Guran shipwrights' words withheld until asked. But the unit's named individuals are Abu'l-Qasim, Mansa Musa, Cresques Abraham, "Master Potter A", Omar ibn Said, Yi Sam-pyeong, Hecht, Seijas, Bloom, Jiang. Half the names a learner meets are modern scholars cited in-line ("Hecht writes", "Seijas writes", "Bloom writes", "Jiang and colleagues"). The makers in every station are "potters not named".
- Indian Ocean draft: three people (Ben Yiju, Ashu, Ibn Battuta); Ashu is the only woman and is "named in his deed but with no row of her own". The "Leaves out" row is honest and good, and would be better as the page's closing paragraph than a card field.
- Ballet brief, claim 7 ("Ballet's map is wider than its history"), names Alonso, Howes, Stevenson and the schools in Beijing, Havana, Cape Town and Harlem. This is the best inclusion move in the set because it is a *claim*, not a disclaimer.

### 11. What learning science supports, and where the module sits

- **Retrieval practice and the testing effect.** Roediger and Karpicke (2006) showed that retrieving beats re-reading for retention a week later (https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x). Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate practice testing and distributed practice as the two highest-utility techniques, with elaborative interrogation and self-explanation as moderate, and rereading and highlighting as low (https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266; summary at https://sites.nd.edu/kaneb/?p=1307). The Edo rebuilds and coda are practice testing; the F1.13 closing questions on band codes are not, because the thing retrieved is the course's apparatus.
- **Pretesting and productive failure.** Kornell, Hays and Bjork (2009) found that an unsuccessful retrieval attempt before the answer improves later memory for it (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19586265/; Richland, Kornell and Kao on pre-testing: https://learninglab.uchicago.edu/Pre-Testing_files/RichlandKornellKao.pdf). The module's "guess before the reveal" is exactly this and is the single best design decision in the set. Its value is lost when the answer is visible on the stage (Edo b5-2) or when the reveal arrives in four seconds with no commitment (F1.13 predictions).
- **Spacing.** Cepeda et al. (2006) meta-analysis: spaced study beats massed for retention at every interval tested (https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354). Edo's carried-forward rebuild tile (one per act) is the only spacing in the module. Nothing returns after a day.
- **Worked examples and cognitive load.** Sweller's worked-example effect (Sweller and Cooper 1985; review in Atkinson et al. 2000, https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543070002181): novices learn a procedure better from a worked example than from solving. Edo's "margin" tool ("Follow a curator through its five marks, in the order a curator reads them", b6-4) is a worked example and should be followed by a faded one (a fourth print, fewer hints), which `EDO_FULL_CASE.md` I1 already specifies and the build has not got.
- **Multimedia principles.** Mayer's coherence, signalling, redundancy, segmenting and pre-training principles (summarised at https://lucidea.com/blog/mayers-12-principles-of-multimedia-learning and https://blog.stcloudstate.edu/ims/2021/07/10/mayers-12-principles-of-multimedia): remove extraneous material; do not present identical text on screen and in narration; segment; teach key terms before the lesson. The F1.13 lecture violates redundancy (captions duplicate narration) and coherence (claim brackets, confidence words, policy statements); the scene-sheet page violates coherence by rendering build notes; neither unit pre-trains the four or five terms a learner needs (kiwame, aratame, nishiki-e, mon; flow, relay, Kraak). Edo's overture is good segmenting and signalling.
- **Elaborative interrogation and self-explanation.** Dunlosky et al. rate these moderate. The Edo argued cards ask "why" with evidence; F1.13 asks the learner to "hold both", which is not elaboration.
- **Objectives and backward design.** Wiggins and McTighe's *Understanding by Design* (ASCD, https://www.ascd.org/books/understanding-by-design-expanded-2nd-edition) and the OpenLearn pattern (every course opens with "After studying this course, you should be able to..."; e.g. https://www.open.edu/openlearn/history-the-arts/studying-the-arts-and-humanities/content-section---learningoutcomes) are the plain standard the module does not yet meet.

### 12. What comparable resources do better

- **The Met, Heilbrunn Timeline, "Woodblock Prints in the Ukiyo-e Style"** (https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/woodblock-prints-in-the-ukiyo-e-style): six paragraphs, twelve captioned objects, no inline citation, a further-reading list and a citation block at the foot. It explains the four trades (publisher, designer, carver, printer) in one paragraph without a single accession number in the prose; the accession is in the caption. That is the layering this module should copy: sources and codes at the foot or in the caption, never in the sentence.
- **Smarthistory, "Hokusai, Under the Wave off Kanagawa (The Great Wave)"** by Leila Anne Harris (reprinted at https://uen.pressbooks.pub/arth2720/chapter/hokusai-under-the-wave-off-kanagawa-the-great-wave/): ~600 words, opens by naming the object and saying it was mass-produced and cheap in the second sentence, two subheadings, plain voice. Its weakness is exactly the module's strength: it never names the publisher or the carver, so the module's thesis genuinely adds something. But Smarthistory's pages are complete, short and self-contained, and every one reads as a finished lesson, not a build.
- **Khan Academy's art history** (which republishes Smarthistory) adds to each essay a short practice set and places the essay in a sequenced unit with a stated scope; the module's F1 sequence (31 units, four batches of specs) has no learner-facing map of where a unit sits or what came before.
- **MoMA Learning** (https://www.moma.org/momaorg/shared/flattened_pages/moma_learning/about.html) organises by theme and by question ("What is modern art?"), with each theme a few hundred words plus three to six works, and separate "tools and tips" for teachers. The module conflates the two audiences on one page.
- **OpenLearn** states learning outcomes, time ("about 4 hours", "Level 1: Introductory") and gives a statement of participation at the end, with no score. This is the "no points, no badges" assessment model the module says it wants and has not built.
- **The British Museum's "Great Wave: spot the difference"** (Korenberg, cited in `teaching.md`) is already identified in the project's own research as "itself a teaching structure": diagnostics a learner can apply to date an impression. The Edo "three waves" tool uses the images but does not yet ask the learner to apply a diagnostic and commit.

## What to keep

- The Edo script's beat discipline: 40–100 words, no sentence over 25, one instrument state per beat, a year on every beat, sources collapsed behind one line. Enforced by a build lint, which is how it stays kept.
- The thesis and its pay-off structure in Edo: stated in p-2, demonstrated four times, paid off in b8-5, restated in b9-4; one-line "bets" as act theses.
- Guess before reveal, as the default move; the nine guess types; the argued card with evidence, verdict and "why".
- The act-end rebuild with one tile carried from an earlier act; the coda order. The "margin" worked example and the "peel" and "three waves" tools.
- The evidence discipline itself: the four confidence words, two readings for a contested claim, "not recorded" as an honest value, people named where the record names them, the human-flow rule. Keep the discipline; move its display.
- The content-note wording and the skip in F1.13 s8; the language rule (AR-080); the decision to withhold the Guran shipwrights' names until asked.
- The specs' success criteria and the Apply tasks (F1.13 §8 and the maker/briefer rows; F1.1 §8; Indian Ocean "Apply"). These are the objectives and the assessments; they exist, they are good, they are not on any page.

## A learner test in under an hour

Purpose: find out whether a page teaches what it claims, with real readers, before the next build pass. Five readers, one facilitator, one page (Edo Act II or F1.13 stations 1–2), 50 minutes, no scores.

1. **Before (5 min).** Each reader writes, on paper, one sentence answering the unit's essential question as they would guess it now ("How did a city turn pictures into a mass market, and who got named?") and marks three words from the unit's term list as known or unknown. Collect.
2. **Read (15–20 min).** Readers go through the page alone on their own phone or laptop, with sound if the page has narration. The facilitator notes, per reader, where they scroll back, where they tap, where they skip, and the time at each act or station boundary. No help is given; a question from a reader is written down and not answered.
3. **Immediate recall (5 min).** Paper, face down until started: (a) "In one sentence, what is this unit's argument?" (b) three objective-level questions drawn from the stated objectives, written as open prompts, not multiple choice (Edo II: "Where did the blue come from and why did that matter to a publisher?"; "Name the four trades and say which two the law never named"; "What is the difference between a tsunami and what Hokusai drew?"). (c) "Which sentence or screen confused you? Point to it."
4. **Think-aloud on one screen (10 min).** Two readers, one at a time, are shown one dense screen (an argued card, or the scene-sheet beat with its claims row) and asked to say what every element is for. Count the elements they cannot explain or that they say they ignored.
5. **Delayed recall (5 min, two days later, by message).** The same one-sentence argument question and one of the three objective questions. Compare with step 3.
6. **Scoring the test, not the readers.** The page passes if four of five readers can state the thesis in their own words at step 3 and three of five at step 5; if the three objective questions are answered correctly by at least three readers each; if no element on the think-aloud screen is called "ignored" by more than one reader; and if no reader's "confused" pointer lands on the same sentence twice. Any element two readers ignore is apparatus to move down a layer; any sentence two readers point to is rewritten before the next test. Record results in `review-logs/` with the page's build hash, and run the same test again after the change.
