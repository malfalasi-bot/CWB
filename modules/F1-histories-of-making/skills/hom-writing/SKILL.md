# Writing for the learner

The prose comes before any HTML. Write the page as text with its captions and its objectives; the toolbox dresses it afterwards. The learner is an adult with no background in the subject who has twenty minutes and a phone.

## 1. Before the first sentence: the subject, then the brief

A page is written from a planned unit, never from the first good object. Before any page: the subject is scoped from every angle the course needs (for an art form: history and institutions; the body or the hands and their technique; the objects, makers and materials; the stage, space or setting; money, labour and ownership; the record and how it is taught), from the scholarly base and not from a handful of articles; the scope says what a learner must, should and could know, the concepts and debates, and what each session carries (`research_notes/Ballet/scope/` is the worked example). A page is one part of that plan, and its reader should see where it sits: what came before, what this page adds, what comes next. A page that tells the story of one object without the idea it serves is a feature article, not teaching.

Then write from the unit's brief (`hom-research` makes it): the thesis, five to seven claims with their strongest source, three verified objects, one argument, one living maker, what the sources leave out, the spine the subject suggests. If there is no brief, write one first; a page without a thesis becomes a catalogue.

## 2. The opening

- **Objectives, in the second person, before anything else.** "By the end you will be able to…" three lines, each a verb a reader can test on themselves (date a print from its margin; name the four trades and who the law named; explain why the maker's name is missing). Drawn from the unit spec's success criteria, written by the editor.
- **The context first, then the thesis**: what the reader needs to know to understand why this matters (the period, the place, the institution, the idea), in two or three sentences; then the thesis in one sentence; the first object within the first 200 words.
- **No question or guess at the opening.** The page opens with context and the idea, never with a quiz, a poll or a "choose one" (decided by the course owner, 21 October 2026). Questions belong at the end of a section or a session, after the reader has the material to answer them, and as making tasks rather than right-or-wrong choices.
- **The five terms the page will use** that the reader does not know (nishiki-e, kiwame; flow, relay; pointe, turnout), each glossed in a clause the first time it appears, never in a glossary alone.
- Where the subject carries a content note, it comes once, here, with a persistent control to skip the stations it covers; never repeated station by station.

## 3. Reading orders

Choose the one the subject has; name it in the brief; do not mix two in one page.

- **An object taken apart**: wearer or user → material → why that material then → construction, step by step → care and afterlife. The London Museum's Pavlova costume is the exemplar. Each step headed, each with its own picture, so the reader can stop after any.
- **A workshop walked**: room by room, one craft per room, one concrete number per room, a photograph of the room not the finished thing, the maker named where they are and "maker unknown" where they are not.
- **One work re-made**: the same work in three or four makings (date, place, makers, budget, bodies), so the reader sees making repeated under different conditions.
- **A bench read outward**: one maker, one object, one chain of names, from the raw material to the stage or the shop.
- **A route followed**: one object or material from where it was made to where it was found, stop by stop with the dates the sources give and dashed where they do not.
- **A dispute posed**: the question, the evidence in the order a reader needs it, then the two readings, then the reader's lean.

Edo's lecture-and-beats pattern (`references/beats.md`) is one more order, for a unit that has full lectures; it is optional.

## 4. The paragraph

- One idea per paragraph; the sentence that carries the date carries it in words a reader can hold ("about 1830", "the 1590s", "between 1841 and 1843"), never a decimal, never "c. 1830–32" in running prose (that form is for captions and readouts).
- The maker is named in the first paragraph that shows their work, as the record names them; "maker unknown" is written, not skipped.
- The hedge is in the sentence: "probably goose, pending analysis"; "the Met's record says"; "historians disagree about when". Never a bracket, a superscript or a code in the learner view.
- One concrete number per panel or section, with who counted it in the sentence or the caption.
- Sentences: median 14–24 words, none over 40; a first sentence under 16 words. Reading level about grade 8–9. A technical term is explained the first time it appears.
- Voice: second person for objectives and prompts, third person for the history, present tense for what the object is and past for what happened. No "we write", "we hold", "this unit", "the register", "the course": the project does not speak on the page.
- Banned in the learner view (they are the tooling's words): tick, tile, lens, station, instrument, focus mask, register, claim, record id, beat, scene player, stepper, component, build, placeholder, "as defined by", "membership confidence", "documented".

## 5. Captions

Under the image, never over it. A caption says what the eye cannot see ("Prussian blue in the dark stripes, indigo under it"), not what it can ("a wave"). The credit line follows the caption in smaller type: holder · accession · date · materials · licence, and the maker. Alt text describes the picture for someone who cannot see it and does not repeat the caption. Where the picture is a placeholder for an image not yet fetched, the learner view shows nothing in that slot; the editor view shows the frame. When no photograph is free to use, a schematic drawn in code may stand in: a section or an exploded view, parts numbered on the drawing with a key in body-size text under it (labels inside an SVG are unreadable on a phone), and a caption that says it is a schematic, names the parts only as the page's sources describe them, says who drew it and its licence, and never claims the makers as its source. Draw a hollow where there is a hollow; a filled toe teaches the misconception the page corrects.

## 6. What to leave out

The thesis chooses. A fact that does not serve one of the brief's claims goes back to the dossier, however good it is. A page's reading is 400–900 words of prose (the lint counts everything a learner sees, key and guess included, so a page with a figure key runs to about 1,000); a unit is a twenty-minute read; what does not fit is a link, not a paragraph. A named person stays on the page only with a number or a line of their own; a reader keeps three names, so a list of seven loses them all. A page does not end on what its sources leave out. Gaps that matter to the argument go inside the argument, where the reader meets them ("the makers are known to us only from a journalist's visit" belongs in the paragraph about the makers); the list of what the page could not include, why there is no photograph, and how the names were sourced go to the editor view. The page ends on the idea and on where the reader goes next. Where a gap is part of the learning, it is written for the reader, not as an apology to the project: no objects the reader cannot see, no verification state ("being fetched", "not yet checked"), which belongs to the editor view only. Negatives are scoped to what was read ("no source read for this page", "the nearest count this page found"), never to the world ("no source anywhere", "the only count that exists").

## 7. Guesses, arguments and the ending

- No opening guess or quiz (see §2). Where a session uses a question, it comes after the material, asks the reader to apply or argue rather than recall, and has no right-or-wrong reveal.
- An argued card records the reader's lean and shows the evidence against it with one sentence for the reader who leaned the other way.
- One spaced return per unit (a question from two sections back) and one across units.
- The unit ends with something the learner makes: the attribution line, the SRC line, the margin read, written by the learner with a worked example first. That is the assessment; there are no points.
- Then the self-check: the three objectives again, as questions.
- Objectives are written in words the reader can say back ("spot which numbers on this page are a company's own", not "tell a date a source supports from a figure a company gives about itself") and each is checked against the finished page: if the page does not let a closed-book reader do it, the objective or the page changes.
- A figure someone told a journalist, a company or a union is written with its teller in the sentence ("Ray told the writer he makes forty pairs a day", "Freed says", "the union put it at") and the register grades it documented with the rule "a told figure, labelled as the teller's in the sentence"; a figure with no teller carries "probably" or "contested" in the sentence. Two figures from the same company that disagree are shown together; that disagreement is the lesson, not a thing to tidy.
- A source the page leans on by name ("the blog says", "the series") is introduced at its first use, in the reader's terms ("Spitalfields Life, a local blog that visited his bench in 2013").
- People named from a press source: the page says they spoke for that piece and not for the course, gives them as the source recorded them (first names stay first names, and says why), and carries the undertaking that any of them may have their name taken out by asking. The register's people block records each with who recorded them; nobody is given a surname, a room or a gender the source does not give.

## 8. When not to build

Say so in a paragraph a commissioning editor would accept, and build the smaller thing:

- Five places in a list do not need a globe; a list with dates is the page.
- A journey with no dated legs gets a bar with the line "no leg of this route carries a date", not a scene.
- A subject under the content thresholds (fewer than five open objects, under 60 % dated) gets its reading and its cards, and a "what waits" table in the editor view.
- An interactive that reveals what a paragraph could hold is cut; film is for touch and time, a still image for evidence.

## 9. Two views from one source

The same source renders a learner view (objectives, the reading, the pictures with captions, the guesses, the ending, one Sources line) and an editor view (everything: record ids, confidence on every value, "as defined by", rights, honesty lines, what waits, the colophon). Build notes never reach the learner view.

## 10. Before it is called done

Run `hom-lint`'s prose lint (sentence lengths, banned words, priority claims for the judge, doubled labels) and the claims lint; read the learner view cold on a phone; then the human read. The page is finished when a reader can say the thesis in their own words and name the first thing they learned.
