## F1.10 The Islamic world as a network, read through routes and the waqf

We read the Islamic world as a network: routes moved paper, cobalt, cloth, skills and people, and endowments paid makers to build and keep things working.

**Spec**

| Row | Content |
|---|---|
| Essential question | What moved along the routes of the Islamic world, who paid for what was made, and who kept it working? |
| Opening move | **Data first (forms 12, 3).** A map shows nine pins, each labelled with the holder's own culture line: "Egypt", "Iran, probably Kashan", "Turkey, Iznik", "France", "Europe". Prompt: "Sort them: Islamic art, or not?" The learner taps, on the map, the pins they would leave out; the reveal follows within seconds. **Claim:** Islamic art is art made by Muslims for the faith. **Counter-claim:** Blair and Bloom (2003) call a distinct "Islamic" tradition "a product of late nineteenth- and twentieth-century Western scholarship". The Met (2001) defines it by faith, lands, patrons or makers. The reveal: a Paris lamp copies Mamluk glass; one Egyptian loom tradition runs from the 4th century to the 10th; a Jewish trader keeps a workshop's accounts in India. Case: the Brocard lamp (Cleveland 1981.10). |

P. Morgan, Kevorkian), with gaps marked "not recorded". The learner sees that route one is often rich and route two often thin. |

- **People on the routes:** mamluks bought as enslaved boys (waqf page); Ashu, enslaved and freed by Ben Yiju (F1.9); Gulf divers held in debt (OCC201, PLC298), named only, for local authors.

**Colonial-context screen:** LOC 2017657142 is a Swiss firm's tourist print of Cairo, made about 1890–1910 under British occupation; shown with this line: "Photoglob, a Swiss firm, printed this view of Cairo for tourists, about 1890–1910, while Britain occupied Egypt."
