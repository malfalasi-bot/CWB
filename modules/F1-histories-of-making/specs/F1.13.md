## F1.13 Networks: objects, materials, skills and people

A network is four flows at once (objects, materials, skills and people), and this unit follows one jar's blue through all four.

**Spec**

| Row | Content |
|---|---|
| Essential question | What moved along a route (an object, a material, a skill or a person), who moved it, and on what terms? |
| Opening move | **Data first, with a prediction (forms 12, 15).** A map holds one dot, Jingdezhen, and a jar painted in blue, 1300s (Cleveland 1962.154). A dashed line runs west to an unlabelled source. Prompt: "Where did the blue come from?" One tap on the map; the reveal follows within seconds. **Claim:** blue-and-white is wholly Chinese. **Counter-claim:** its potters and clay were in Jingdezhen; its blue came from Iran. The reveal gives two readings of which Iranian source. Case: the jar. |
| Lab or main interactive | **Route Tracer (form 4, with the Historian's Connection check).** Trace one object in four layers. **Object:** where made, sold, used. **Material:** of what, from where. **Skill:** who knew how, and how the knowing travelled. **People:** who moved, by choice or by force, how many, named where the record names them. Every edge carries a source and a confidence, or "not recorded". The people layer is drawn apart, never as an arrow shared with goods. Its words: "enslaved people" or "captives", and "enslaver"; never "slaves", "owner" or "master" in our voice (section 2; AR-080). Starters: section 5's open records. |
| Concept | 600 words. **A network is a reading:** most exchange was relay, port to port and oasis to oasis, and the names came later, from outside. **Four flows** share routes but not terms. **Who carried:** merchant diasporas (Sogdians, Gujaratis, Hadramis, the Geniza merchants) beside people moved by force (weavers resettled by Mongol order, Korean potters, the *chinos* of New Spain, 12.5 million Africans). **The argued question:** did routes make objects, or did demand for objects make routes? **The rule:** people are never goods; we write them as subjects, count them as people, and name them where the record does. |

**Map rule.** People have their own layer and mark, never the arrows used for cloth or silver.

**Language rule (AR-080),** after the community-sourced guide by senior slavery scholars of colour (Foreman et al.): "enslaved people" or "captives", never "slaves" as a noun in our voice; "enslaver" for those who held people, not "owner" or "master"; enslaved people named wherever the record names them. A source's own words are quoted, attributed, and kept to the Deep tier (N6).

**Step 8 content note, as learners see it (AR-080):** "Content note: this step is about the Atlantic slave trade. Traders forced about 12.5 million African people onto ships, 1501–1866, and many died at sea. We show no images of bodies or ships. You can skip this step; the summary keeps the count."

**Excluded:**

- Ship plans that draw people as cargo (the Brooks diagram and its kind): named only.
- Shackles, and images of enslaved people's bodies.
- The Belitung cargo: commercial salvage, linked out.
- Kilwa's pillar tombs: graves, named only.
- The Pitt Rivers manilla page's price per person: it prices people like goods.

**Shown, with a content note:**

- Omar ibn Said's leaves. The English title page's "Owned by Governor Owen" is the enslaver's framing; we lead with his name and his Arabic text. The title page is not shown in the walk; its phrase is quoted only in the Deep tier, attributed as the enslaver's framing (N6). Caption, as learners see it: "Omar ibn Said wrote this account of his life in Arabic in 1831, while enslaved in North Carolina. He was born in Futa Toro and captured in 1807." A leaf with Qur'anic text is shown whole, never cropped (F1.10's rule) (AR-081). (Fact-check, 3 Oct 2026: LoC's record confirms the manuscript carries Sūrat al-Mulk; "Governor Owen" on the title page is John Owen, but James Owen was the enslaver; see claim 22.)
- Steps 3–8: enslavement, forced movement, war.
- The Catalan caption: a European mapmaker's framing, quoted and attributed.

No object is funerary, sacred or an Indigenous community's material, so no Cultural note is needed on those grounds; Omar ibn Said's leaves carry the content note above.
