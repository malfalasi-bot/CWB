## F1.16 Ritual and belief as drivers of making

Belief drives making through who commissions, what a rite requires, how often it repeats, where pilgrims spend and who reorganises the workshop; this unit follows those drivers, not theology.

**Spec**

| Row | Content |
|---|---|
| Essential question | When a rite or a belief asks for an object, what changes in who pays, who makes, how many, and by what rule? |
| Opening move | **Object first.** A small wooden pagoda, unlabelled, with a paper roll inside (Met 30.47a–c, c. 764–770). Prompt: "Someone ordered a million of these. The reveal: both. Belief and rule share one commission. Case: the Hyakumantō (One Million Pagodas). |
| Lab or main interactive | **Driver overlay** inside each object story (form 3). Tags are checked against the record text, not against opinion. On the mission case the overlay opens a **two-readings panel**: imposition and local reinvention, each with its source. |
| Concept | 650 words. Six drivers, one example each: commission and merit (Wang Jie, 868); rule (keros in pairs); material (Raku bowls); repetition (a million pagodas; 80,000 Tripitaka blocks); economy (Canterbury badges; Hōryūji's 1908 appeal); regime (missions in Kongo, New Spain, Cuzco, Guaraní lands, Japan). Then the reverse: belief also destroys making (drives against keros; Canterbury, 1538). Then foodways: rites of drink and food commission vessels. Rule: we describe how rites shaped making; we do not explain or rank beliefs. |
| Cases | **1. The scroll made for two parents (F1.8; Mahayana Buddhism).** The Diamond Sutra from the Mogao library cave, dated 868, the earliest dated printed book known. **2. Protocol screen**

**Excluded, and why:**

- **Kongo minkisi, including nkisi nkondi (BEL045):** they hold empowering substances; canon flags looting. The crucifix carries the Kongo case.
- **Andean ancestor bundles (mallqui) and huaca objects seized in the extirpation (OCC075):** human remains and sacred objects. Named in text, never shown.
- **Hopi katsina masks and figures (BEL039):** canon records the masks as not for sale or display; figures left out too.
- **Aboriginal ceremonial objects and restricted designs (BEL050), Sámi drums (BEL053), Ghost Dance garments taken at Wounded Knee (BEL040):** sacred, secret or taken from the dead.
- **Relic-bearing objects** (byeri guardians GAP057, Tibetan bone implements, relic deposits): human remains.
- **Malagan figures (BEL055):** made to be displayed once, then left.
- **Ethiopian altar tablets (tabot):** sacred; the holders' basis is not verified here.
- **Recitation of sacred texts:** the pagoda's invocation is described, never read aloud.

**Shown, with display basis and content note:**

- **Pagoda (Met 30.47a–c):** made in a million copies for temples; Hōryūji gave some to donors in 1908. Content note: devotional object.
- **Diamond Sutra:** made "for universal free distribution". Linked out. Content note: removed from Mogao in 1907.
- **Kongo crucifixes (Met 1999.295.7; Cleveland 2010.444):** the Met records public use as pendants and insignia of office. Content note: conversion under Portuguese pressure, destruction of Kongo objects, and the Atlantic slave trade through the same kingdom. Enslaved people are named as people, never as goods.
- **Kero (Cleveland 1946.233):** a drinking vessel. Notice: Andean Indigenous material with living descendant communities. Content note: Spanish drives against Andean rites. One of Cleveland's two keros shows heaped body parts; show only the geometric one.
- **Raku bowl (Met 17.118.74), pilgrim badge (Met 2001.310):** made for use and sale. Badge content note: the shrine was destroyed in 1538.
- **Link-outs with Notice:** the feather *Mass of St Gregory* (Nahua makers); Guaraní mission sites.

**6. People and credit**

- **Named commissioners:** Empress Shōtoku; Wang Jie; Diego de Alvarado Huanitzin; Sen no Rikyū. The Goryeo court for the Tripitaka.
- **Named makers:** Chōjirō, tentatively (Met). Pedro de Gante as probable supervisor, not maker.
- **Unnamed makers the unit must still name as makers:** the carvers, turners and printers of a million pagodas; the block cutter of 868; the Goryeo block carvers; the Kongo casters, "a trained specialist in metallurgy" (Fromont, Met); Andean kero carvers and inlayers; the Nahua feather artist; Guaraní carvers; the Japanese lacquerers; the jebena potter, "usually an elderly woman".
- **Left out by the credit:** the timber, clay and metal suppliers; Wang Yuanlu, the cave's keeper, often named only as "the abbot"; the donors of 1908; chicha brewers (not verified who, so not stated); the people held and sold through the Kongo kingdom in the same centuries.

**8. Success criteria**

1. For one case, the learner names the commissioner, the maker (or "unnamed maker") and the rule the maker worked to, each tagged to a line in the record.
2. For the Kongo crucifix, the learner writes both readings in two sentences, each with its source, and does not merge them.
3. The learner's rite brief (either lane) tags every fact as "belong", "told by", "read in" or "assumed", with no untagged sentence left.
