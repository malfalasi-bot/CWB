## F1.18 Textiles and the making called women's work; women's making beyond textiles

Institutions put women at the loom and the kiln, and credit lines then decided whose hands the record kept.

**Spec**

| Row | Content |
|---|---|
| Essential question | Who decided that this making was "women's work", and whose names did the record keep? |
| Opening move | **Making first, before any text.** The learner twists a two-ply cord from a strip of paper or yarn for two minutes, guided by a 40-second film of our own hands (no words on screen). Then three open cloths appear with the question "Who made this: a woman, a man, or both?" One tap per cloth; the reveal follows within seconds. A kente cloth (Met 85576, Asante), a Diné wearing blanket (Cleveland 1921.556, "woman artist") and an Inca tunic (Cleveland 1957.136). **Claim:** "Weaving has always been women's work." **Counter-claim:** who wove depended on place and on who was made to. "Most kente weavers are men" (Smarthistory); Ghana's own 2024 UNESCO file says women are "in charge primarily of weaving" and men make the looms, so the sources disagree. Inca fine tapestry was made by women and men (Met essay; Cleveland record). Ur III weavers were mostly women, but one tablet names male weavers (Garcia-Ventura 2014). Opening case: the kente cloth, because its own sources disagree on who weaves, and that breaks the claim at once. |
| Lab | **Every hand.** A credit ledger laid over one object. The learner drags role cards (designed, spun, dyed, wove or stitched, shaped, polished, painted, fired, supervised, sold, named on the label) onto the AIC *Lotus* curtain (1918.298), then onto the Cleveland plate signed "Marie and Santana" (1951.363, link out). Each card must carry a name, "unnamed" or "not known". The ledger then shows the holder's own label beside the learner's, so the gaps are visible. |
| Concept | About 700 words. Three mechanisms. **Assignment:** institutions placed women at looms: the Ur III state workshops (rations), the Inca aqllawasi (tribute), Lowell's mills (wages), the Bauhaus "women's workshop" (a school's policy), the Dhaka garment floor. **Erasure:** credit moved to a father (Honeysuckle, 1890), a firm (Morris & Co.), a trader (INS013) or a teacher (Cardew and Kwali). **Naming:** women put their own names on the work: "Marie + Julian", "Marie and Santana", Kwali's name on the Abuja pottery (renamed in the early 1980s or after her death in 1984; sources differ), Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay's boards and emporia. Beyond textiles: Tewa pottery, Gbari pottery (Ladi Kwali), Anangu and NPY fibre sculpture (Tjanpi, link out). It closes on the difference between rations, wages and a signature, and says what the record cannot tell us: how most of these women saw their work. |

**3. Claims**

1. HSS IV 3, as Waetzoldt (1972) read it, lists 4,272 women and 1,800 children from Guabba in the weaving sector. *Documented* (secondary). Vermaak 2008, https://www.harappa.com/content/guabba-meluhhan-village-mesopotamia. Depth: full text of the article; Waetzoldt not opened.
2. CDLI catalogues HSS 04, 003 (P110276) as an Ur III account from Girsu: "large balanced account; sesame oil and lard", Harvard SM 1899.02.001, photo © Harvard. *Documented.* https://cdli.earth/artifacts/110276. Depth: full record. How the weaver count sits in the account: not verified.
3. Most Ur III weavers were women, but not all: UET 3, 1449 says "the male workers are weavers". *Documented.* Garcia-Ventura 2014, https://www.academia.edu/8710629/Weaving_in_Ur_III_Mesopotamia_Womens_Work. Depth: summary.
4. Women workers in Ur III usually received half the rations of men. *Probable.* Same source. Depth: summary.
5. Ur III labour came mostly from dependent people who owed service to the crown; enslaved people were a limited part, working beside people of other statuses. *Probable* (for the weavers specifically: not stated). Reid 2023, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-review-of-social-history/article/punishment-for-the-coercion-of-labour-during-the-ur-iii-period/63F85846BCF0661180D19F3ECE24864F. Depth: abstract and introduction.
6. Inca officials collected girls aged eight to ten from the provinces "as tribute for the state"; they trained about four years in spinning, weaving, food and chicha in the aqllawasi. *Documented* in Spanish-era sources as summarised. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aclla. Depth: summary; the chronicles were not opened.
7. Whether selection was coercion or a route to status: *contested.* Reading one: girls were taken as tribute and later assigned as wives or to temples (Wikipedia, Aclla). Reading two: the women held rank and made the empire's most valued cloth (Cleveland 1957.136 text). The women's own view is not known.
8. Fine Inca tapestry "was made … by women of noble families, by professional weavers, and by the Aclla (Chosen Women)"; the Met says "a select group of female and male weavers". *Documented.* https://clevelandart.org/art/1957.136; https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/andean-textiles (McHugh 2020). Depth: full record; full essay.
9. Who weaves kente is *contested*. Reading one: "Most kente weavers are men" (Micots, Smarthistory). Reading two: Ghana's nomination, as UNESCO inscribed it in 2024, says "Both women and men are involved", with women "in charge primarily of weaving and producing cotton yarn" and men making the loom and tools. The two may describe different places or times; neither says which. https://smarthistory.org/kente-cloth/ ; https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/craftsmanship-of-traditional-woven-textile-kente-02130. Depth: full text; full page (corrected from documented, 3 Oct 2026).
10. May Morris designed *Honeysuckle* (1883). An 1890 *Woman's World* article credited it to William Morris. A Morris & Co. catalogue of about 1909 first gave it to May, and a letter of hers confirms it. *Documented*; the gallery notes that some still argue her father was chief designer (*contested*: give both readings). https://wmgallery.org.uk/object/honeysuckle-wallpaper/. Depth: full record.
11. The *Lotus* door curtain: design attributed to May Morris; producer Morris & Co.; embroidered by the Decorative Arts Society, Chicago (founded 1877), 1888, for Frances Glessner's parlour. *Documented* (the design attribution is the AIC's: *probable*). https://api.artic.edu/api/v1/artworks/74514. Depth: full record (rechecked and corrected 3 Oct 2026).
12. At the Bauhaus, weaving became known as the "women's workshop". Anni Albers went into it "unenthusiastically, as merely the least objectionable choice". *Documented.* https://www.tate.org.uk/whats-on/tate-modern/anni-albers/exhibition-guide. Depth: full text (the quotation's original source not opened).
13. Gunta Stölzl became a junior master in 1927 and ran the weaving workshop. She was forced out in 1931 amid Nazi pressure, after swastikas were painted on her door. *Documented* (the dates). That Mies van der Rohe required her resignation is *probable*: Wikipedia says so; other accounts say she resigned without his support. https://www.moma.org/artists/5675 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunta_St%C3%B6lzl. Depth: summary (confidence split 3 Oct 2026).
14. Maria Martinez (Po've'ka) coiled and polished; Julian Martinez painted; Hewett asked her to remake pottery from excavated sherds. *Documented.* Turnbow 2025, https://womenshistory.si.edu/blog/fragments-ancient-pottery-inspired-maria-martinez. Depth: full text.
15. Signatures ran: unsigned (1918–23), "Marie" (1923–25), "Marie + Julian" (1925–43), "Marie + Santana" (1943–54), "Maria/Popovi" (from 1956). *Probable* (dates differ by source). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Martinez. Depth: summary.
16. Cleveland 1951.363, c. 1943–51, is "Signed: Marie and Santana"; the record credits both women as artists. *Documented.* https://clevelandart.org/art/1951.363. Depth: full record.
17. Ladi Kwali learned pottery from her aunt and joined Cardew's Pottery Training Centre at Abuja (founded 1952) in December 1954 as its first woman trainee. *Documented.* She received an MBE in 1963 (MoMA, Art UK; AWARE says 1962). The centre was renamed for her in the early 1980s (Wikipedia) or after her death in 1984 (AWARE): *contested*. https://post.moma.org/pots-mastery-and-the-enduring-legacy-of-ladi-dosei-kwali/ ; https://awarewomenartists.com/en/artiste/ladi-kwali/ ; https://artuk.org/discover/artists/kwali-ladi-19201984 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ladi_Kwali. Depth: full text; summary (sources added and dates corrected 3 Oct 2026).
18. Lowell's workers were "young Yankee women, generally 15–30"; most worked 12 to 14 hours a day, and women struck twice in the 1830s. *Documented.* https://www.nps.gov/lowe/learn/historyculture/the-mill-girls-of-lowell.htm. Depth: full page.
19. Over 1,100 people, mostly garment workers, died when Rana Plaza collapsed near Dhaka on 24 April 2013 (ILO, April 2023); the ILO's *World Social Protection Report 2017–19* gives "at least 1,132" killed and "more than 2,500" injured. The typical Bangladeshi garment worker is "female, 23 years old, and an internal migrant". *Documented.* (ILO documents also give 1,134 and 1,136; "over 1,100" is the safe figure, with 1,132 as the ILO's dated count.) https://webapps.ilo.org/infostories/en-GB/Stories/Country-Focus/rana-plaza.html ; https://www.ilo.org/sites/default/files/wcmsp5/groups/public/@dgreports/@dcomm/@publ/documents/publication/wcms_604882.pdf. Depth: full page; report excerpt (figures added 3 Oct 2026).
20. **The unit's framing:** "women's work" is a category that states, schools, firms and markets produced and enforced, not a fact about women. *Interpretive.*

Supporting, not counted: Kamaladevi Chattopadhyay was the first chair of the All India Handicrafts Board and set up Cottage Industries emporia (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kamaladevi_Chattopadhyay; the 1952 date is from canon, not confirmed on that page). Tjanpi "represents over 400 artists from 26 communities" (https://tjanpi.com.au/).
