## F1.19a War, crisis and making: rationing, military production, reconstruction, propaganda, displacement

War does not stop making; it changes who pays, what is allowed, what the message says and where makers are forced to live, and this unit counts the people in that ledger before it counts any design.

**Spec**

| Row | Content |
|---|---|
| Essential question | When a war or a crisis arrives, what happens to a maker's patron, materials, message and home, and who pays for the change? |
| Opening move | **Story first.** Karakorum, 1254. The friar William of Rubruck hears of "a certain master goldsmith, William by name, a native of Paris" (Rockhill translation, 1900). William Buchier was taken in a Mongol campaign in Europe about 1241–42. At Karakorum he made a silver tree whose four silver lions poured mare's milk. **Claim:** war destroys making. **Counter-claim:** war moves and pays for making; the tree exists because a goldsmith was captured. The reveal: both, and the unit's rule: we never count what war gave design without counting who was taken, killed or made to work. Case: Guillaume Buchier (Boucher). |
| Lab or main interactive | **The crisis ledger** (form 12, map). Forced moves of makers and one arms-trade network, in two layers that never merge: *people* (home, destination, dates, source) and *goods* (guns, cloth, templates). Each arrow opens its source; people are never drawn as a flow of goods. For each case the learner fills five columns: *patron* (who paid), *material* (what was cut, swapped or supplied), *message* (what the object had to say), *home* (where the makers lived, by whose order) and *cost*, which cannot be left blank. |
| Concept | 650 words. Five things war does to making. **Patron:** the state becomes the largest client (dazzle; the Eames splint; British guns to 1815, as Satia argues). **Scarcity:** rationing writes the brief (Utility furniture). **Message:** the state sets words and type (the 1939 posters). **Removal:** makers taken or driven out (Mongol colonies; Samarkand; Korean potters, 1590s; Bosque Redondo; Hmong camps; camps for Japanese Americans). **Rebuilding:** prefabs, 1945–49. The rule: every gain is reported beside its cost, in the same sentence. |
| Cases | **1. Cloth of gold for the khans (F1.12a steppe; F1.10; F1.8).** Cleveland 1989.50, silk and gold lampas, Central Asia, 1225–75, beside the Met's Yuan cloth of gold (2001.595). The Mongols moved whole weaving communities from Central Asia and Persia to China to weave it (Rossabi, 1997). At Karakorum, captive goldsmiths served the court: Buchier, and Cosmas, who made Güyük's throne and seal. Coda: Timur's craftsmen at Samarkand, c. 1400. **2. The navy as client (F1.11; F1.7).** Dazzle, 1917–18: Wilkinson's unit, with 11 women art students, drew a scheme for each ship; over 2,300 ships were painted. Whether it worked was never shown. Strip: the Eames leg splint; Utility furniture; the 1944 prefabs; the reserve poster of 1939. Open object: AIC 1919.1048, a 1917 Red Cross appeal poster. Link F1.15: dazzle as ornament with a military brief. **3. Weaving after the Long Walk (F1.7; F1.9).** Cleveland 1937.903, a Diné rug, c. 1900. The US Army killed Diné flocks and marched more than 8,000 Diné to Bosque Redondo, where they were held four years (NPS). Cleveland's label links the shift to rugs for collectors to the trade networks broken by that imprisonment. Strip (link out): Hmong story cloths from Thai refugee camps; objects made from scrap in the US camps for Japanese Americans. |

Never borrow a real refugee's story. |

**2. Protocol screen**

**Excluded, and why:**

- **Photographs of the dead, injured or starving** from any war, famine or camp. The Kazakh famine and the Mongolian purges stay in the Atlas.
- **Atrocity and racial propaganda** (for example Nazi posters): named, not reproduced; typography taught through our own drawings.
- **Diné ceremonial textiles and sacred designs:** not shown.
- **Human remains and grave goods:** none needed; none shown.

**Shown, with display basis and content note:**

- **Cloths of gold (Cleveland 1989.50, Met 2001.595):** court textiles. Content note: woven by people moved by force after conquests that killed city populations. Captives are named as people, never as "booty".
- **Diné textiles (Cleveland 1937.903, 1921.556):** made for trade and use. **Notice:** Diné material; living descendant community. Content note: the Long Walk and Bosque Redondo, 1864–68. "Woman artist" is kept as the museum's wording; her name was not recorded.
- **AIC 1919.1048:** a public appeal poster; no graphic content.
- **Story cloths and camp objects (link out only):** content note on war, flight and incarceration; Hmong-run and Japanese American sources first. "Incarceration" in our voice; "internment" and "relocation" quoted and attributed to the US government and the 2010 exhibition.
- **Satia's 2018 interview wording ("trading for slaves"):** not quoted in learner-facing text; our text says "enslaved people".

**8. Success criteria**

1. For one case, the learner fills all five ledger columns (patron, material, message, home, cost), each tagged to a source line.
2. The learner writes the two readings of dazzle's effect, or of the story cloths' origin, in two sentences, each with its source, without merging them.
3. In either Apply lane, every substitution or clause names who bears its cost, and no row is left "unpriced".
