## F1.2 The reference-to-appropriation plane

Every borrowing sits somewhere on two separate scales: how much of the source it takes, and how much harm it does to the people it came from.

**Spec**

| Row | Content |
|---|---|
| Essential question | When one maker uses another people's form, what decides whether it is a nod, a copy, a trade good or a taking? |
| Opening move | **Prediction first (form 15).** Three unlabelled images: a porcelain dish showing a French fable; ivory elephants carved trunk to tail along a tusk; a flask printed "Navajo". Prompt: "Who copied whom? Which one ended in a lawsuit?" Reveal: Chinese painters copied a French print for European buyers (Cleveland 1970.48). A carver in Ekibondo, then Belgian Congo, made the elephants for European and American buyers from an Asian composition (Cleveland 1929.342). A US retailer sold the flask, and the Navajo Nation sued (D.N.M. 1:12-cv-00195). **Claim:** borrowing is how design has always moved, so no use is wrong in itself. **Counter-claim:** some uses take what a community has not offered, and the harm lands on that community. **Case:** Diné, where both happened to one people (below). |
| Lab or main interactive | **The plane.** Horizontal: reference → quotation → adaptation → translation → appropriation. Vertical: harm to the source community, from none to severe. Learners drag each case onto the plane before and after its object story; the board keeps both positions, so movement is visible. No correct cell is stored. |
| Concept | About 550 words. (1) The two axes are independent: a bare name can do more harm than a full copy. (2) The five positions defined in plain words, each with one case. (3) Borrowing runs every way: Chinese, Indian and Congolese makers adapting forms for European buyers, not only Europe taking. (4) The **tourist, souvenir and export arts** strand: makers adapting a form to a buyer's idea of it. Nelson Graburn (1976) separated arts made for the makers' own society from arts "made for an external, dominant world", "sometimes called 'tourist' or 'airport' arts". Grand Tour views (Panini), netsuke as collectors' objects (Atlas GAP036) and Kamba and Makonde carving (STY178, STY177) are Atlas links. (5) Communities answer back: the Navajo Nation's trademark, the Maasai Intellectual Property Initiative's licensing plan (founded 2010), the Tlahuitoltepec press conference. Forward to F1.26 and R2. |

**2. Protocol screen**

- No human remains, sacred or secret material in any case. Diné sandpainting designs (COM097 is flagged "sacred") are excluded; only the museum's wearing blanket is shown.
- **Notice** on case D (Diné) and case E (Ayuujk, Santa María Tlahuitoltepec): the community's own name first, and a line saying the community has spoken publicly about this use.
- **Case E images:** do not reproduce the Tlahuitoltepec blouse design, or Marant's version of it, and do not draw it ourselves. Showing it again would repeat the use in question. Link out only.
- **Case D images:** no images of the retail products. Link to the court docket and news reports.
- **Case A content note:** ivory; the ivory trade in colonial Congo depended on the killing of elephants and forced porterage (MAT074 notes). The object was acquired in 1928 under Belgian rule.
- People are never described as goods. Graburn's terms "Fourth World" and "Cuna" are quoted and attributed; the community's name is Guna (COM127 Dule).

**6. People and credit**

- **Named:** Kehar Singh (case C); Giovanni Paolo Panini (F). For case D, President Russell Begaye speaks for the Navajo Nation. Judge Bruce D. Black. Isabel Marant. Nelson Graburn (1936–2025, per PER466). Paul Travis and the Gilpin Players (case A).
- **Unnamed:** the Ekibondo carver; the Chinese factory's painters and potters (case B); the Diné woman who wove the blanket; the Tlahuitoltepec embroiderers.
- **What the credit leaves out:** in A, the carver is unnamed and the museum's "did not draw from local aesthetics" speaks for him. In B, the record names La Fontaine but no Chinese maker. In C, Singh is named but the spinners and weavers he painted are not. D's trademark protects the Nation's name but not the individual weaver. In E, the Paris court case was between two French firms; the community was not a party. In F, Panini is named, but the Pantheon's builders and the people shown inside it are not.

**8. Success criteria**

1. The learner places all six cases and gives at least two of the four answers for each, each tied to a named source.
2. The learner names one case where the horizontal position and the harm level diverge, and cites the evidence.
