## F1.22 The planetary present: making as extraction

Every made thing starts in a place that gave something up; this unit maps who dug, grew or carried it, what the place lost, and who counted the loss.

**Spec**

| Row | Content |
|---|---|
| Essential question | What did one object take, from which places and from whose work, and who counted it? |
| Opening move | **Data first.** A world map with two unlabelled dots. Prompt: "One country mined most of the world's cobalt in 2024. Which, and what share?" Reveal: Congo (Kinshasa), 226,000 of 302,000 tonnes, about 73% (USGS, February 2026). The second dot is China's rare-earth mining, 270,000 of about 380,000 tonnes (rare-earth oxide equivalent). **Claim:** extraction is a geography of the present, with a few places carrying most of the weight. **Counter-claim:** extraction is old; in 1556 Agricola recorded critics who said mining fells woods, kills birds and beasts and poisons streams. The reveal: both. What changed is the distance between the pit and the user, and how many hands the material passes through. Case: cobalt, from southern Congo to a phone battery. |
| Lab or main interactive | **Chain explorer** (form 4) laid over the map (form 12). The learner picks an object: a phone battery, a sack of guano (1865), an embroidered cotton cover, or a pair of jeans. Each link shows a place, a material, who worked there, one dated and sourced number, and a "left out" note. Layers: land, water, labour, waste. A time scrub shows what one place gave up in turn: Katanga copper then cobalt; Aral fish, then cotton water, then dust. Rule (module file row 5.4): people are drawn as people with a content note, never as arrows of goods. |
| Concept | 600 words. Extraction here means taking from a place more than it restores, for use elsewhere. Four measures: stock (what is taken), labour (who does it, on what terms), distance (how far the use is from the pit) and waste (what stays behind). Three worked examples: Bengal indigo under planters' contracts (1859–60 revolt); synthetic indigo (1897), which moved the weight rather than removing it; the Aral Sea, where a water system was spent on cotton. Then the counting problem: a company study, an NGO report and a UN monitor count different things. Rule: every number carries a source and a date, and the unit never ranks harms across worlds. |
| Cases | **1. Red rubber to cobalt (F1.6).** The Congo Free State (1885–1908) enforced rubber collection by violence and hostage-taking. Two readings of how red rubber ended: reformers' pressure, or the exhaustion of the rubber supply by 1906 (Harms, 1975). The same country now mines most of the world's cobalt; Amnesty (2016) traced artisanal cobalt through traders and a processing company to battery makers. No open object: our own diagram. **2. The Great Heap (F1.7).** NYPL photograph, Chincha Islands, 1865, captioned "2,000,000 tons guano". Guano was dug by enslaved people, convicts and Chinese contract workers; nearly 100,000 Chinese men arrived in Peru in 1849–1874. Then Chile's saltpetre works, where workers from Chile, Peru and Bolivia made nitrate fertiliser. Strip: Potosí silver under the mita (link F1.13). **3. A cover with a cotton ground (F1.12a).** Cleveland 2016.89, a suzani from Shakhrisabz, 1800–1850: silk embroidery on six strips of cotton cloth, made for a dowry. Then the cotton: American-type cotton from the 1880s; Soviet river diversions in the 1960s; the Aral Sea at about 10% of its 1960 area by 2014. NASA's images (2000–2018) are the map layer. Strip: rare earths at Bayan Obo, near Baotou. |
| Apply — maker | **The material chain of one object you own.** Pick one object. Trace three materials to three places. For each link, give one number with its source and date, name who does the work, and write one line on what the number leaves out. Then propose one design change and say where it moves the weight. Output: a chain map and a 150-word PLW entry. |
| Apply — briefer | **What the brief must ask the supply chain.** 200 words for a supplier you would hire. List the materials and the places you need named. Say which numbers you require, with dates and who measured them. Split the brief into three lists: known and audited, reported by the supplier, and unknown. Tag each sentence "measured by [who, when]", "reported by [company]" or "assumed". |
| Partner | **Planet.** Agenda: it speaks for places and long timescales, and it distrusts numbers without dates. Question: "What did this take from where, and what did the number leave out?" It never supplies a figure from memory and never judges the learner's purchase. **Test it ends with:** every link in the learner's chain names a place, a source and a date; at least one link carries a "left out" line; any undated figure is returned marked "undated". |
| Forms | 12 (map), 4 (explorable). |
| Consumption forms | **Five-minute:** the Aral map scrub, 2000 to 2018, with a 90-second narration and transcript. **Session (about 55 min):** opener, concept, chain explorer with three cases, one Apply lane. **Reference:** claims sheet, a numbers register (figure, source, date, what it counts), nodes list, protocol screen. |
| Media | A map (form 12) with layers and a table view of every point. A chain explorer (form 4) with three case chains and the jeans worked example. NASA image pairs for the Aral Sea and Bayan Obo. Four open records. Our own chain diagrams. No photographs of injured or dead people. |
| Artifact fields | **PLW:** first entry in the ledger: object, three materials, three places, one dated number each, and what each number leaves out. **SRC:** the source and date behind every number. |
| Threads | **Planetary Weight** (owner unit in F1; seeds the PLW ledger that F5 continues). **Attribution and Authority:** who counts (company, NGO, UN monitor, survey) and who is left uncounted. **Writing and Argument:** two readings of how red rubber ended. **Accessibility level 1:** every map point also in a table; colour never the only carrier; alt text for every image pair. |
| Closing contribution | SRC line: "Object: [ ]. Material: [ ] from [place]. Number: [ ] ([source], [date]). Who did the work: [ ]. What the number leaves out: [ ]." |
| Claims sheet | 20 claims below: 16 documented, 1 probable, 2 contested (both readings given), 1 interpretive (the unit's framing). Every URL was opened on 29 September 2026. |

**2. Protocol screen**

**Excluded, and why:**

- **Reform-campaign photographs of mutilated Congolese people:** injured people who had no say in the images' use. Named, never shown.
- **Burials of contract workers or miners:** human remains. Never shown, never linked.
- **Images of child miners** in Congo: identifiable minors. Amnesty's text is linked; its photographs are not reproduced.

**Shown, with basis and content note:**

- **NYPL "The Great Heap" (1865):** the diggings; check for identifiable workers. Content note: coerced and contract labour. Chinese workers are named as workers, never by price or as cargo.
- **Cleveland suzani 2016.89:** a household textile, made for a dowry. Notice: Uzbek and Tajik embroidery is a living practice (PRA162).
- **Wellcome 31355i (1778):** an engraving made in England of an indigo works, with an overseer watching. Content note: the workers are unnamed, and the catalogue's place ("India") is to be confirmed.
- **Aral Sea:** Notice for Karakalpak communities (COM212) on the former shore. Health effects are stated only as the sources state them.
- **Potosí:** Notice for Quechua- and Aymara-speaking communities; the mita is named as forced labour.
- **Congo cases:** content note on violence, hostage-taking and child labour. The phrase "red rubber" is the campaigners' term, in quotation marks.
