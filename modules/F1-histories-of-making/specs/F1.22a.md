## F1.22a Use and consumption: how things were lived with, worn out, repaired, resold, inherited

An object's history does not stop when its maker lets go: use, wear, repair, resale and inheritance leave marks, and those marks are evidence.

**Spec**

| Row | Content |
|---|---|
| Essential question | What happened to this object after it left its maker, who did it, and what marks did that life leave? |
| Opening move | **Object first.** A small celadon tea bowl crossed by gold seams (Met 16.122.5, 13th century). Prompt: "Where was this made, and where was it used?" **Claim:** the glaze says China; the Met gives the Longquan kilns. **Counter-claim:** the seams say Japan; the Met reads kintsugi, "a technique unique to Japan", as a sign the bowl was "used and cherished there". In China, broken ceramics were usually stapled. The reveal: both. Made in one world, lived with in another. Case: the Bakōhan bowl, sent from Japan to China for a replacement and returned with a metal clamp (a record of 1727). |
| Lab or main interactive | **Use-life scrub** (form 5). The learner drags between two states of each object: as made and as it reached us. Each mark is tagged to one of seven stages: made, used, worn, repaired, altered, passed on (sold, inherited, given as dowry), kept. Every tag cites a line in the record. "Not in the record" is a valid tag and is counted. The strip becomes the template for LCY. |
| Concept | 650 words. **The use-life:** seven stages; most records describe only the first and last. **Wear as evidence:** an Egyptian artificial toe with "considerable signs of wear" (Manchester, 2011). **Repair traditions:** gold lacquer and staples; darning samplers, on which schoolgirls learned to mend; kantha stitched from worn saris; ger felt shaped on an old "mother felt". **Second lives:** *salaula*, Zambia's second-hand clothes; a blanket bought as a rug; a cassone pair split between two museums. **Inheritance and dowry:** the cassone; kantha. **Four lenses:** the household as a site of making; furniture and interiors (the cassone, the Turkmen tent's bags); play and the child (babies wrapped in kantha, children who learn to raise the ger); body and access (the Egyptian toes, the Jaipur foot). **Repair as law:** the EU directive of 2024. Rule: we describe use from records and users' own sources, and never rank thrift above display. |
| Cases | **1. The house that moves (F1.12a).** The Mongol ger is made, struck and raised by a household; children learn by watching (UNESCO, 2013 and 2024). New felt is shaped on the old *ekh esgii*, "mother felt". Object story: a Turkmen tent door hanging (ensi), Met 22.100.42, 19th century, made for a home that had to be "collapsible and portable". Strip: the chuval storage bag (Met 22.100.40a); Kyrgyz felt carpets (UNESCO, 2012). **2. The blanket that became a rug (F1.7; Diné).** Form-5 pair: a wearing blanket by a Diné woman artist (Cleveland 1921.556, c. 1875–80) and a rug modelled on the "chief blanket" (Cleveland 1937.903, c. 1900). Behind the change: the Long Walk and Bosque Redondo, 1864–68; the 1894 wool-tariff removal; traders who bought weaving by weight into the 1960s. Content note. **3. A quilt of worn saris (F1.9).** Kantha, Cleveland 1996.350, Bengal, 19th century: made by women for their own homes, used to wrap babies, part of some brides' dowries. Dowry strip: a Florentine cassone (Met 15.119.4), carried in the wedding procession with the bride's dowry; its top was later replaced. Resale strip: *salaula*, where Zambian tailors refit other people's clothes (F1.6). |
| Apply — maker | **The use-life of your anchor.** Write your anchor's use-life in the seven stages, 30 words each. Mark the first place it will wear, and draw that spot new and worn (form 5). Name the repair, who does it, and what parts and tools they need. Name its second life: resold, handed down, given or thrown away. Output: the LCY field and one drawing pair. |
| Apply — briefer | **What the brief must say about use.** 200 words for a maker you would hire. Who uses the object, for how long, and where it will wear. Who may repair it, and with what parts, tools and instructions. What happens when it passes on or is thrown away. Mark each line "known", "told by [user]", "read in [source]" or "assumed". |
| Partner | **The Mirror.** It reflects the learner's use-life back. Question: "You described the object on the day it is finished. Who holds it in year ten, and what are they doing to it?" It counts stages that name a person. It flags passive stages ("it will be repaired") and use described only by the designer. **Test:** the learner names one wear point and one repair, each with the person who does it; one stage where the object leaves their control; and one source on how their users actually live with such things. |
| Forms | 3 (object story: opener plus three cases), 5 (before/after: as made and as found). |
| Consumption forms | **Five-minute:** the tea bowl before/after, with audio description and transcript. **Session (about 50 min):** opener, concept, three object stories with use-life scrub, one Apply lane. **Reference:** claims sheet, use-life template, nodes list, protocol screen. |
| Media | Three form-5 pairs: the bowl against our drawing with the seams traced; blanket and rug, with our diagram of placement (shoulders, floor); the cassone with our overlay on the replaced top. Four object stories with audio description. Our own diagrams of the ger's parts and the mother-felt cycle, and a map of the six chapters. |
| Artifact fields | **LCY:** users, wear points, repair route (who, parts, tools), second life (resale, inheritance, gift), end. **SRC:** how each use claim was learned. |
| Threads | Planetary Weight (repair lengthens use; links to F1.22's PLW). Attribution and Authority (menders, lacquerers, Ram Chandra Sharma). Accessibility level 1 (the body-and-access lens; audio description; transcripts). Writing and Argument (two readings of where two vessels were repaired). |
| Closing contribution | SRC line: "Use-life of [anchor]: users; wear point; who repairs it; where it goes next. How I know: [record / told by / read in / assumed]. The stage no record covers." |
| Claims sheet | 20 claims below: 15 documented, 2 documented with a probable part, 1 probable, 1 contested (both readings), 1 interpretive (the framing). Every URL was opened; Cleveland records were read through the open-access API. |

**2. Protocol screen**

**Excluded, and why:**

- **The Cairo toe:** found on a woman's body buried near Luxor. Named, never shown.
- **Diné ceremonial textiles:** CARE rule from the Americas chapter. Only a wearing blanket and a trade rug are shown.
- **Pazyryk felts and saddle (ARC108):** from burials, in a state collection without an open licence. Named only.
- **Cleveland 1960.274, "Ceremonial Quilt":** its rite is not recorded. We use 1996.350 instead.

**Shown, with basis and content note:**

- **Longquan bowl, cassone, Turkmen textiles, kantha:** household objects. Kantha and cassone carry a dowry note: dowry is a painful subject in parts of South Asia; brides are never described as part of the transfer.
- **Diné blanket and rug:** made for wear and sale. **Notice:** Diné material, living community; the museum names no weaver. **Content note:** the Long Walk and Bosque Redondo. People are named as people, never as goods.
- **Greville Chester toe:** link out only; a funerary context is likely but not verified.

**8. Success criteria**

1. For one object, the learner fills four or more use-life stages, each tagged to a record line, and marks the rest "not in the record".
2. For the tea bowl, the learner states where it was made and where it was probably used, with the evidence for each.
3. The learner's LCY entry or brief names one wear point, one repair with its repairer, and one next owner or destination.
