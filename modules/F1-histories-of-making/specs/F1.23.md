## F1.23 Obscured labour: the credit map

The name on an object usually belongs to whoever owned, ruled or sold, and the hands that made it were left off by law, force or habit.

**Spec**

| Row | Content |
|---|---|
| Essential question | Whose name is on this object, who made it, and what kept the makers' names off it? |
| Opening move | **Object first.** Three marks appear, cropped from their objects: a reign mark in blue (Cleveland 1964.213.1), a reversed stamp in clay (Cleveland 1916.2009) and a signature cut into a jar, "Dave" (Met 2020.7). The learner answers "Whose name is this?" for each, then sees the whole object. **Claim:** "The name on an object is the name of its maker." **Counter-claim:** the reign mark names an emperor; the stamp pairs an owner with a man whose status scholars dispute; the signature was cut by an enslaved potter where teaching enslaved people to write was banned. Opening case: the Dave jar, the one signed by its maker. Content note first. |
| Lab | **The credit map** (explorable). The learner places every hand around one object in three rings: **named**, **grouped** (only a trade, a people or "fifty workmen"), **not recorded**. A marker shows **who took the credit** (king, reign, owner, firm, famous name). Two worked maps come first: Darius I's Susa charter (F1.9a), which names peoples by craft and credits the king, and the Strike Papyrus (F1.6), where the tomb-builders' own words survive. The learner then maps the Kangxi dish from d'Entrecolles and Song Yingxing. Every hand needs a source or "not known". People are drawn as people, never as arrows of flow. |
| Concept | About 700 words, four ways credit is lost. **To power:** Darius "built this palace"; a reign mark; an owner's stamp. **To one name:** Tiffany over Clara Driscoll's department; Aleijadinho over his enslaved assistants; Boucher over his fifty workmen. **By coercion:** slavery (Edgefield, Arezzo), the plantation (GAP071), deportation (Karakorum, Tabriz; contested for the kurtaš), transportation (Australian convict sites), incarceration (Japanese American camps, link F1.19a). **By category:** "outsider art" and disabled makers (Judith Scott, Aleijadinho); "women's work" (link F1.18). It closes on traces makers left: Dave's verses, convict love tokens, the strike's quoted words. Not known: most makers' own view of the credit. |
| Cases | Three object stories. **1. "Dave", 1858 (F1.7).** A 25-gallon storage jar from the Stony Bluff Manufactory, Old Edgefield District, South Carolina, signed "Dave" and inscribed with its buyer and a couplet (Met 2020.7, open). Link out to "Hear Me Now" (2022–23), where the face vessels' makers are "unrecorded". Content note. **2. Two names in one stamp (F1.11).** An Arretine bowl mould, 27 BCE–14 CE, stamped "M. PERENN / TIGAANI" in reverse (Cleveland 1916.2009, open), paired with a finished bowl "signed by Perennius Tigranus as owner" (Met 10.210.37, open). The museum credits the owner; the stamp keeps a worker's name; whether that man was enslaved is given in both readings. Content note. **3. Seventy hands, one reign (F1.8).** A famille-verte dish, Jingdezhen, 1662–1722, marked "Made in the Qing dynasty, Kangxi reign", with no creator listed (Cleveland 1964.213.1, open). D'Entrecolles (1712) wrote that one piece "passes through the hands of seventy workers", and dismissed the painters as he counted them. |
| Apply — maker | **Credit map of your own project.** Map every hand behind one piece of your own work: collaborators, assistants, fabricators, factory or print shop, material source, software and its makers, any training data, the client. Place each in named, grouped or not recorded. Mark who takes the credit on the final label. Write "not known" where you cannot find a hand. Add one confidence word per line. |
| Apply — briefer | **Credit map for a commission.** For a project you would commission, map the hands before it starts. Then write the brief's credit clause: which hands the supplier must disclose (designer, studio assistants, factory, subcontractors, material origin), how each is credited, and what evidence the supplier must give that no hand works under force. Ask what the final label will say, and who is left off it. |
| Partner | **Historian.** Agenda: the record keeps those who paid, ruled or owned; its gaps are evidence too. Question: "Who is missing from this credit, and how do you know they were there?" Test: the learner names one hand the record leaves out (the fifty workmen, the Edgefield face-vessel makers, the dish's painters), cites the document that shows the hand existed, and writes one sentence with a confidence word. |

**2. Protocol screen**

- **Human remains:** none shown. The Valley of the Kings appears only as a plan of our own; no tomb interior. The Newton Plantation burial ground (PLC154) is not used.
- **Altar and votive objects:** the Met stela 21.2.6 is a hymn to Amun-Re's barque. It is shown with its origin ("probably from Thebes") and a note that it was made for worship.
- **People never described as goods:** write "enslaved potter", "enslaved workers", "deported artisans", "transported convicts". Never "slaves" as stock, never "units", never flows of people drawn like trade arrows. Where a source says "slave", quote and attribute it (Bryn Mawr; Traina). "Fifty workmen" is Rubruck's phrase; quote it.
- **Perpetrators' and period terms in quotation marks, attributed:** "internment" (use "incarceration", after Densho); "convict stain" (Powerhouse); "Aleijadinho" means "little cripple": explain once, then use his name as the record does; "outsider art".

**3. Claims**

1. Met 2020.7 is a storage jar dated 1858, Stony Bluff Manufactory, Old Edgefield District, signed "Dave" with a couplet; the Met calls him "the enslaved African American potter and poet David Drake". The 2022–23 "Hear Me Now" show had about 50 objects, including face vessels "whose makers were unrecorded". *Documented.* https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/747045; https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/edgefield. Depth: full record; full page.
2. Drake was born about 1800 (the Met says ca. 1801) and enslaved by five men, the last Lewis Miles. Twenty of his vessels carry verses and 50 more his signature or date. South Carolina law barred teaching enslaved people to write (1740, and again in the 1830s). *Documented.* https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Drake_(potter). Depth: summary; the statutes not opened.
3. Roman red-gloss stamps name the people in production: masters, freedmen and enslaved workers. One Bryn Mawr sherd sets "a slave's name, Nicephorus" over his owner's. *Documented.* Fülle 1997, https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-roman-studies/article/abs/internal-organization-of-the-arretine-terra-sigillata-industry-problems-of-evidence-and-interpretation/73AD21990173E86AA8F2C6968CC49845 (abstract and first page); https://specialcollections.blogs.brynmawr.edu/2011/10/12/bryn-mawr-college%E2%80%99s-collection-of-roman-terra-sigillata/ (full text).
4. Cleveland 1916.2009 carries the reversed stamp "M. PERENN | TIGAANI" and credits "M. Perennius" as creator. Met 10.210.37 is "signed by Perennius Tigranus as owner". *Documented.* https://clevelandart.org/art/1916.2009; https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/248324. Depth: full records.
5. Whether Tigranus and Bargathes were enslaved is *contested.* Reading one: most scholars see them as Armenian war captives. Reading two: Oxé argued they were free, skilled men. The workshop passed to Tigranus around 15 BCE. Traina 2010, https://www.academia.edu/1574885/Tigranus_e_Bargathes_due_armeni_ad_Arretium_in_G_Camporeale_G_Firpo_%C3%A9d_Arezzo_nellantichit%C3%A0_Giorgio_Bretschneider_Rome_2010_p_217_s. Depth: summary of the page; full article not read.
6. Cleveland 1964.213.1 is marked "Da Qing Kangxi nian zhi (Made in the Qing dynasty, Kangxi reign)"; no creator is listed. *Documented.* https://clevelandart.org/art/1964.213.1. Depth: full record.
7. D'Entrecolles wrote in 1712 that "one piece of fired porcelain passes through the hands of seventy workers". He called the painters "little less destitute than the other workers" and belittled their skill. *Documented* (in translation). https://www.gotheborg.com/letters/letters_first.shtml. Depth: excerpts; translator not stated in the excerpt.
8. Song Yingxing's *Tiangong kaiwu* (1637) says a piece "過手七十二，方克成器": it passes through 72 hands before it is complete. *Documented* (secondary). https://www.163.com/dy/article/L2P0QC8B05346936.html; date and author, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiangong_Kaiwu. Depth: summary; the original not opened.
9. The Strike Papyrus (Turin, Cat. 1880), by the scribe Amennakht, records year 29 of Ramesses III: the crew shouted "we are hungry!" after 18 days without rations, their pay for work in the Valley of the Kings. *Documented.* https://collezioni.museoegizio.it/en-GB/material/Cat_1880. Depth: full record.
10. Darius I's Susa charter names crafts by people ("The stone-cutters who wrought the stone, those were Yaunâ and Lydians") and credits the king: "I built this palace". *Documented.* https://www.livius.org/sources/content/achaemenid-royal-inscriptions/dsf/ (after Lecoq 1997). Depth: full text in translation.
11. Who the kurtaš were is *contested.* Reading one (Briant): workers "recruited and paid by the state", not enslaved captives. Reading two (Izdimirski 2018): the kurtaš included deportees resettled as dependent labour. https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/class-system-ii/ (full text); https://antiquitasviva.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/68.1-2.06.-izdimirski-m.-deportations-of-kurtash-workers-in-the-achaemenid-empire-according-to-classical-literary-tradition.pdf (abstract and conclusion).
12. In the Persepolis tablets, skilled women and men got equal rations; less-skilled women got a third less. Mothers of boys got twice the extra ration of mothers of girls. *Documented.* Brosius, https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/women-i/. Depth: full text.
13. Rubruck met "a certain master goldsmith called William, a Parisian by birth" at Karakorum in 1254. Möngke gave him 300 *iascot* "and fifty workmen". He had been captured in 1241 or 1242. *Documented.* https://medievalslavery.org/notes-from-the-field/high-school/dbq-slavery-and-the-mongol-expansion/ (excerpt of the primary text; edition not named); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guillaume_Boucher (summary). Rockhill 1900 not opened at the passage.
14. After Çaldıran (1514), artisans from Tabriz went to Istanbul and were entered in the Ehl-i hiref salary books (1526–1566) by name and pay. *Probable* as to force: the abstract says "brought"; the canon (OCC055) says "deportation". Give both readings. Uluskan 2021, https://belleten.gov.tr/eng/abstarct/3669/eng. Depth: abstract.
15. About 166,000 men, women and children were transported to Australia, 1787–1868, with "forced labour to help build the colony" (UNESCO, 2010). The Hyde Park Barracks (1817–19) were designed by the convict architect Francis Greenway and built by convicts. *Documented.* https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1306/ (full page); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyde_Park_Barracks,_Sydney (summary).
16. About 350 convict love tokens are known. The Powerhouse calls them "one of the few classes of items convicts made … purely of their own volition". On 2003/40/1 "the maker is not known". *Documented.* https://collection.powerhouse.com.au/object/12096. Depth: full record.
17. Aleijadinho (born c. 1738) was the son of Isabel, an enslaved African woman. He worked with three enslaved assistants, Maurício, Agostinho and Januário. His illness from about 1777 is undiagnosed; how disabled he was is *contested*. Reading one: Bretas (1858) says severely. Reading two: 1796 receipts show a "firm and clear" hand. Attributions grew from 163 (1951) to 425 (2006), mostly without documents. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleijadinho. Depth: summary.
18. Clara Driscoll headed Tiffany Studios' Women's Glass Cutting Department and designed over thirty lamps, credited to Tiffany until her letters were found around 2005. The Met's records still list "Louis C. Tiffany (Artist)" first and "Possibly Clara Driscoll (Designer)". *Documented.* https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clara_Driscoll_(glass_designer) (summary); https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/16476 (full record).
19. Judith Scott (1943–2005), born deaf and with Down syndrome, was institutionalised for 35 years. From 1987 at Creative Growth, Oakland, she made over 200 fibre sculptures. A 2014 Brooklyn Museum retrospective showed her as a contemporary artist. *Documented.* https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judith_Scott_(artist). Depth: summary.
20. **The unit's framing:** credit follows ownership and power more often than hands, and coercion is one of the main ways names are removed. *Interpretive.*

Supporting, not counted: "The Art of Gaman" showed objects by incarcerated Japanese Americans, "largely shopkeepers and farmers and gardeners and fishermen"; Densho uses "incarceration" (https://encyclopedia.densho.org/The_Art_of_Gaman:_Arts_and_Crafts_from_the_Japanese_American_Internment_Camps,_1942-1946_(exhibition)/). The Met stela 21.2.6 names "the scribe Amennakht, his son Pentwere, and the chief carpenter, Amenemope" (https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/544766); whether this Amennakht wrote the Strike Papyrus is not verified.

**8. Success criteria**

1. The learner's credit map places at least six hands in the named, grouped and not-recorded rings, each with a source or "not known".
2. For one case, the learner quotes the mark or record that carries the credit and names who it credits.
3. The learner rewrites one label (museum, shop or their own) to add a missing hand, with a confidence word.
