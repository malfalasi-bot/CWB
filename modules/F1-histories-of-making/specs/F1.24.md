## F1.24 The museum as author: collecting, provenance, restitution

A museum authors an object's second life through what it collects, records and returns, and every link it writes can be checked.

**Spec**

| Row | Content |
|---|---|
| Essential question | Who writes an object's story after it leaves its makers, and how do you check what they wrote? |
| Opening move | **Argument first (form 13).** Three records, one event. Cleveland 1999.1: "Sent to England by Sir Ralph Moor following the Siege of Benin (1897)". AIC 1933.782: "taken during the British military raid by George W. Neville". Smithsonian (2022): "the royal palace was burned and looted, and the oba (ruler) was exiled." **Claim:** a provenance record is a neutral list of owners. **Counter-claim:** it is authored; each museum chooses the verb for how the object left. Prompt: "Pick the words you would print, and cite what supports them." One tap on one of the three wordings; the reveal follows within seconds and shows what supports each: each wording matches its source; the choice of verb is the museum's. Case: the Benin plaques. |
| Lab or main interactive | **Provenance query.** Six open records (Cleveland, AIC) and three register rows (WS-092, a Luristan cheekpiece in Cleveland since 1961; WS-X03; WS-043). The learner splits each provenance field into dated links: made, held, removed, sold, given, returned. Silences are marked "not recorded"; brackets and "said to be from" are flagged as the museum's own doubt. Two columns: the museums' 1970 test (AAMD, 17 November 1970) and "how did it leave its makers?" They disagree. The Benin plaques pass the test and were taken in 1897; the stag plaque and the Luristan bit fail it and tell no story. The Luristan bit gets a short object story here. The query reads the Atlas's provenance edges ("a query, not a drawing", module file). |
| Concept | 700 words. Three acts of authorship. **Collecting:** commission, gift, purchase, taking in war, excavation, the market, salvage. **Writing the chain:** provenance as a genre (dated links, brackets, agents, "said to be from"), its silences, the label's verb. **Returning:** restitution to a state, repatriation to a community, transfer of ownership with a loan back, and "to whom?" The rules: UNESCO 1970 (not retroactive), the museums' 1970 test, NAGPRA (1990; revised 2024), national law as the Dutch courts applied it. Two positions, both sourced: the "universal museum" declaration (2002) and "a new relational ethics" (Sarr and Savoy, 2018). Then brands: some heritage claims are certified by law (Harris Tweed); others borrow a community's name (the "Navajo" case). Rule: we teach how to read and write a chain; we do not decide who owns an object. |
| Cases | **1. A plaque, two chains, one guild (F1.6).** Cleveland 1999.1 and AIC 1933.782, brass, 1500s–1600s, cast by the Igun Eronmwon for the Oba's palace. Both chains pass through 1897. Cleveland's runs through the British Museum, which sold it in 1950; AIC's through Neville's heirs and a London sale in 1930. Coda, to whom: Germany transferred ownership of 1,117 objects to Nigeria in 2022; a 2023 presidential declaration named the Oba owner and custodian. The guild still casts on Igun Street (PR-003; link F1.6). **2. Gold that waited in Amsterdam (F1.12a).** In 2014 the Allard Pierson showed 565 objects lent by four Crimean museums, among them a 4th-century BCE gold helmet. Russia annexed Crimea during the loan. The Crimean museums, backed by Russia, and the Ukrainian state both claimed them. Three Dutch courts sent them to Ukraine; they reached Kyiv on 26 November 2023. Beside it: Cleveland's Scythian stag plaque (1985.77), bought in 1985, no owner published. Same culture, two paper trails. **3. Two returns (F1.12; F1.7).** In 2024 Trinity College Cambridge returned four spears taken at Kamay (Botany Bay) in 1770 to the La Perouse Aboriginal Community. The British Museum's shield, long linked to the same day, is "not one collected by Cook". Strip: Gothenburg returned 140 Paracas textiles to Peru (2014–2021); Cleveland's Paracas mantle (1946.226) has a chain that starts in Cleveland. |
| Apply — maker | **A provenance chain.** One object in your world (an heirloom, a tool, a second-hand chair) or one Lab record. Write its chain as dated links: who made it, who held it, how it moved (made, bought, given, inherited, taken, found), with a source for each. Mark each gap "not recorded"; never fill it. Output: a chain diagram and 150 words on the link you are least sure of. |
| Apply — briefer | **What to ask a museum partner.** 200 words to a museum you would borrow from, license from or co-brand with. Ask where the chain is published and how the object left its makers. Ask who has asked for its return, and who speaks for the makers' descendants, with credit and pay. Ask for the image licence. Add one line on your own copy: which heritage claims are certified (Harris Tweed's Orb) and which borrow a name you hold no right to. Mark each answer "published", "told by [museum]" or "not answered". |
| Partner | **Historian.** Question: "Who wrote this chain, from which papers, and where is it silent?" It cites records and rulings and never says who should own an object. Asked "should this go back?", it lists the claims on record and who has made them. **Test it ends with:** every link or answer carries a source; any without one comes back marked "assumed". |

**2. Protocol screen**

**Excluded, and why:**

- **Paracas mummy bundles; Pazyryk and other frozen burials (ARC108):** human remains. Named only.
- **Benin ancestral-altar heads** (for example AIC 2003.16): not needed; the plaques carry the case.
- **NAGPRA material and the closed halls:** ancestors and sacred objects. Told in words only.
- **The Kamay spears and shield:** Aboriginal material; the spears were handed to the La Perouse Aboriginal Community on 23 April 2024. Link-out only, to the community's and AIATSIS's pages (https://aiatsis.gov.au/whats-new/news/return-gweagal-spears-la-perouse-aboriginal-community), with a Cultural note (our own wording; not a Local Contexts Notice): "Cultural note: the La Perouse Aboriginal Community, including Gweagal descendants, speaks for these spears. The La Perouse Local Aboriginal Land Council calls them 'an important connection to our past'."
- **Diné designs on the disputed products:** not reproduced.

**Shown, with display basis and content note:**

- **Benin plaques (Cleveland 1999.1; AIC 1933.782):** palace plaques, not altar objects. Content note: "In 1897 British forces invaded Benin City, looted and burned the Oba's palace, and exiled Oba Ovonramwen. Nigeria's 2023 declaration calls it 'the Benin Massacre of 1897'; British accounts called it a 'punitive expedition'." The brass came partly through Atlantic trade that also sold people; they are named as people, never as goods.
- **Scythian stag plaque and wolf (Cleveland 1985.77, 1986.26):** canon flags Scythian material as funerary (CIV087). Content note: findspots not recorded; possibly from burials.
- **Luristan bit (Cleveland 1980.102):** content note on looting; ARC058 notes most were dug from graves.
- **Paracas mantle (Cleveland 1946.226):** from burials. Cultural note: Andean Indigenous material. Content note: burial and the 1930s export.
- **Crimean objects:** linked out. Content note: "Russia's annexation of Crimea in 2014 (the word Ukraine, the Dutch press and most states use; the Allard Pierson wrote 'afgescheiden', separated) and the war since 2022. Some of these objects may be grave goods; this is not verified object by object."
