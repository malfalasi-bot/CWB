## F1.25 Reading an object as history: slow looking and the autopsy

An object read slowly, in five layers (material, technique, labour, trade, meaning), shows a web of hands and routes; its museum record is one more source, with its own authors and gaps.

**Spec**

| Row | F1.25 |
|---|---|
| Essential question | How much of an object's history can you read from the thing itself, and how much comes from a record someone else wrote? |
| Opening move | **Lab first, as station 1 of the tour (form 15, then 1 and 4).** A small gilt bronze buckle fills the screen with no label (Cleveland 1952.115). A slow-looking timer runs: look for two minutes, note ten words, look again, note ten more (the notes are the learner's own and stay on device; after Project Zero's "Ten Times Two", linked, not copied). Then the unit's one prediction, one tap on three options: who cast it? Casters in the Ordos steppe; a Chinese workshop for steppe clients; not recorded. The reveal follows within seconds: the record's "Ordos region, Western Han dynasty" allows both readings (claim 8). **Claim:** "Look long enough and the object tells you its history." **Counter-claim:** "The bronze shows two long-eared animals. Who cast it, for whom, and where it was buried are not on it, and not in its record either." **Case:** the buckle (case A). |
| Lab or main interactive | **Object Autopsy, reading mode.** The buckle opens with its five layers already filled and shown, each sentence tagged seen / recorded / guessed by us, with its source or "not recorded". *Material*: what it is made of, where that came from, what taking it cost (Planetary Weight enters here). *Technique*: the operational-sequence strip, shown as documented steps and "not recorded"; after the explanation, the strip reopens as an optional one-drag ordering puzzle. *Labour*: every hand, named or described. *Trade*: the routes the material and object travelled, on the map. *Meaning*: what it did for its users, and who says so. The guess boxes are gone: nothing is reconstructed before it is shown. The tagging stays, because here it is the skill: the learner re-tags any of our sentences they read differently, then writes and tags their own autopsy (Apply). Under the layers sits the **provenance chain** as a tappable strip, and the "what was happening elsewhere" panel on the date, fed by the Time lens. **Practice Reading** is the sibling tab for a living practice: who holds it, how it is taught, what it needs to continue. |
| Concept | About 650 words. (1) Slow looking as a method: Tishman's definition and four moves (decomposition, perspective shifting, collaborative observation, iterative noticing). (2) Five layers, and why technique was added (finding 4.1): an object is also a sequence of gestures (Leroi-Gourhan, Lemonnier). (3) Seen, recorded, guessed: the record is a source, written by a cataloguer with a date. (4) The provenance chain as the object's biography (Kopytoff), and the 1970 line. (5) Practice Reading: when the knowledge lives in people, not in a thing. Forward to F1.28 (grading the record), F1.28a (reconstruction), F1.29 (oral history), F4.12 (material dossier). |

| Apply — maker | **A full autopsy of an object you use in your work** (a tool, a material sample, a reference piece). | Apply — briefer | **A full autopsy of an object your organisation sells, gifts or cites as "heritage".** Same five layers. Then mark each "not known" that a supplier or maker could answer, and write it as one question. The answers belong to the supplier and maker; the learner records only what they publish or consent to share. |
| Partner | **The Mirror.** It reflects the autopsy back: "Here is what your reading is doing." It counts sentences per tag and per layer. It says when a layer is all guesses, and when owners and donors are named but makers are not. It does not judge the object. Question: "Your autopsy names [n] people. How many of them made it?" **Test:** the learner turns one guessed sentence into a question with a named source to check, and names one hand their reading leaves out. |

| Narration script | **Station 1, the buckle, unlabelled.** A small gilt bronze buckle fills the screen, with no label [claim 5]. Look at it for two minutes and note ten words; then look again and note ten more. The routine follows Project Zero's "Ten Times Two", which we link and do not copy [claim 2]. You can see two long-eared animals. Our record does not name the species, so we will not [section 10]. Shari Tishman defines slow looking as "observing detail over time to move beyond a first impression" [claim 1]. Now one tap. Who cast this: casters in the Ordos steppe, a Chinese workshop working for steppe clients, or not recorded? The record says "China, Ordos region, Western Han dynasty", 202 BCE to 9 CE [claim 5]. That wording allows either workshop, and the question is argued [claim 8]. The tour now reads the object in five layers and tags every sentence seen, recorded or guessed [claim 4]. **Station 2, material.** The buckle is bronze with a gold surface [claim 5]. In China, gilding with mercury came into general use around the Warring States period and was common in Han noble tombs [claim 6]. The gilders knew the vapour harmed them [claim 6]. Whether this buckle was gilded that way has not been tested, so for this object we write "probably" and tag the sentence guessed [claim 6]. The material layer also asks what the taking cost: what was mined, and what was burned off in the gilding [Threads row; claim 6]. Those costs are not in the record. They stay in the layer as a question. **Station 3, technique.** An object is also a sequence of gestures. Lemonnier, after Leroi-Gourhan, calls it "the series of operations involved in any transformation of matter" [claim 3]. The strip on screen shows the steps our drawing documents for gilt bronze of this kind. A wax model, a mould, a pour, a finish, then the gilded surface [section 7; claim 6]. Where the record is silent, the step reads "not recorded" [claim 4]. Nothing here is for you to guess before you have seen it. When the tour ends, the strip reopens as a one-drag puzzle, after this explanation and never before it [Lab row]. **Station 4, labour.** Who touched it? The record names Vladimir G. Simkhovitch of New York, who owned it, and the museum, which has held it since 1952 [claim 5]. It names no caster and no gilder [section 6]. So the labour layer holds two kinds of hand. The work describes some: a modeller, a caster, a gilder. The archive names others: an owner and a museum. The Mirror will count them later and ask how many of the named people made it [Partner row]. **Station 5, trade.** The map shows the Ordos region and the Han frontier beside it [section 7]. Gold belt plaques from Xigoupan tomb 2 in the Ordos, 4th to 3rd century BCE, are attributed to a Qin state workshop [claim 7]. A workshop inside a Chinese state made plaques in steppe taste for steppe clients, two centuries before our buckle. That is the precedent for one reading [claim 8]. The other reading stands beside it: casters in the steppe made such pieces for their own people [claim 8]. The map draws both routes and marks both "argued". Beside the buckle sits the Met's gilded plaque pair of the 1st century, which its record calls Xianbei [section 5]. We do not choose between the readings. **Station 6, meaning.** What did it do? Its title says it fastened a belt; that is recorded, not seen [section 5]. For whom, the record is silent [claim 5]. The Met's record frames its own pair. They "once adorned a leather belt belonging to a member of a nomadic community, likely the Xianbei, that migrated seasonally" [claim 9]. That is the holder's framing, and we quote it as the holder's. The animals' species are not in our record, so the meaning layer says "not known" where an easy sentence would name a deer [section 10]. Meaning is the layer most often made of guesses, and the tags show it. **Station 7, the provenance chain.** Content note: belt plaques like this are known mostly from burials. This buckle's findspot is not recorded, so it may have been taken from a grave [section 2]. No images of burials are shown. You can skip this station; the summary keeps the dates. The chain has two links: Vladimir G. Simkhovitch, New York, then the Cleveland Museum of Art in 1952 [claim 5]. Before Simkhovitch, nothing. Where it was found, who sold it and how it left China are not recorded [section 5]. The 1970 line passes because the museum held it by 1952; the test does not see the gap before that [section 5]. The chain is the object's biography as the archive wrote it, and this archive starts late. **Station 8, elsewhere at this date.** The timeline widens to the band 202 BCE to 9 CE [claim 5]. Within this unit it holds three neighbours. The Han noble tombs where mercury gilding was common [claim 6]. The Xigoupan plaques, two centuries earlier [claim 7]. The Met's Xianbei plaques, a century later [section 5]. The rest of the row belongs to the Time lens: what else was being made at this date. Tap it when the tour ends. The habit is the point of the station. Before a date is read as early or late, ask what else was being made then. **Station 9, autopsy two: the wearing blanket.** Cultural note: Diné material. We read meaning only from the holder's record and from Diné-published sources, and we do not interpret designs [section 2]. Content note: this station describes Hwéeldi, the US Army's forced removal and imprisonment of Diné people. No images of people are shown. You can skip this station; the summary keeps the dates [section 2]. A Diné woman wove this blanket in about 1875 to 1880 [claim 10]. The record does not give her name. It names the donor, J. H. Wade, who gave it to Cleveland in 1921 [section 5]. It keeps her choices: handspun wool, Germantown yarn and bayeta [claim 10]. Bayeta is trade cloth, unravelled and rewoven as weft [claim 11]. Diné weavers were doing that with red bayeta bought from the Spanish shortly after 1800 [claim 12]. Germantown is commercial American yarn dyed with aniline, a dye made commercially from 1856 [claim 11]. The sheep came earlier: churro, with the Spanish in 1598 [claim 11]. Between the wool and the yarn sits Hwéeldi. From fall 1863, US cavalry under Kit Carson forced about 8,500 Diné to Fort Sumner, and over 2,000 died there [claim 13]. The 1868 treaty let them return to Diné Bikéyah [claim 13]. The record ties this blanket to a shift from garments for Diné use to pieces for non-Native collectors [claim 10]. One more record: a saddle throw titled "Germantown Eyedazzler", whose technique field lists handspun and bayeta and no Germantown yarn [claim 14]. A record can disagree with itself. Tag the field recorded, and tag the title guessed. **Station 10, autopsy three: the gold weight.** An antelope, cast in the 1800s as a weight for gold dust [claim 15]. Its caster modelled it in wax before casting [claim 15]. The museum credits an "Akan-style artist"; that is the museum's attribution, not ours [section 6]. Weights like it were used since at least 1600, the record says [claim 15]. In the museum's reading, the horns "may allude" to the proverb "had I known" [claim 15]. We keep the "may" and tag the sentence recorded, not seen. The trade layer runs north and south. Copper-alloy and gold casting probably reached the Akan area in the 14th century with the trans-Saharan gold trade [claim 16]. Brass working then grew with European trade and the reuse of imported brass [claim 16]. The weights carried standards from several directions. Geometric weights followed the mithqal, about 4.6 grams; Europeans brought the ackie [claim 17]. By the 19th century Islamic, Portuguese and troy standards were combined [claim 17]. The chain: the Charles Ratton Gallery, Paris, then Cleveland in 1935; before Paris, not recorded [claim 15]. This is a trade tool, not regalia. Asante royal gold in the same collection stays out, because its history may run through the British looting of Kumasi [section 2]. Three autopsies, three records. Each names an owner, a donor or a dealer; none names a maker [section 6]. The tour ends here and the lens stays live. Open the autopsy and start tagging. **What we simplified.** Cleveland's record gives two Han start dates, 202 and 206 BCE [section 9]. The tour uses 202; the pre-read holds the pair. The Xigoupan plaques' inscription is in claim 7 and not in the script, because the fact-check did not find it in the passages read [section 10]. Mercury gilding is described as the period's technique; this buckle is untested [claim 6]. The 1863 start of Hwéeldi and the Cultural note wording are held for a Diné reader [section 9]. The animals are not named [section 10]. The Practice Reading for Kyrgyz felt is a tab in the lab, not a station [claim 18; Cases row]. The "elsewhere" row beyond this unit waits on the world-band card, which is to write [Reference pages row]. |

**2. Protocol screen**

- **No human remains, sacred or secret material** in any case.
- **Case A, funerary rule.** Belt plaques like this are known mostly from burials, such as Xigoupan tomb 2. This buckle's findspot is not recorded, so it may be a grave good. Its card states the origin as the record gives it ("Ordos region") and carries this content note: "Content note: belt plaques like this are known mostly from burials. This buckle's findspot is not recorded, so it may have been taken from a grave." No images of burials or bodies.
- **Case B, Cultural note.** Diné material carries a Cultural note. Meaning is read only from the holder's record and Diné-published sources; designs are not interpreted. The Long Walk is named plainly as forced removal and imprisonment by the US Army, with deaths counted from a Diné-authored source. No images of people from Hwéeldi.
- **Case C.** The gold weight is a trade tool, not regalia. Asante soul-washers' discs and royal gold in the same collection ("before 1871", "before 1896") are excluded: their history may run through the British looting of Kumasi (OCC155). If named, it is named as looting by British forces, in the Manhyia Palace's own words where they exist: the palace calls the 1874 objects "stolen by the British from the Palace of Asantehene Kofi Karikari" (https://manhyiapalace.org/stolen-asante-artefacts-from-british-and-va-museums-now-home-2/, 10 April 2024).
- **Practice Reading.** UNESCO's text is a state's nomination (F1.3). No named living maker without consent.
- **The learner's own object.** For a family or religious object, the Mirror does not ask for the meaning layer.
