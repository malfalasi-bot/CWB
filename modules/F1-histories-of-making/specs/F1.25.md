## F1.25 Reading an object as history: slow looking and the autopsy

An object read slowly, in five layers (material, technique, labour, trade, meaning), shows a web of hands and routes; its museum record is one more source, with its own authors and gaps.

**Spec**

| Row | F1.25 |
|---|---|
| Essential question | How much of an object's history can you read from the thing itself, and how much comes from a record someone else wrote? |
| Opening move | **Lab first (form 15, then 4).** A small gilt bronze buckle fills the screen with no label (Cleveland 1952.115). A slow-looking timer runs: look for two minutes, write ten words, look again, write ten more (after Project Zero's "Ten Times Two", linked, not copied). Then five guess boxes, one per layer. **Claim:** "Look long enough and the object tells you its history." **Counter-claim:** "The bronze shows two long-eared animals. Who cast it, for whom, and where it was buried are not on it, and not in its record either." **Case:** the buckle (case A). |
| Lab or main interactive | **Object Autopsy, full.** Five layers, each guessed before it is revealed. *Material*: what it is made of, where that came from, what taking it cost (Planetary Weight enters here). *Technique*: an operational-sequence strip; the learner orders the steps, then sees the documented sequence or "not recorded". *Labour*: every hand, named or described. *Trade*: the routes the material and object travelled, on a map. *Meaning*: what it did for its users, and who says so. Each sentence is tagged seen / recorded / guessed. Under the layers sits the **provenance chain** from the record, and a "what was happening elsewhere" panel on the date. **Practice Reading** is the sibling tab for a living practice: who holds it, how it is taught, what it needs to continue. |
| Concept | About 650 words. (1) Slow looking as a method: Tishman's definition and four moves (decomposition, perspective shifting, collaborative observation, iterative noticing). (2) Five layers, and why technique was added (finding 4.1): an object is also a sequence of gestures (Leroi-Gourhan, Lemonnier). (3) Seen, recorded, guessed: the record is a source, written by a cataloguer with a date. (4) The provenance chain as the object's biography (Kopytoff), and the 1970 line. (5) Practice Reading: when the knowledge lives in people, not in a thing. Forward to F1.28 (grading the record), F1.28a (reconstruction), F1.29 (oral history), F4.12 (material dossier). |
| Cases | Three worked autopsies (form 3). **A. Belt buckle with animals,** Ordos region, 202 BCE–9 CE (Cleveland 1952.115), beside a Xianbei belt plaque (Met 24.174.7) and the inscribed gold plaques of Xigoupan tomb 2. **B. Wearing blanket,** Diné woman weaver, c. 1875–80 (Cleveland 1921.556), with a saddle throw whose title and record disagree (Cleveland 1929.922). **C. Gold weight in the form of an antelope,** Akan, 1800s (Cleveland 1935.306). **Practice Reading:** ala-kiyiz and shyrdak, Kyrgyz felt carpets (PRA160). |
| Apply — maker | **A full autopsy of an object you use in your work** (a tool, a material sample, a reference piece). Five layers, every sentence tagged, at least one source per layer or "not known". Order the technique strip. Write the provenance chain as far as you can trace it. End with the attribution line and the material's origin. |
| Apply — briefer | **A full autopsy of an object your organisation sells, gifts or cites as "heritage".** Same five layers. Then mark each "not known" that a supplier or maker could answer, and write it as one question. The answers belong to the supplier and maker; the learner records only what they publish or consent to share. |
| Partner | **The Mirror.** It reflects the autopsy back: "Here is what your reading is doing." It counts sentences per tag and per layer. It says when a layer is all guesses, and when owners and donors are named but makers are not. It does not judge the object. Question: "Your autopsy names [n] people. How many of them made it?" **Test:** the learner turns one guessed sentence into a question with a named source to check, and names one hand their reading leaves out. |
| Forms | 15, 4, 3, 14, 12 |
| Consumption forms | **Five-minute:** the buckle, the ten-word look, five guesses, one reveal. **Session (about 45 minutes):** opening 8, lab 10, concept 7, three autopsies 12, apply 6, Mirror 2. **Reference:** the five-layer autopsy card with its tags, the operational-sequence template, the Practice Reading card, printable. |
| Media | Open images from Cleveland (CC0) and the Met (public domain, checked per record); our own drawings for the technique strips (casting, upright loom, felting); our own map for the trade layer; link-outs for Project Zero, UNESCO and Diné-published sources. No video. Optional audio: a learner's own spoken slow-looking notes, kept on device. |
| Artifact fields | SRC |

Accessibility level 1 (slow looking without sight: the autopsy accepts touch and spoken description of the learner's own object; every case image has a long description written as a slow look). |

**2. Protocol screen**

- **No human remains, sacred or secret material** in any case.
- **Case A, funerary rule.** Belt plaques like this are known mostly from burials, such as Xigoupan tomb 2. This buckle's findspot is not recorded, so it may be a grave good. Its card states the origin as the record gives it ("Ordos region") and carries this content note: "Content note: belt plaques like this are known mostly from burials. This buckle's findspot is not recorded, so it may have been taken from a grave." No images of burials or bodies.
- **Case B, Cultural note.** Diné material carries a Cultural note. Meaning is read only from the holder's record and Diné-published sources; designs are not interpreted. The Long Walk is named plainly as forced removal and imprisonment by the US Army, with deaths counted from a Diné-authored source. No images of people from Hwéeldi.
- **Case C.** The gold weight is a trade tool, not regalia. Asante soul-washers' discs and royal gold in the same collection ("before 1871", "before 1896") are excluded: their history may run through the British looting of Kumasi (OCC155). If named, it is named as looting by British forces, in the Manhyia Palace's own words where they exist: the palace calls the 1874 objects "stolen by the British from the Palace of Asantehene Kofi Karikari" (https://manhyiapalace.org/stolen-asante-artefacts-from-british-and-va-museums-now-home-2/, 10 April 2024).
- **Practice Reading.** UNESCO's text is a state's nomination (F1.3). No named living maker without consent.
- **The learner's own object.** For a family or religious object, the Mirror does not ask for the meaning layer.

**8. Success criteria**

1. The learner's autopsy fills all five layers. Each sentence is tagged seen, recorded or guessed, and each layer has a source or "not known".
2. The learner orders an operational-sequence strip for one case and marks at least one step the record does not document.
3. The provenance chain names each holder the record gives and the first gap. For case A, both readings of who made it appear with sources; which the learner prefers is not scored.

**9. Readings and risks**

- **Historian pre-read (pack v2) should check:** the funerary wording for case A (rule 7); "Hwéeldi" and the 1863/1864 start (rule 5, names; OCC141); that the Mirror does not interpret Diné designs (rule 6); "earliest known" wording for Akan casting (claim 16, a "supposition"); the two Han start dates (202 and 206 BCE) in Cleveland's record.
- **Sensitive steps to hold until a reader clears them:** the Hwéeldi paragraph and Cultural note text (a Diné reader, ideally); the exclusion note on Asante gold; the Mirror's prompts on family and religious objects; the mercury paragraph, so it informs without implying the buckle was tested.
- **Balance:** the steppe appears as maker and client, not as raiders on China's edge; the Diné weaver as a designer choosing materials, not a supplier to traders. Project Zero's routine is NC-ND: link it, never adapt it.
