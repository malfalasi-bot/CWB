## F1.28 Primary sources and archives

A primary source carries who wrote it, for whom, in which language and through whose hands, so grading it means reading those layers before its content.

**Spec**

| Row | Content |
|---|---|
| Essential question | What can this source carry, and what has been added between its writer and you? |
| Opening move | **Problem first (form 15).** A designer's slide: "Karakorum's silver fountain, made by a Parisian goldsmith for Ögedei Khan." Beside it: a friar who was there, a museum record of Mongol cloth of gold, an online encyclopedia. Prompt: "Which would you trust, and for which part?" **Claim:** an eyewitness is the best source. **Counter-claim:** an eyewitness is one layer; who wrote, for whom, in which translation, and what else agrees decide what it carries. The reveal: William of Rubruck, there in the 1250s, names Mangu (Möngke), not Ögedei. The encyclopedia says Ögedei while quoting Rubruck. The museum record's chain starts at a London dealer in 1989. No source opened reports a surviving trace of the tree. The goldsmith was a captive. Case: the silver tree. |
| Lab or main interactive | **Source Grader.** The learner grades one source on four criteria. **Provenance:** who made it, when, where; how it reached us. **Bias:** for whom and to what end. **Distance:** eyewitness, report, copy, translation (translator and year required). **Corroboration:** what independent evidence agrees or disagrees. Each gets one of three steps and a cited sentence. The result is a profile, not a score. Two extra layers: a translation log, and a catalogue reading (creator, date, rights, silences). Demonstration set, five worlds: Rubruck (F1.12a), Vitruvius in four versions (F1.11), the Florentine Codex and its catalogue record (F1.7), the *Tiangong kaiwu* (F1.8), the *Satire of the Trades* (F1.6). |
| Concept | 700 words. **Primary for what?** Rubruck is primary for Möngke's Karakorum, secondary for Ögedei's. **Four criteria**, mapped to a published teaching frame (sourcing, contextualization, corroboration, close reading; Digital Inquiry Group). **Translation is a layer** with a date and a translator; word choice and word order move. **The archive is an author:** catalogue fields are written later, by an institution; read the creator field for who it leaves out, and the rights line for what you may do. **Corroboration can come from other evidence:** excavation at Karakorum; genomics at Botai, a revised "first" (F1.12a). Rule: grading describes a source's reach; it does not decide what happened. "Earliest known", never "first". |
| Cases | **1. The silver tree (F1.12a).** Rubruck's report, 1253–55: a silver tree at the palace entry, four lions pouring mare's milk, four pipes for wine, *caracosmos*, *bal* and rice mead, an angel blowing a trumpet worked by a hidden man. The maker: Guillaume Boucher (Rubruck's "master William"), of Paris, captured at Belgrade and passed between Mongol households. Graded: eyewitness; a missionary's aims; uncorroborated for the tree. Excavation found the artisans' quarter; the "palace" proved a Buddhist temple. Translation strip: Rockhill (1900) against Jackson (1990). **2. Three triads, one sentence (F1.11).** Vitruvius: *firmitatis utilitatis venustatis*. Wotton (1624): "Commoditie, Firmenes, and Delight", reordered on purpose. Gwilt (1826): "strength, utility, and beauty". Morgan (1914): "durability, convenience, and beauty". A modern site misquotes Wotton's order. **3. One codex, two columns, one creator (F1.7).** The Florentine Codex (1577). The Library of Congress record names one creator, Sahagún. Its own summary says Nahua students made the 2,468 illustrations. The Getty edition names seven Nahua authors and artists and calls the Spanish column shorter and divergent. |

**2. Protocol screen**

**Excluded, and why:**

- **Florentine Codex Books 1–3 and 12:** deities, rites and the conquest. Not shown; Book 9 (merchants and craftworkers) is shown instead.
- **Matthew Paris's 1240 passage** in Rockhill's introduction ("a detestable nation of Satan"): not displayed. Named in risks as a bias example for a reader to clear.
- **Rubruck's report of famine among the Comans** (cannibalism): not used.

**Shown, with content note:**

- **Guillaume Boucher:** a captive, enslaved by his own report through Rubruck. Rubruck's words ("William is his slave"; "became the property of") are quoted and attributed. In our voice he is a goldsmith held captive, never property. Content note: war captivity.

**8. Success criteria**

1. Given one source, the learner records author, date, holder and translator (or "original") and gives a four-part profile, each part with a cited reason.
2. Given two dated translations of one passage, the learner names one difference and says what it changes for a reader.
3. The learner quotes a catalogue record's creator field and names one contributor it leaves out, with a source.
