## F1.29 Vernacular intelligence: oral history, folktale, local taxonomy, with consent

What people say, tell and name about making is evidence, and it reaches the record only on their terms.

**Spec**

| Row | Content |
|---|---|
| Essential question | How do you record what people know about making without taking it from them? |
| Opening move | **Making first (the Kit on yourself).** The learner is the first narrator. They sign the consent form, record three minutes on their own device about something they make or mend, and list five words they use for its tools or faults. Then they review their own transcript: delete a line, restrict one, clarify one. **Claim:** oral sources are weaker evidence than written ones. **Counter-claim:** every source is someone's account; an oral one says who is speaking, and can be asked again. The reveal: two Kyrgyz words, *ala-kiyiz* and *shyrdak*, name two ways of making felt (case 1). The learner's five words are a taxonomy too. |
| Lab or main interactive | **Oral History Kit**, seven steps. (1) **Plan:** purpose, uses, what you will not ask. (2) **Consent:** free, prior and informed, recorded before recording (OHA); "sustained" (UNESCO), so asked again at review. (3) **Record** on the learner's device only (data policy option A). (4) **Review:** the narrator may delete, restrict, redact, clarify, or keep it closed until a date (OHA, 2018). (5) **Protocol:** a Cultural note by default for Indigenous narrators; a Label only where the community itself applies one. Stop rule: sacred or secret material is not recorded, and is deleted if it comes up. (6) **Clear:** upload only the approved excerpt and the fact that a consent record exists. (7) **Withdraw:** the narrator's route out, on the form. Add-on cards: **Names sheet** (term, language, speaker, what it tells apart, nearest outside word or "none") and **Tale card** (teller, occasion, what it explains, other versions, what it is evidence of). Demonstration: an Archives of American Art transcript its narrator reviewed and corrected (claim 19). |
| Concept | 700 words. Three kinds of vernacular intelligence, each read as evidence. **Testimony:** Benin court oral histories date lost-wax casting to the 13th century and give the teacher two names (claim 14); the record keeps both. **Story:** a tale about making is evidence of what a place said about its craft, not proof of the event (case 2). **Classification:** harakeke weavers name cultivars by fibre and leaf (claim 15); Hanunóo colour words also mark wet against dry (claim 17). Then the rules: OHA practice, UNESCO's ethical principles, our data policy. Then the edge: "folktale" is an outsider's category; the community decides what is sacred. Last, funded documentation and its limit: EMKP deposits copies in-country, under a non-commercial licence (claim 18). |
| Cases | **1. Two felts and the epic (F1.12a).** Kyrgyz felt carpets, Urgent Safeguarding List, 2012: two types, with "the semantics of ornamentation" part of the element. UNESCO's reports name women "the principal bearers"; men prepare wool and press felt. Beside them, the akyns (proclaimed 2003, inscribed 2008) and the Manas trilogy (2013), told by "women and men, of all ages". Method: build a names sheet from a file, and ask who supplied each term. **2. Seeds in a head-dress (F1.12a; F1.8).** Xuanzang's record (646; Beal, 1884) tells how a Chinese princess hid mulberry seed and silkworm eggs in her head-dress for Khotan. A votive panel from a Dandan-Uiliq shrine paints the story. Cleveland's Southern Song scroll pictures silk making in 14 steps. Method: the tale card; one subject, three media, two points of view. **3. A fanner and a family (F1.7; F1.6).** Lynette Youson learned to coil a rice fanner from her great-grandmother at five. A fanner made in Senegal in 2010, maker unrecorded, is in NMAAHC. Historians disagree on what such baskets prove (claim 13). Method: what family testimony carries (transmission), and what it cannot settle alone. |
| Apply — maker | **One consented account.** Ten to twenty minutes with one narrator on a practice they hold: how they learned, five or more of their names for things, one story of how it began. Run all seven steps. Output: a cleared excerpt (up to 300 words), a names sheet, a tale card if a story was told, the SRC consent record, and one line on what you will not use, and why. |
| Apply — briefer | **One consented account, for a brief.** The same Kit, with someone whose practice a brief would draw on. Rewrite the brief's reference line: the narrator named as they asked, the cleared excerpt cited, the consent status stated. Add a clause: nothing from the account is used beyond what the narrator cleared. |
| Partner | **Historian**, consultation-coach mode. Question: "Who agreed to this, to what, and how can they take it back?" It walks the steps and never approves the plan. Asked "is this story sacred?", it says only the narrator or community can rule. **Test it ends with:** every quoted line traces to a consent record with a review status; any other line comes back "not cleared". |
| Forms | 2 (the Kit), 3 (three cases), 8 (a demonstration interview recorded by our team under the Kit), 10 (names wall), 13 (the fanner debate). |
| Consumption forms | **Five-minute:** the names wall. **Session (about 60 min):** opener, Kit, concept, two cases, Apply begun. **Reference:** claims sheet, Kit templates, protocol screen, glossary (free, prior and informed consent; narrator review; Notice; Label). |
| Media | Our own forms; our own diagrams of the two felt techniques and of a harakeke leaf (keel, margin). Three open images (§5); everything else linked out. One team-recorded interview with transcript. |
| Artifact fields | **SRC:** "What it is: person or interview"; consent record (form or statement, review status, restrictions, withdrawal route); cultural protocol; the names sheet as a linked note. |
| Threads | Attribution and Authority (lead: the narrator as copyright holder and co-author). Writing and Argument (tale card; two readings). Accessibility level 1 (transcripts; plain-language forms read aloud on request). Planetary Weight, light touch (wool, harakeke, sweetgrass). |
| Closing contribution | SRC line: "Account: [narrator, as they asked to be named], [date], [place]. Consent: [form or statement]; reviewed [date]; restricted [what]. Withdrawal: [route]. Held: on my device. Cleared excerpt: [link]. Names: [n] terms in [language]." |
| Claims sheet | 19 claims below: 16 documented (five carry a qualifier: claims 6, 8, 9, 14, 16), 2 contested (both readings given), 1 interpretive (the unit's framing). Every URL was opened; claim 5 cites an internal document. |

**2. Protocol screen**

**Excluded, and why:**

- **Sacred or secret material in any recording:** stop rule (Kit step 5). UNESCO: customary access "should be fully respected, even where these may limit broader public access".
- **Popol Vuh (TXT119):** a K'iche' sacred text. Named only, never offered as a "folktale about making".
- **Wajãpi kusiwa designs (PRA056):** canon says do not reproduce without consent. Named only.
- **The manaschy's "prophetic dream":** UNESCO's words only; learners are not prompted to record such experiences.
- **Kākahu and other taonga:** not shown. **Harakeke cultivar names: link-out only.** We do not reproduce cultivar names or their kōrero. We say that weavers named cultivars by fibre and leaf, and link to Manaaki Whenua's catalogue; Manaaki Whenua holds the collection as kaitiaki and notes that provenance is "mostly limited to region, rather than to a specific person or hapu". Cultural note (our own wording; not a Local Contexts Notice): "Cultural note: harakeke names are mātauranga Māori, held by weavers and their iwi. We point to where they are published and do not reuse them."

**Shown, with display basis and content note:**

- **Dandan-Uiliq panel (British Museum 1907,1111.73):** a votive panel from a shrine (D.X.4), removed by Stein's expedition (1900–01). Linked out with origin and content note.
- **Benin plaque (Cleveland 1999.1):** a palace plaque, not an altar object. Content note: the British assault of 1897.
- **Fanner baskets:** content note on slavery; enslaved people, never goods; historians' terms quoted and attributed. The case opens with Lynette Youson's own account as her record gives it, and links the Gullah/Geechee Nation's own site, whose head of state writes "We are the ones that are to tell our story!" (https://gullahgeecheenation.com/2020/06/27/the-insanity-and-audacity-of-racism-and-white-privilege-a-memo-from-the-gullah-geechee/). The two scholarly readings follow, labelled as outside readings. Content note: "Content note: Lowcountry rice was grown by enslaved Africans and their descendants. Scholars argue here over their knowledge; the Gullah Geechee people who carry it today speak for it themselves."
- **Gullah Geechee and Māori material:** a Cultural note on each item. The Kyrgyz files are state nominations; the community is still named first.

**8. Success criteria**

1. The learner's SRC consent record has all four fields filled, and no excerpt is uploaded before its review status reads "reviewed".
2. The names sheet holds at least five terms, each with speaker, language, what it tells apart, and nearest outside word or "none".
3. On the tale card, the learner states what one story is evidence of and what it is not, naming one other version or source. On the fanner card, they state both readings without being asked to choose.
