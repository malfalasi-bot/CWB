## F1.3 How histories get written: canons, periods, the timeline as an argument; design history versus design studies

Every design timeline makes an argument about who counts, and someone made it at a known date. The learner learns to read a canon, a period and a civilisation label as claims by a source, and to annotate what they leave out.

| Concept | About 650 words, in five short parts. (1) **Canon:** a list of named makers that sets a standard (Vasari; Pevsner as a later example). (2) **Hierarchy of the arts:** academies (Florence 1563, Paris 1648, London 1768) and the Salon split "fine" art from "decorative" or "applied" work; craft, art and design became ranks, not only kinds. (3) **Periods:** a period is a definition by a source with a place and dates. Thomsen's three ages were built on Danish collections and are always regional. (4) **Civilisation as an argument:** Mirabeau to Childe's criteria, which later readers turned into a test; Toynbee and Huntington as historiography; Graeber and Wengrow (2021) against a single path of stages. **3. Claims (20)**

1. Mirabeau's *L'Ami des hommes* has the use of "civilisation" long cited as the earliest in French: "c'est le premier ressort de la civilisation". Benveniste found the work was written, and probably printed, in 1756 and published in 1757, so 1756 and 1757 are both right for different events. Whether it is the earliest use is contested: the Collège de France reports that recent lexicographic tools find the word earlier, and in English before French. — documented (the dates); contested ("earliest"). Corrected 3 Oct 2026. — https://www.persee.fr/doc/barb_0001-4133_1990_num_1_5_22686 (Arnould 1990, citing Benveniste 1953) ; https://www.college-de-france.fr/en/agenda/special-events/civilizations-questioning-identity-and-diversity/is-law-civil-civilization-as-word-and-story ; https://www.larousse.fr/encyclopedie/divers/civilisation/34231 — full text (article); summary (event page).
2. The word stood for progress and was "opposed to barbarism or rudeness". — documented. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civilization — summary.
3. In Mabo (No 2), 1992, Brennan J quoted the 1919 Privy Council (Lord Sumner, *In re Southern Rhodesia* [1919] AC 211 at 233): "Some tribes are so low in the scale of social organization…". He called that basis "false in fact and unacceptable in our society" (para. 39; (1992) 175 CLR 1 at 39–40; the quotation is set out at para. 38 and recalled at para. 41). — documented. — https://alor.org/Storage/Library/Mabo%20v%20Queensland%20[No%202].html ; http://www.mabonativetitle.com/info/recognitionNT.htm (Nettheim) ; https://henley.austlii.edu.au/cgi-bin/viewdoc/au/journals/AULegHist/2007/13.html (Reynolds 2007) — full text (unofficial mirror); quoted passages with CLR pins (checked 3 Oct 2026; the AustLII judgment page itself not opened).
4. Childe's 1950 *Town Planning Review* article (vol. 21, no. 1, pp. 3–17) set out "ten rather abstract criteria" of the "urban revolution". — documented. That later readers used the list as a test for "civilisation" is interpretive (our framing). — https://elearning.uni-regensburg.de/pluginfile.php/1508194/course/overviewfiles/Childe.pdf?forcedownload=1 (JSTOR copy of the article) — full text (source replaced 3 Oct 2026).
5. Toynbee's *A Study of History* traced the rise and, mostly, decline of civilisations, but his count changed: 19 or 21 in 1934, 21 or 23 in 1954, and 13 full civilisations in his 1961 *Reconsiderations*. Huntington argued the 21st century would see a "clash of civilizations". — documented (the count varies by volume; "some two dozen" in the canon row is fair for 1934–54 only). Corrected 3 Oct 2026. — https://www.encyclopedia.com/arts/culture-magazines/study-history ; https://kwharbaugh.blogspot.com/2005/03/study-of-history.html (tabulates vol. XII, pp. 558–61; a blog, to replace with vol. XII itself) — summary.
6. Graeber and Wengrow (2021) argue that people tried many social forms rather than passing through fixed stages. — documented as their argument. Two readings: supporters take it as a break with stage models; one reviewer calls it "a very defensible argument already made several times". The brief's wording "no single original form" is not verified verbatim; a review quotes "no 'original' form of human society". — https://www.sapiens.org/culture/dawn-of-everything-excerpt/ (excerpt); https://notevenpast.org/a-false-dawn/ (review) — partial text.
7. Thomsen arranged Denmark's national collection by Stone, Bronze and Iron Ages. He mentioned "the old idea of the three ages" in a letter of 1825; Vedel Simonsen had proposed it earlier. He published the scheme in *Ledetraad til nordisk Oldkyndighed* (1836). — documented. — https://natmus.dk/historisk-viden/temaer/nationalmuseets-historie/de-tre-tidsaldre/?force=desktop (National Museum of Denmark) — summary (source replaced 3 Oct 2026).
8. For PeriodO, a period needs a name, time bounds, a region and "some citable source". An authority is "a set of periods that share a source". — documented. — https://perio.do/technical-overview/ — full text.
9. The Met holds the 1568 edition of Vasari's *Le vite* with woodcut illustrations, catalogued as public domain. — documented. — https://collectionapi.metmuseum.org/public/collection/v1/objects/358298 — full record.
10. The Florentine Academy of Design was founded in 1563 by Cosimo I, "at the instigation of" Vasari, and set "in contrast to the guilds". — documented. — https://www.britannica.com/place/Academy-of-Design — summary.
11. The French and English royal academies were founded in 1648 and 1768. From the late 18th century they had "a virtual monopoly" on taste and official patronage. — documented. — https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-salon-and-the-royal-academy-in-the-nineteenth-century — full essay (Rosenfeld, 2004).
12. The Royal Academy's 36 founders included two women, Mary Moser and Angelica Kauffman. The next woman became a full Academician in 1936. — documented. — https://www.royalacademy.org.uk/page/a-brief-history-of-the-ra — full page.
13. Kristeller (1951) called Batteux's 1746 treatise "the decisive step" towards the system of the fine arts. — interpretive (a scholar's thesis). — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Batteux — summary.
14. Bent's *The Ruined Cities of Mashonaland* (1892) concludes that Great Zimbabwe's builders were "of a Semitic race and of Arabian origin". The phrase was read in the 1895 edition (introduction, p. xiv, in the argument from the ruins' orientation); the 1892 first edition was not checked. — documented. — https://www.gutenberg.org/files/69067/69067-h/69067-h.htm — full text (edition corrected 3 Oct 2026).
15. Randall-MacIver (1906) and Caton-Thompson (excavated 1929, published 1931) found Great Zimbabwe to be African work; Randall-MacIver named the ancestors of the Shona. In 1970 the Rhodesian Front government told its employees that no official publication might say plainly that Great Zimbabwe was an African creation, and Garlake was forced into exile that year. — documented. — https://sas-space.sas.ac.uk/4213/1/Preben_Kaarsholm_-_The_past_as_battlefield_in_Rhodesia_and_Zimbabwe.pdf (Kaarsholm, citing Garlake 1973 and 1982) ; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/239789630_Peter_Garlake_1934-2011_Great_Zimbabwe_and_the_politics_of_the_past_in_Zimbabwe (Pikirayi obituary) — full text; excerpts (sources replaced 3 Oct 2026); also CL-134.
16. Blair and Bloom (2003) call a distinct "Islamic" art tradition "a product of late nineteenth- and twentieth-century Western scholarship". They add that much of it was not made by Muslims for the faith. — interpretive (a scholarly position, widely cited). — https://www.collegeart.org/pdf/artbulletin/Art%20Bulletin%20Vol%2085%20No%201%20Blair%20and%20Bloom.pdf — full text.
17. The 1910 Munich exhibition showed more than 3,600 objects from about 250 collections in eighty halls (Troelenberg 2012; Wikipedia says more than 260). It raised things once seen as "folk art or arts and crafts" to "masterpieces" (Wikipedia's wording). The Met's 1930 handbook by M. S. Dimand still said "Mohammedan Decorative Arts". — documented. Corrected 3 Oct 2026. — https://arthistoriography.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/05/troelenberg.pdf (Journal of Art Historiography 6) ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meisterwerke_muhammedanischer_Kunst (summary); https://www.metmuseum.org/met-publications/a-handbook-of-mohammedan-decorative-arts (publisher page).
18. Margolin (*Design Studies*, 1992) proposed folding design history into design studies. Forty replied in the *Journal of Design History* (1993), and *Design Issues* ran a special issue in 1995. — documented. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Design_studies — summary.
19. The Decolonising Design manifesto (Abdulla, Ansari and others, 2019) says "It is not sufficient for design institutions to simply include a greater diversity of actors or perspectives." — documented. — https://jfsdigital.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/12-Ansari-Decolonising-Design.pdf — full text.
20. The record is uneven. Cleveland's CC0 set held 42,500 records on 3 Oct 2026 (42,316 on 29 Sept): 2,544 in Chinese Art, 388 in African Art and 294 in Islamic Art; a keyword search for "Oceania" gave 112. The AIC Asante kente wrapper (1901/50) is flagged "not public domain". On Ghana's nomination, UNESCO inscribed the craftsmanship of Kente on the Representative List in December 2024 (19.COM, Asunción). — documented (the counts are a snapshot; rerun 3 Oct 2026). "The record is biased" is interpretive. — https://openaccess-api.clevelandart.org/api/artworks/?cc0=1&limit=1 ; https://api.artic.edu/api/v1/artworks/65187 ; https://ich.unesco.org/en/decisions/19.COM/7.B.38 — full records.

**Unit framing (interpretive):** a timeline is an argument by a source; civilisation is a search label, never a rank. Cleveland's Islamic count does not show the "strong on the Islamic world" pattern that the decided addition states. That pattern may hold for the Met; a checker must confirm it before the claim is written. (Fact-check 3 Oct 2026: the Met's own page for Dimand's 1930 handbook speaks of "The Metropolitan Museum of Art's strong Islamic art collection"; that is the holder's description, not a count.)
