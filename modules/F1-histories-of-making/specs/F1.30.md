## F1.30 Re-timing the Atlas: other calendars and periodisations

Every date in the Atlas began in some calendar, and someone converted it to "CE". The learner puts their own lineage on another calendar and records what each conversion kept, widened or lost.

**Spec**

| Concept | About 650 words, in five parts. (1) **A calendar is an authority:** an epoch, a year type and someone who sets it (a caliph, a court, a committee, a keeper). (2) **Conversion has conventions:** tabular or observed months; elapsed or current years; proleptic Gregorian; a correlation constant. (3) **Ranges widen:** what a conversion cannot narrow, it must show. (4) **Dating methods:** inscription, mark, document, radiocarbon (BP, cal BP, IntCal20). A date can hold while its reading changes (the Botai revision, F1.12a). (5) **Periods are arguments:** Mill's three religious periods; Shaffer's Indus Tradition, a scheme without rise and fall; "Middle Ages", "Jahiliyya" and "Pax Mongolica" as labels with authors. Closing note: adding calendars while CE stays the hidden axis is pluralism, not decolonisation (Ansari, F1.3). |

**2. Protocol screen**

- **Cleveland 1967.29 (stela):** a royal monument, not a grave good, but possibly cut from its site. **Link-out only** (AR-043): no image; text and a link to the Cleveland record, with its origin and this content note: "Content note: this stela comes from El Perú-Waka', Guatemala, a site looters reached in the 1960s. How it left Guatemala is not recorded. Guatemala's law today forbids the export and sale of its pre-Hispanic heritage." Never show Lady K'abel's tomb or any human remains from El Perú-Waka'.
- **Winter counts:** a Cultural note (our own wording; not a Local Contexts Notice) on Lakota, Nakota and Dakota material: "Cultural note: each winter count belongs to its keeper's band and family, and its stories are theirs to tell. We name each count by its keeper and nation, as the holder records it." Link out, reproduce none. Some years are named for epidemics and killings: add a content note. The tool never "converts" an event-named year. It records the keeper's year name and the correlation the holder or community gives.
- **Living religious calendars** (Hijri, Hebrew, Ethiopian, the 260-day count, the Kali Yuga): each is its tradition's own reckoning, never an error. No divination; no feast dates as authority ("Ask your community's own authority"). The 260-day count carries this Cultural note: "Cultural note: the Cholq'ij, the 260-day count, is a living sacred calendar kept by Maya Ajq'ijab' (day keepers). The tool gives a day's number and name only, never its meaning or a reading; for that, ask an Ajq'ij." (AR-106) The Kali Yuga is named as Hindu reckoning only: the tool never dates its start, never calls it history or myth, and points to the tradition's own almanacs (pañcāṅga) (AR-107).
- **Orkhon inscriptions:** memorial monuments; link out; no burial material.
- **Mongol conquest:** "Pax Mongolica" is quoted and attributed; conquests, deportations and killings are named plainly. Deported artisans are people, never cargo.
- **Astrolabes:** used for prayer times and the qibla, but not sacred objects; no content note. Confirm with a reader.

**3. Claims (20)**

1. Caliph 'Umar set the Hijra as the era's epoch around 638 (17 AH). The year has 354 or 355 days and moves through the seasons in about 33 years. — documented. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_calendar — summary.
2. The tabular Islamic calendar begins Friday 16 July 622 (Julian); religious months begin with the sighted crescent. By the tabular rule, 690 AH runs 4 January–23 December 1291 and 1065 AH runs 1 November 1654–20 October 1655 (Julian). — documented (our computation on Fourmilab's algorithm, rerun by script on 3 Oct 2026 with the same results; observed dates can differ by a day or two). — https://www.fourmilab.ch/documents/calendar/ — full text.
3. Met 444408 is "dated 690 AH/1291 CE". Its maker, the Rasulid prince 'Umar ibn Yusuf (ruled 1295–96), wrote a treatise on making astrolabes. The record's range, 1290–1292, is wider than the Hijri year. — documented. — https://collectionapi.metmuseum.org/public/collection/v1/objects/444408 ; https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/444408 — full record.
4. Met 451699, by Muhammad Zaman al-Munajjim al-Asturlabi, is "dated 1065 AH/1654–55 CE", attributed to Mashhad. — documented. — https://collectionapi.metmuseum.org/public/collection/v1/objects/451699 — full record.
5. The Jalali calendar was adopted on 15 March 1079 under Malik-Shah I, advised by astronomers including Omar Khayyam. Iran adopted the Solar Hijri calendar by law on 31 March 1925; Afghanistan in 1922 (1301 Š.). Its year begins at the March equinox. — documented (1925 adoption, equinox rule and Afghanistan's 1922 confirmed in a converter's documentation and Iranica; the day-dates 15 March 1079 and 31 March 1925 rest on the summaries). — https://www.fourmilab.ch/documents/calendar/ (full text); https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/calendars-iii-afghan-calendars (full text); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jalali_calendar ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_Hijri_calendar — summary.
6. The earliest Chinese era name, Jianyuan, was proclaimed by Emperor Wu of Han. Sources give 140 BCE or, "more formally", 110 BCE. China replaced era names in 1912. — contested date: give both. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_era_name — summary.
7. The sexagenary cycle pairs ten stems with twelve branches. It dates days on Shang oracle bones, and years from about the mid-3rd century BCE. The oracle bones' years come from radiocarbon (CL-103). — documented. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexagenary_cycle — summary.
8. Cleveland 1957.61 and 1964.218 carry one mark, "Da Ming Chenghua nian zhi". Cleveland dates the first 1465–87 and the pair 1662–1722 (Kangxi reign), calling their mark "apocryphal". — documented. Why Kangxi potters used the older mark is interpretive (the canon says homage). — https://openaccess-api.clevelandart.org/api/artworks/1957.61 ; https://openaccess-api.clevelandart.org/api/artworks/1964.218 — full records.
9. Japan's earliest era name is Taika (645). One era per reign has applied since 1868, by law since 1979 (Act on Era Names, Act No. 43 of 12 June 1979: era names change "only when there has been a succession to the Imperial Throne"). Reiwa began on 1 May 2019; an era's first year is *gannen*. — documented (Taika and *gannen* rest on the summary). — https://www.japaneselawtranslation.go.jp/en/laws/view/4844/en (official translation); https://japan.kantei.go.jp/98_abe/statement/201904/_00001.html ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_era_name — full text; summary.
10. India adopted its national calendar on 22 March 1957 (1 Chaitra 1879 Śaka; Fourmilab: "officially adopted on 1 Caitra, 1879 Saka Era"), after the Calendar Reform Committee headed by Meghnad Saha (Saha's role rests on the summary). Wikipedia puts Śaka year 0 in 78 CE; Fourmilab counts from the equinox of 79. Both state one convention (elapsed versus current years). — documented. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_national_calendar ; Fourmilab (above) — summary; full text.
11. The Ethiopian year number is eight below the Gregorian until 10 or 11 September, then seven. New Year is 11 September (12 before a Gregorian leap year); the thirteenth month is Pagume. The era follows Annianus of Alexandria (c. 400). It is Ethiopia's official civil calendar. — documented. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethiopian_calendar — summary.
12. The Hebrew calendar is lunisolar, with a leap month in 7 of every 19 years, counting *Anno Mundi* from 3761 BCE. Tradition credits Hillel II with fixing it "in the year 670 of the Seleucid era" (358–359 CE); scholars question this. Hebcal gives 2 October 2026 as 21 Tishrei 5787. — documented; Hillel date contested. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_calendar (summary); https://www.hebcal.com/converter?cfg=json&gy=2026&gm=10&gd=2&g2h=1 (full record).
13. A k'atun is 7,200 days, about 19.7 years. The GMT correlation (584,283) is the consensus; 584,285 and others exist. The earliest known Long Count dates are on non-Maya monuments: Chiapa de Corzo Stela 2 (36 BCE) and Tres Zapotes Stela C (32 BCE). — documented; correlation probable (most specialists). — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerican_Long_Count_calendar — summary.
14. Kennett and others (2013) radiocarbon-dated a sapodilla-wood lintel at Tikal bearing the Long Count 9.13.3.7.18. A Bayesian model put the carving at AD 658–696, which "strongly supports" the GMT correlation. — documented. — https://www.nature.com/articles/srep01597 — full text (CC BY-NC-ND 3.0).
15. Cleveland 1967.29 is the front face of a stela from El Perú-Waka', dated 692, marking a k'atun's end and showing Lady K'abel. Cleveland bought it in 1967 from John A. Stokes Jr.; it is rebuilt from more than 40 fragments. A blog says it "was looted from El Perú-Waka' in the sixties"; Trafficking Culture's encyclopaedia calls it "a different looted stela from El Perú-Waka' now in the Cleveland Museum" (https://traffickingculture.org/encyclopedia/case-studies/el-peru-waka-stela-fragment-returned-to-guatemala-in-1999/, image caption). — documented (record); looting probable (a scholarly encyclopaedia's caption, not a primary record). — https://openaccess-api.clevelandart.org/api/artworks/1967.29 (full record); https://www.livescience.com/archaeology/americas/front-face-of-a-stela-a-1-300-year-old-carving-of-a-powerful-maya-lady-supreme-warlord-that-weighs-over-2-tons (summary); https://www.anonymousswisscollector.com/2012/10/replica-monuments-and-warrior-queens-in.html (blog).
16. Winter counts mark each year by one event; Lakota years ran first snowfall to first snowfall. Garrick Mallery matched "The Year the Stars Fell" to the Leonid storm of November 1833 to align counts with Western calendars. NMAI attributes Lone Dog's count to Nakota people. It holds Long Soldier's count (1798–1902) as 11/6720: its exhibition page says "Hunkpapa Lakota", while its collection record names the maker as Long Soldier, Oglala Lakota (ca. 1847–ca. 1925), and the culture as "probably Hunkpapa Lakota (attributed)". — documented; the keeper's nation is recorded two ways by the holder. — https://museumsandtheweb.com/mw2005/abstracts/prg_280000983.html (Leopold, National Anthropological Archives, on Mallery); https://americanindian.si.edu/collections-search/object/NMAI_125731 (collection record); https://americanindian.si.edu/exhibitions/horsenation/wintercount.html (holder page); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_count (summary).
17. The *Secret History of the Mongols* says it was completed when, "in the Year of the Rat", the Great Assembly convened. Proposed years: 1228 (Cleaves, Onon); 1229 (de Rachewiltz: the assembly called for 1228 met in August 1229); 1240; 1252 (Atwood); 1264 (Hung). — contested: give the readings. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secret_History_of_the_Mongols (summary); https://cdn.angkordatabase.asia/libs/docs/Rachewiltz-The-Secret-History-of-the-Mongols.pdf (partial text; mirror of https://cedar.wwu.edu/cedarbooks/4/, CC BY-NC-ND 4.0).
18. The Kül Tigin inscription (Orkhon valley) dates his death: "Kül tigin koy yılka yiti yegirmikə uçdı", he died in the Year of the Sheep, on the seventeenth (731 CE), as published translations give it (Malov 1951; Ibodullaev 2021). Mahmud al-Kashgari's *Dīwān* (Baghdad, 1072–74) tells of a khagan who mistook a battle's year and named the years after twelve animals. — documented (the inscription's wording, through published translations; Tekin's edition not opened); the story is documented as a story, not as an origin (read through a 2024 article quoting the *Dīwān*; Dankoff and Kelly not opened; the *Dīwān*'s dates rest on the summary). — https://cyberleninka.ru/article/n/life-and-time-in-orkhon-yenisey-monuments/pdf (Ibodullaev, DOI 10.37547/ot/vol-01issue-02-12); https://exa.ai/library/publication/j7glhk4jwyl (Guliyev 2024, quoting Kashgari); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D%C4%ABw%C4%81n_Lugh%C4%81t_al-Turk — full text; summary; summary.
19. "BP" counts back from 1 January 1950, before large-scale nuclear testing. Uncalibrated ages are "14C yr BP"; calibrated ages "cal BP". IntCal20 (Reimer et al. 2020, CC BY 4.0) is the Northern Hemisphere curve for 0–55 cal kBP. — documented. — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Before_Present (summary); https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/handle/10023/20465 (abstract).
20. James Mill (1817) split India's past into Hindu, Muslim and British periods by rulers' religion; he never went to India. Romila Thapar says this caused "significant distortions". — documented (Mill); interpretive (Thapar). — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_History_of_British_India — summary.

**Unit framing (interpretive):** a converted date is a computation plus a choice; a period is an argument by a source. Every Atlas date should keep its original calendar beside its CE range.
