## F1.30a Counterfactual history: a history of making that did not happen

Change one fact about the past, trace what follows through sources, and label the result fiction: done this way, a "what if" tests which causes a history of making really rests on, and it can seed a world.

**Spec**

| Row | Content |
|---|---|
| Essential question | If one fact in a history of making changed, what would follow, and how would you know which parts you invented? |
| Opening move | **Problem first (form 1).** A scroll ends on a prediction: "Remove the Battle of Talas (751). Does Samarkand still make paper?" Learners choose yes, no or unsure. **Claim:** Chinese prisoners taken at Talas brought papermaking west (al-Tha'alibi, 11th century; retold in *Aramco World*, 1964). **Counter-claim:** paper was used in Samarkand, and probably made there, decades before the battle (Bloom). The reveal is a Sogdian merchant's letter on paper, written 313–314 and addressed to Samarkand. Removing the battle changes nothing, so the battle was not the cause. **Case:** A. |
| Lab or main interactive | **Rule Card and branch view.** The Rule Card (form 13) has four parts: one changed variable, dated and placed; what stays fixed; three consequences, each with a source and confidence; the label "Fiction". The branch view greys the changed node in the learner's Atlas lineage and redraws later edges as dashed, each tagged "sourced" or "invented", with a running count. No consequence saves without a source or the "invented" tag. |
| Concept | About 600 words. (1) A counterfactual tests a cause: if the outcome survives the change, the cause was elsewhere. (2) Two readings: Ferguson (1997) allows only alternatives people at the time considered; Evans (2014) calls most counterfactuals politics or wishful thinking, accepting short-range ones. (3) The discipline: one variable; minimal rewrite (Tetlock and Belkin); sourced consequences; stop where sources stop; label it fiction. (4) Design's tradition: Narrative Environments, design fiction (Bleecker 2009) and speculative design (Dunne and Raby 2013) build worlds forward; this unit points the tools backwards. (5) Limits: atrocity is never the variable; some histories belong to the communities who would author them. Forward to M1.9, M1.10, F1.27, F1.30. |
| Cases | Three object stories. **A. Paper without Talas** (Central Asia, Islamic world, China; 313–c. 800). Variable: no battle in 751. Consequence: none, since paper was already there; the case exposes a legend. Objects: a 9th-century parchment Qur'an folio beside a 12th-century paper leaf (Cleveland). **B. Red that never crossed** (Andes and Mesoamerica, Europe, Ottoman and Safavid lands, China; 1520s–1800s). Variable: cochineal is never shipped to Seville. Sourced consequences: Venetian velvets, Dutch scarlet, painters' lakes, *yang hong*. It shows the distance limit: each step outward is weaker. Object: an Inca-colonial tunic fragment (AIC). **C. The imitation Java accepted** (Java, Gold Coast, Netherlands; 1850s–1900s). Variable: Javanese buyers accept Dutch imitation batik. Consequence: less reason for a West African market, which shows what wax print owes to West African buyers and traders. Object: a North Coast batik sarong (Cleveland). |

It never imitates a museum record (no invented accession number or holder). Premise in PRM, sources in SRC. |
| Apply — briefer | **The brief that world would have issued:** for example, a Venetian velvet commission of 1600 without cochineal, or a Gold Coast trader's 1890 order where Java bought the imitations. It names the sourced consequence it rests on, marks invented assumptions and names one maker it leaves out. PRM and SRC. |

**2. Protocol screen**

- **Atrocity rule.** No atrocity (enslavement, genocide, conquest) is ever the changed variable or a plot device. "What if the Atlantic slave trade had not happened?" is not a design exercise: it makes millions of lives a lever for an aesthetic result. Violence in the actual history stays in the fixed layer, named plainly.
- **Case A.** Talas was a battle; its prisoners were captives (Du Huan among them), never "carriers of technology".
- **Case C.** The Asante king offered enslaved people and prisoners of war to Dutch recruiters; they were "nominally" volunteers (claim 15). People, never goods.
- **Case B.** Colonial production is named, with both readings of the *repartimiento* (claim 13). A Notice on Ñuu Savi (Mixtec), Bë'enzaa (Zapotec) and Inca-colonial material. Content note on tocapu, once restricted to the Sapa Inca. The AIC record gives no funerary context.
- **Qur'an leaves.** Scripture: shown whole, never cropped for ornament or used as texture in learners' objects.
- **Whose counterfactual.** A world without colonisation is for the communities concerned to author (MOV054), not outside learners.
- **Fiction label** on every made object and brief; nothing imitates a holder's record format.

**8. Success criteria**

1. The learner's Rule Card names exactly one changed variable with a date and place, lists at least two things held fixed, and gives three consequences, each with a source and a confidence word.
2. In the branch view, the learner marks the step where sources stop. Every step after it is tagged "invented", and the made object or brief is labelled fiction.
3. After the Stranger test, the learner revises at least one rule that caused drift and records the change in SRC. Whether counterfactuals are good history is not scored; the learner states both readings (claim 16).
