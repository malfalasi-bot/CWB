## F1.9a West Asia before Islam, read through the account

West Asia holds the earliest securely dated records of making, and each record, like each object's own paper trail, counts some hands and leaves others out.

**Spec**

| Row | Content |
|---|---|
| Essential question | What did people in West Asia write down about making, and who does each account count, name or leave out? |
| Opening move | **Object first, with a prediction (forms 15, 3).** A clay tablet fills the screen, unlabelled (Met 1988.433.2). Prompt: "What were the earliest texts about: a king, a god, a story or a store?" One tap on one of the four; the reveal follows within seconds. **Claim:** writing began to record kings and gods. **Counter-claim:** the earliest securely dated Mesopotamian tablets are accounts; this one shares out barley and emmer, about 3100–2900 BCE. Egypt's Abydos labels date to about 3320 BCE; whether Egypt invented writing separately is argued. Case: the barley account. |
| Lab or main interactive | **The Account Reader (form 4).** Five open records, each read twice. **Its account:** what, how many, for whom, from where, who wrote or sealed it, who appears only as a number. **Its own account:** the chain in the holder's record: excavated and divided, held before 1970, sold by a dealer, or blank. Records: the barley account (Met 1988.433.2); a list of Umma temple workers (Walters 48.1767); a stone land transfer (Walters 41.219); a letter about textiles (Met 66.245.11); an Elamite sheep tally (Met 36.30.62). The ancient account is often detailed; the modern one often thin. |
| Concept | 600 words. **The account as a technology of making.** Counting before writing (tokens and bullae, a debated ancestry). Tablets that allot grain, wool and labour. Seals as signatures. Letters as commercial accounts. Recipes as written technique: Nineveh's glass texts, Tapputi's perfume. Scripts beyond cuneiform: Proto-Elamite, still unread; South Arabian letters cut into palm stalks. **What accounts leave out:** makers' voices (Lamassī is a rare exception), the miners behind the copper, people listed only for rations. **The second account:** provenance. Many West Asian objects came through the market, and Luristan is its textbook case; others came through licensed excavation and division of finds (partage). Civilisation names (Sumerian, Elamite, Persian, Sabaean) are labels over many groups. |
| Cases | **1. Copper from Magan (WA-ARB, WA-MES).** Magan is the Oman peninsula, today Oman and the UAE. Its copper is known from Sumerian trade texts, a later copy of Sargon's inscription, and Nanni's complaint to Ea-nasir (British Museum 131236, c. 1750 BCE, link out). Lead isotopes tie Mesopotamian copper partly to Oman. The Hafit and Umm an-Nar periods take their names from sites in today's UAE. Aside: Belovode (Serbia) smelted copper about 5000 BCE, earlier than any secure Near Eastern evidence, which is argued. **2. The empire's ledger (WA-IRN).** Met 36.30.62 counts 205 sheep in Elamite, about 500 BCE; the Met dug it in 1935 and received it by partage in 1936. Its siblings, the Persepolis tablets, went to Chicago on loan in 1936. In 2018 the US Supreme Court ruled that victims of a 1997 bombing in Jerusalem could not seize them under the law they cited. The last tranche returned to Iran in 2019. **3. A breath and a name (WA-LEV).** The earliest closely datable blown glass is from Jerusalem, about 50–40 BCE. Within a century Ennion signed mould-blown vessels in Greek (Met 17.194.226). Nineveh's glass recipes name no glassmaker. |

- **Met 66.245.11:** Assur households held enslaved women (Michel 2021), named as people.
- **Persepolis tablets:** coerced labour is argued (F1.23). Name plainly the bombing victims who brought the 2018 case.
- **South Arabian sticks:** war in Yemen since 2015 and looting (CIV066). Link out only, to the holders' records, with no image on our pages. ICOM's Emergency Red List for Yemen (2018) lists writing "on wooden sticks" among the categories most at risk, and Yemen's antiquities law (No. 21/1994) forbids export. Content note: "Content note: inscribed sticks like these are on ICOM's Red List for Yemen, where war has fed looting. Most were found by local people in the 1970s; how each left Yemen is not recorded."
