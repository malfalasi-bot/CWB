# Ballet as a made world: the blueprint of the full unit

25 October 2026. The first Atlas concept unit, built on `briefs/ATLAS_CONCEPT_UNIT_TEMPLATE_v2.md`. It uses all the research in `../research/` (about 208,000 words in 26 files) and the decisions of 25 October: history first, one worked example and a macro view, records kept apart from teaching, content versioned next to its research.

## The unit in one paragraph

Ballet is the clearest case the course has of a world made on purpose: a concept someone had, built from named sources by a team, given rules a stranger can read, staged as an evening an audience enters, carried round the world by institutions, sold as objects and images, and remade for four hundred years. The unit tells that story from the court of 1581 to the remakes of today, follows one work, *Giselle*, through every chapter where it changed, and sets the rest of the repertory side by side on comparison walls, so the learner leaves with both a whole worked example and a library of precedents.

## Through-question and theme

- **Through-question:** how do you turn ideas, bodies and things into a world other people can enter, and keep it alive after you?
- **Theme:** ballet's worlds were made by many hands, to a brief, under rules, for an audience; the name on the poster is one of them.

## The worked example and the macro view

- **Worked example:** *Giselle*, followed through chapters 0, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10 and 11: Gautier's idea (1841), its two worlds, its score and mime, Petipa's revisions and Stepanov's notation in St Petersburg, Sergeyev's 1924 Paris staging from that notation, Alicia Alonso's Havana version, and the remakes in Louisiana, a Swedish asylum, a garment factory and South Africa.
- **Counter-example:** *Swan Lake*, at chapters 4, 5 and 10 (the lost pas de deux, the Moscow failure and the 1895 remake, the Ek and Masilo remakes beside their *Giselle*).
- **Macro view:** twelve motif walls, one or two per chapter, drawn from the 148-work register, each entered through a pair. The topic also supplies entries to the program's field atlas.

## Chapters

Hours are for the full unit. Each chapter opens on the story named, follows the template's seven moves (§4.1), and ends with a say-back, an Apply task in each lane and a closing contribution.

### 0. What a ballet is, as a made world (2 hours)
- **Opens on:** a critic reading Heine. In 1841 Théophile Gautier read a passage about the Wilis, ghosts of brides who dance men to death, and, by his own account, wrote "Les Wilis, a ballet" at the top of a sheet. His first draft was rejected; a librettist built the plot; a director needed a vehicle for a new star; a choreographer who made her dances went uncredited.
- **Teaches:** a ballet is a concept with an author who is often not the choreographer; the lineage of ballet in one walk, 1581 to 2016; how to read a work as a made concept (brief, sources, idea, world rules, execution, launch, afterlife); ballet as one dance among many.
- **Worked example:** *Giselle* in miniature.
- **Sources:** `works/W1` §5; `scope/06`; `works/W4`.

### 1. The court designs a world, 1581 to 1763 (3 hours)
- **Opens on:** the king as the rising sun. On 23 February 1653, at the end of a twelve-hour night of thieves, witches and gods, the fourteen-year-old Louis XIV appeared as the Sun. The ballet was a minister's brief for a young king after a civil war.
- **Teaches:** court ballet as a team-made spectacle (poet, composer, dance master, designer, machinist); the stage as a machine; the Académie Royale de Danse (1661); five positions and turnout as a standard set of parts; Feuillet's notation (1700) and the 1704 ruling between Beauchamp and Feuillet over who invented it; Noverre's *Lettres* (1760) as a grammar written before the work, and its refusals.
- **Works:** *Balet comique de la Royne* (1581); *Ballet royal de la Nuit* (1653); *Médée et Jason* (1763).
- **Object study:** the stage as a made object, part 1: Torelli's machines.
- **Wall:** court, power and spectacle.
- **Sources:** `scope/01` §A; `scope/04` §1.1; `works/W1` §§1–3; `scope2/G2` §3.1.

### 2. The body made to a standard (3 hours)
- **Opens on:** a teacher paid by the finished dancer. In Moscow in 1773, Filippo Beccari was engaged to train foundlings, probably paid per dancer he produced: 250 roubles for a soloist, 150 for a member of the corps.
- **Teaches:** the school as a machine for making dancers (St Petersburg 1738, Moscow 1773, the Opéra school); technique as a specification; syllabi as exportable technology; pointe as a technique before it was a technology (Hammond: the earliest practitioners were not only women and not Romantic ballerinas); the body ideal, who it left out and what it costs (injury, eating disorders, ageing).
- **Object study:** the pointe shoe, part 1: the idea came before the shoe (Taglioni's darned slipper of about 1842, which has no hard toe).
- **Wall:** none; this chapter feeds the program's body-and-access lens.
- **Sources:** `scope/01` §1.3; `scope/02`; `research/pass2_body.md`.

### 3. The theatre sells a dream: Paris, 1820 to 1870 (4 hours)
- **Opens on:** a tenor's idea. In 1832 Adolphe Nourrit, a singer at the Paris Opéra, turned a story about a male sprite into a ballet about a female sylph; on 12 March Marie Taglioni danced it under gaslight, in white, on her toes.
- **Teaches:** the Opéra run as a private business from 1831, and what the market changed (Garafola: pensions cut, stars paid for drawing power, women dancing men's roles); gas and the white act as a lighting effect; two worlds and two ways of moving as the Romantic grammar; *Giselle* as the full worked example; the ballerina's name as a brand (dresses "à la Taglioni").
- **Works:** *La Sylphide* (1832) and Bournonville's Danish version (1836); *Giselle* (1841), in full; *Coppélia* (1870).
- **Object studies:** the tutu, part 1 (the Romantic bell); the stage, part 2 (gas and the white act); the ballerina's name as a brand.
- **Walls:** two worlds; the white act.
- **Sources:** `works/W1` §§4–6; `scope/04` §1.2; `scope/05` §1.1; `products/` B7.

### 4. Inside a ballet: music, mime and the craft of choreography (3 hours)
- **Opens on:** a pas de deux lost for seventy-six years. In Moscow in 1877 a ballerina wanted a better showpiece; Tchaikovsky wrote new music to fit steps already made. The music vanished until a rehearsal score turned up in the Bolshoi's archive in 1953, and Balanchine made a new duet to it in 1960.
- **Teaches:** the score as the time-frame of the world, and as a made and edited object (specialist ballet composers and symphonists; the conductor's edits; the rehearsal pianist); mime as a sign language an audience learned to read, and why reformers cut it; the craft of choreography as a component library (the class as vocabulary, the pas de deux of entrée, adagio, variations and coda, the corps as geometry, the divertissement); the dancer as interpreter and co-author; where ballet ends and modern dance begins.
- **Worked example:** *Giselle*'s motifs and its mime scene.
- **Wall:** the pas de deux across a century.
- **Sources:** `scope2/G1`; `works/W4`.

### 5. The empire specifies: St Petersburg and Moscow, 1847 to 1903 (4 hours)
- **Opens on:** a letter of proposal. On 13/25 May 1888 Ivan Vsevolozhsky, director of the Imperial Theatres, wrote to Tchaikovsky proposing a ballet on *The Sleeping Beauty*. Marius Petipa then sent the composer a plan that fixed each number's action, character, metre and length in bars.
- **Teaches:** the commission as a specification (obligations as a design tool); the divertissement as a collection of numbers held by one world; costume families (the corps identical, the soloists varied, the principal the anchor); the Imperial scenic studios; Stepanov's notation (1892) and the scores Sergeyev would carry west; "character" dance and classical dance as two registers, and the national and racial types in them.
- **Works:** *The Sleeping Beauty* (1890), in full; *La Bayadère* (1877); *Swan Lake* (1877 and 1895); *The Nutcracker* (1892). *Giselle* in St Petersburg.
- **Object studies:** costume as a family; the tutu, part 2 (the classical plate); *The Nutcracker* as a product world, part 1 (the figure older than the ballet; the suite played before the ballet).
- **Walls:** the exotic other, each Orientalist work paired with one made from inside the culture it shows; the fairy tale.
- **Sources:** `works/W1` §§7–10; `scope/04` §2.1; `products/` B2, B6, C.

### 6. The night and the house (3 hours)
- **Opens on:** a staircase built as a stage. When the Palais Garnier opened on 5 January 1875, its grand staircase was designed for the audience to watch itself arrive.
- **Teaches:** who the audience was and who could get in (the court, the subscribers and the paid claque, the balletomanes, free seats for factory workers in Moscow in 1919 to 1921, Cape Town's whites-only house of 1971); the evening as designed time (doors, bells, darkness, intervals, applause, the curtain call, the programme as souvenir); the building as the first expression of a world (Garnier, the Mariinsky and Mariinsky II, the Bolshoi, the Royal Opera House, Sydney, Oslo's walkable roof, Cairo's two opera houses of 1869 and 1988, the Royal Opera House Muscat of 2011 and its dress code, Dubai Opera of 2016); access as a redesign of the whole world; front stage and back stage; the experience economy and the service blueprint.
- **Object study:** the house as a world.
- **Wall:** opera houses as worlds, by region.
- **Sources:** `scope2/G2`.

### 7. The committee and the avant-garde, 1909 to 1960 (4 hours)
- **Opens on:** a winter of meetings. Over 1908 and 1909, friends of Serge Diaghilev met to invent a Russian fairy tale for Paris; two composers dropped out before Igor Stravinsky, aged 27, was hired; the choreographer dictated the music scene by scene with a piano standing in for a wall.
- **Teaches:** Diaghilev's method (commissioning people, not stories; the veto; the season as a yearly drop); the painter as designer and the scene painter as executant (Golovin and Sapunov; Picasso and Shervashidze); stage to street (Bakst for Paquin, Poiret, Chanel's *Train Bleu*); Fokine's five principles as refusals that generate rules; the Soviet *drambalet*; Balanchine's bare stage; the Bauhaus *Triadic Ballet* as a design curriculum; the émigré diaspora and syllabi as exports. *Giselle* staged from notation in Paris, 1924.
- **Works:** *The Firebird* (1910), in full; *The Rite of Spring* (1913); *Le Train Bleu* (1924); *The Flames of Paris* (1932); *Agon* (1957).
- **Object studies:** stage to street; the season as a collection (posters and programmes); the tutu, part 3 (Karinska); the stage, part 3 (the painted cloth).
- **Walls:** the machine age; plotless and music-led.
- **Sources:** `works/W2`; `scope/04`; `scope/03` §1.6; `products/` B9.

### 8. States and routes, 1945 to 1990 (3 hours)
- **Opens on:** *Swan Lake* in a ruined city. In August 1946, in Tokyo, a company assembled by dancers trained by Russian émigrés in Harbin and Shanghai staged the earliest known complete *Swan Lake* in Japan.
- **Teaches:** the five ways ballet travelled (state teachers, examined syllabi, émigrés, the touring star, the presenting opera house); one Soviet model making different worlds in Beijing, Havana, Tokyo and Cairo; national ballets built from folk material; who was kept out, and Dance Theatre of Harlem (1969); how ballet reached the Gulf (schools from 1974, syllabi, opera houses, licensing), with the structure for the regional slot.
- **Works:** *The Red Detachment of Women* (1964); Alonso's *Giselle* (Havana, 1945 to 1963); *The White-Haired Girl* (Tokyo, 1955); *El-Leila El-Kebira* (Cairo, 2001 or 2002); *Spartacus* (1968).
- **Walls:** folk as national idiom (Baku, Mexico City, Kazan, Beijing, Ankara, Manila, Seoul); a woman against family and patriarch.
- **Sources:** `scope/01` §§1.8, F; `works/W3` §§1–3, 10, 11; `planning/decisions/BENCHMARKS.md` L5.

### 9. How the world meets ballet: media, childhood and popular culture (3 hours)
- **Opens on:** swans on every channel. On 19 August 1991, during the coup in Moscow, Soviet television showed *Swan Lake* in place of the news (why it was scheduled is contested).
- **Teaches:** most people meet a world through its media, not its premiere: souvenir prints and the string that held up a dancer's arm in a studio photograph; film from Pavlova (1916) to *The Red Shoes* (1948), *Billy Elliot* and *Black Swan*; television, cinema relays (from 2008) and social media; ballet and childhood (music boxes, the pink tutu, classes, exams and competitions as the pipeline that makes the next dancers and audiences); manga and anime in Japan; *The Nutcracker* as a transmedia world from Hoffmann (1816) to Disney (2018).
- **Object studies:** practice clothes into everyday wear (the leotard and the ballet flat); *The Nutcracker* as a product world, part 2.
- **Wall:** the ballerina on screen.
- **Sources:** `scope2/G3`; `products/` B3, B4, B6.

### 10. Remaking (3 hours)
- **Opens on:** *Giselle* moved to Louisiana. In 1984 Dance Theatre of Harlem set *Giselle* among free people of colour in 1840s Louisiana, keeping the steps and changing the world around them.
- **Teaches:** the five modes of remaking (re-setting, re-psychologising, re-politicising, re-motivating, re-excavating) and the line between what is kept and what is freed, which is the concept; skin tone, from dancers colouring their own shoes to the brown shoe of 2018; repair after harm (Final Bow for Yellowface, 2017; the Paris Opéra report of 2021).
- **Works:** Creole *Giselle* (1984); Ek's *Giselle* (1982) and *Swan Lake* (1987); Bourne's *Swan Lake* (1995); Masilo's *Swan Lake* (2010) and *Giselle* (2017); Khan's *Giselle* (2016); Ratmansky's *The Sleeping Beauty* (2015).
- **Wall:** the remake families (*Giselle*, *Swan Lake*, *The Rite of Spring*).
- **Sources:** `works/W3`; `scope/03` §2.3; `scope/01` §1.9.

### 11. Keeping a world alive: money, ownership, labour, handover and technology (4 hours)
- **Opens on:** a suitcase of notations. Nikolai Sergeyev left Russia after 1917 with the notebooks that recorded the Imperial repertory; he earned his living staging from them in the West; Harvard bought them in 1969 and digitised them in 2024.
- **Teaches:** who pays (court, market, state, philanthropy) and the cost disease; *The Nutcracker* as the anchor that funds the repertory; dancers as a workforce (contracts, unions, the Paris pensions); who owns a ballet (US copyright from 1978, Hanya Holm's 1952 registration, the Graham ruling, the Balanchine Trust, Ashton's bequests); handover documents (notation, the répétiteur, production bibles, technical riders, revival packages; archive and repertoire); technology and AI (Cunningham's software from about 1989, Forsythe's CD-ROM that stopped playing, McGregor's AI work of 2019, datasets without consent, the likeness clauses of 2025).
- **Object studies:** the pointe shoe, part 2: a product line today (makers' marks, piecework, three ways to name variants, skin-tone ranges, the endangered craft); ballet now as fashion and identity.
- **Wall:** who owns the steps.
- **Sources:** `scope/05`; `scope2/G4`; `products/` B1, B5, B8; the built page `learner/the-shoe/`.

### 12. Ballet as a made world: what a world-builder keeps (2 hours)
- **Opens on:** the poster, the programme and the sole. "The dancer is on the poster. The designer is in the programme. The maker is a symbol on a sole."
- **Teaches:** the lessons gathered (below); the field-atlas entries the topic supplies; the learner's own world placed in a lineage.
- **Apply (Maker):** write your world's lineage in five dated links; one rule a stranger can read; the credit line naming every hand; the handover a stranger would need.
- **Apply (Briefer):** write a commission in the form Vsevolozhsky and Petipa used: the proposal, then the obligations by part, length and character; then judge three responses against it.

**Total: about 41 hours.** Orientation path: chapter 0 and the chapter 12 lessons (2 hours). Practice path: chapters 0, 3, 5, 7, 10 and 12 (about 18 hours), or chosen by route.

## What a world-builder keeps

From the research and the reviews, stated plainly, each taught in the chapter named:
1. The idea rarely comes from the person credited with it (0, 7).
2. Every world is built from sources you can name (3, 7).
3. A concept leaves documents: a proposal, a scenario, a specification, designs, notation (1, 5).
4. Worlds are built from rules and refusals (1, 3, 7).
5. A world must teach its audience to read it (4).
6. Strong worlds run on a small, strict, reusable grammar (1, 4).
7. A world is experienced in time, and the time is designed (4, 6).
8. The building is the first expression of the world, and it decides who belongs (6).
9. The collection holds the object: the divertissement, the season, the costume family (5, 7).
10. Most people meet a world through its media, not its premiere (9).
11. A world recruits its next makers from childhood (2, 9).
12. The performer co-authors the world every night (4).
13. A world is defined against its neighbours (4, 7).
14. Remaking is how a concept stays alive; the line between kept and freed is the concept (10).
15. The credit line is a design decision (0, 7, 11).
16. A world survives only if its paper and its people are handed over together (11).
17. The same world is read differently by different audiences (5, 6, 8).
