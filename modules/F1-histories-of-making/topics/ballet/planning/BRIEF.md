# Ballet, as a history of making: the teaching brief

Written from the two research passes (`pass1_dossier.md`, `pass2_verify.md`, `pass2_body.md`, `pass2_presentation.md`), 20 October 2026. This is the one page the unit is designed from. Nothing below is a page design; it says what the subject teaches, in what order a first-time reader should meet it, and what the sources cannot support.

## Thesis

Ballet looks like the least material of the arts, a body moving in light, and it is built on some of the most labour-intensive making in the theatre: a shoe hand-made in an hour and worn out in a night, a skirt sewn for forty hours and never seen up close, a stage machined for flight. The dancers and designers are named; the makers almost never are. That gap is the unit's subject.

## Seven claims worth teaching

1. **Pointe is a concept that came before its technology.** The weightless figure was wanted on stage (Didelot's wires, 1796; Brugnoli 1823; Taglioni 1832) decades before the hardened box made it safe; who was "first" is contested and should be taught as a dispute. *Sources:* pointemagazine.com; ballet.org.uk; Wikipedia "Amalia Brugnoli" (Migel 1981). Confidence: the sequence documented; the first contested.
2. **A pointe shoe is a paper-and-paste object with a one-night life.** Hessian, paper, paste and satin, turned inside out and hammered; a principal can wear three pairs in a performance; a company's shoe bill is a standing six-figure budget line that it raises by public appeal. *Sources:* Freed; getballetbox.com (construction); CBS (Tiler Peck); SOFII (RBO appeal). Confidence: construction documented; counts are company or fundraising figures and are taught as such.
3. **The maker's mark is the one place a maker's name reaches the stage.** Frederick Freed made his makers stamp the sole so returns could be traced; dancers began ordering by the mark. Michael Cripps, mark "J", has made shoes since 1979, 152 pairs a week. *Sources:* freedoflondon.com; pointemagazine.com "Custom creations"; Spitalfields Life 2013 (16 named makers). Confidence: the system documented; the makers' names rest on one journalist's visit.
4. **The tutu is engineered, and its labour is counted only by the makers themselves.** Ten to twelve layers, a hoop, a basque; about 38 hours at Dutch National Ballet (Oliver Haller); 500 metres of hand-scalloped net and two weeks for one Kitri tutu at Birmingham Royal Ballet (Isabelle Homer, Katie Green). *Confidence:* documented and named. Its own attribution dispute: the Romantic tutu is "sometimes credited" to Eugène Lami, and the design has not been found.
5. **The stage is a machine, and its lighting changed what a dancer could be.** Gas at the Paris Opéra from 6 February 1822 (probable); the Romantic white act is a gaslight effect; the Savoy's incandescent stage on 28 December 1881 (documented, *The Times*); the Garnier's 5 % rake; the sprung and vinyl floor in the twentieth century. *Confidence:* dates documented or probable; the *Sylphide* flying machinery not found at source.
6. **The body carries the cost.** Royal Ballet dancers: 3.9 (women) and 3.1 (men) medical-attention injuries per 1,000 hours over five seasons (Mattiussi et al., BJSM 2021); 19–27 hours a week by rank, up to about 50 (Shaw et al., 2022); retirement at 42 at the Paris Opéra, defended by a strike performed on the Garnier steps on 24 December 2019. *Confidence:* peer-reviewed and documented; the 1698 origin of the pension probable only.
7. **Ballet's map is wider than its history.** The canon runs Paris → St Petersburg → Paris → New York; the companies and schools run further: St Petersburg 1738 by decree, Beijing 1954 by Soviet teachers, Havana 1948 and 1959 (Alicia Alonso), Cape Town 1934 (Dulcie Howes), Harlem 1969, and the flesh-tone tights a founding ballerina insisted on (Llanchie Stevenson; the year not found). *Confidence:* documented or probable; the dates the sources could not give are said so.

## Three candidate spines

- **A. The body outward** (the shoe → the tutu → the stage → the company → the dispute). Case: it starts where a first-time reader already is, with the dancer's foot, and every step adds one craft; the chronology arrives through the objects (Taglioni's shoe, Pavlova's swan, Bakst's Schéhérazade) rather than before them. Risk: history can feel like background.
- **B. One ballet followed through time** (*The Sleeping Beauty*: St Petersburg 1890 → London 1921 → Covent Garden 1946 → today). Case: one work, four makings, so the reader sees the same thing re-made by different hands, budgets and bodies. Risk: three of the four stagings have restricted images; it leans on description.
- **C. One bench read outward** (Freed's Hackney factory: a pair of shoes followed from paste to stage). Case: the clearest making story with named makers and the maker's mark; a twenty-minute read with one room, one object, one chain of names. Risk: it is a unit about one firm's shoe, and the firm's own numbers are PR.

**Recommended: A, with C as its first chapter.** The shoe opens it because the shoe is where the body, the craft and the money meet in one object, and the maker's mark gives the unit its thesis in a picture.

## The learner's path, about twenty minutes

1. **Open on a worn pair of pointe shoes** (a CC0 photograph is not yet in hand; the ROH factory film or a Freed still is the placeholder) and the maker's mark on the sole. Three sentences: what it is made of, how long it lasts, whose mark that is. *Object 1.*
2. **Taglioni, 1832.** The Carnavalet lithograph (CC0): the figure on its toes, the bell skirt, the gaslight; one paragraph on the concept that preceded the technology; the pointe dispute posed as the unit's one argument: *who was earliest, and why does the answer keep moving?* *Object 2.*
3. **The tutu, taken apart in making order** (borrowed from the London Museum's Pavlova page): wearer → material → why that material then → construction → hours, with the makers named where they are and "maker unknown" where they are not. The Pavlova swan costume is link-out only (London Museum ©); the Degas *Dancers Practicing at the Barre* (Met, CC0) stands for the corps and its labour. *Object 3.*
4. **The stage as a machine.** One drawing of the Garnier's rake and traps if a public-domain one can be found, else prose; the gas and electric dates; what the white act owed to the lamp.
5. **The company.** The Ballets Russes receipt for $500 in Rio, 1917 (Library of Congress): a company as a touring economy; a short span list of the schools (1738 · 1934 · 1948 · 1954 · 1969) as the wider map, with the dates the sources could not give left blank and said so.
6. **The body.** The injury and workload figures with their papers; the Garnier steps, 24 December 2019, in one photograph if a free one exists, else the sentence.
7. **End on a living maker:** Michael Cripps at his bench, mark "J"; and the line that Heritage Crafts lists pointe-shoe making as critically endangered in the UK (two businesses).

Where a device earns its place: a timeline strip under step 5 (the schools), because five dates across three centuries are hard to hold in prose; a step-through of the tutu's layers in step 3, because the order of layers is the point. Where it does not: no globe (the places are few and a list carries them), no scene player, no lane chart; this unit is read, not played.

## Figures solid enough to teach, and figures that are PR

Teach as fact, with the paper named: the injury and workload rates (Mattiussi 2021; Shaw 2022; Allen 2012); the Savoy 1881; the Garnier rake; the tutu hours from named staff (DNB, BRB, London Museum); the Minden patents (1990, 1991, 1998; "1993" is a launch, not a patent). Teach only with the label "the company's figure": every pointe-shoe count and budget; Freed's annual output (150,000 to 333,000 depending on who is quoted); the Opéra's €8 million strike loss. Contested or unfound: the 1698 pension; the barre and mirror dates; Brugnoli 1822/1823; the "riot" word (1922 or 1924).

## What the subject leaves out, and how the unit says so

The unit's last line is a list of the people it could not name: the dyers, cutters, wig makers and stage hands at the Royal Opera House, the Paris Opéra and the Bolshoi, whose institutions publish their work and not their names; the sixteen Freed makers known only from one visit; the net and satin's own makers. And the places the sources barely reach: Japan's earliest studio (Kamakura, 1920s, date unconfirmed), South Africa under apartheid, the Brazilian tours of 1913 and 1917 that the canon treats as footnotes.
