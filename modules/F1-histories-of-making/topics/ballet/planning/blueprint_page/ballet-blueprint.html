In short

  • Every chapter now opens on history. Each of the unit's thirteen chapters starts on a dated, sourced story from the research (a critic reading Heine in 1841, a king as the rising sun in 1653, a suitcase of notations carried out of Russia). There are no prediction or quiz openers anywhere in the unit.
  • The research covers every angle. Four gap passes (music, mime and the craft of choreography; the night, the audience and the house; media, childhood and popular culture; technology, AI and handover) added about 41,500 words. Ballet's research is now 26 files and about 208,000 words.
  • One worked example and a macro view, together. Giselle runs through eight of the thirteen chapters; fourteen comparison walls set the rest of the 148-work repertory side by side.
  • The long version is now the template for every novel concept in the Atlas: art forms, movements, schools, styles, networks.
  • Learner content sits beside its research and cannot change silently. A check run on every push fails if a page's text changes without a new version, and warns when the research under a page has moved.

The worked example and the macro view

You asked why we teach from one narrow point, and whether we are losing the macro perspective. The study (planning/decisions/EXEMPLAR_STUDY.md) found that the two directions are not rivals. Novices learn fastest from one example worked in full; they transfer what they learned only when they then compare varied cases side by side, with the principle named after the comparison. One example alone gets copied. Many examples alone leave fragments.

Inside the ballet unit

The unit does both. Giselle is followed from Gautier's idea in 1841 through St Petersburg, the 1924 Paris staging from notation, Havana, and its remakes. Swan Lake returns at three fixed points as the counter-example. Each chapter also holds one or two comparison walls, each entered through a pair of works.

Across the program

The same pattern, at program scale, is the study's design E. Your wish for an internationally known world is design D: a real world as the one spine. The study recommends keeping recognition and history where real worlds work well, and a fictional spine where nothing real can go.

History-first openers

Every module opens on a story from its lead world in a fixed cast of eight real worlds: the Bauhaus, The Nutcracker, One Thousand and One Nights, Morris & Co., the Sydney Opera House, the Ballets Russes, and LEGO and IKEA as text only.

The field atlas

For each Concept DNA field, three to five of those worlds side by side. It is a view of the existing Atlas, not a new library, and the regional slot adds a regional world to each module.

One historical twin

The Bauhaus, worked fully in M2 where the Concept DNA is taught, read forwards from its own documents (reserve: The Nutcracker).

One spine built forwards

Salt Road, given a researched lineage from real salt worlds, so it too opens on history. It is the world the engines generate from.

Why not a famous real world as the spine

LimitWhat happens with a real spine
The enginesThe spine is fed to the Stranger Lab and the area engines, which generate objects, images and briefs. The program forbids generated images of historical objects, people or places, so a real world cannot do that job.
RightsThe spine appears in every unit and export; in the commercial build most of a real world's best images become link-only.
CopyingA famous worked example is the one learners copy most ("make it look Bauhaus").
ReachOne culture's history would sit at the centre of every module.
SchemaThe learner schema holds the three fictional seed worlds; a real world is not a seed without a change.

The three strongest real candidates, for the twin and the openers: the Bauhaus, The Nutcracker and the Sydney Opera House. No data yet say which names learners in each region know; a ten-minute recognition check with the testers would settle it.

The unit, chapter by chapter

About 41 hours in full. An orientation path takes 2 hours (chapter 0 and the closing lessons); a practice path about 18 (chapters 0, 3, 5, 7, 10 and 12, or chosen by route). Each chapter opens on its story, demonstrates the concept through it, takes one or two works apart in the order of a design job, holds its object studies, sets works side by side on a wall, says what it teaches a world-builder, and ends with a say-back and an Apply task in each lane.

What a world-builder keeps

The unit closes on seventeen lessons, each taught in a named chapter. Five of them:

  • The idea rarely comes from the person credited with it.
  • A concept leaves documents: a proposal, a scenario, a specification, designs, notation.
  • The building is the first expression of the world, and it decides who belongs.
  • Remaking is how a concept stays alive; the line between what is kept and what is freed is the concept.
  • A world survives only if its paper and its people are handed over together.

The template for every Atlas concept

briefs/ATLAS_CONCEPT_UNIT_TEMPLATE_v2.md makes the long version the standard. A concept is taught in three layers: the record (research by angle, the works register, the claims register), the Atlas cards in the voice guide's four tiers, and the unit the learner reads.

No learner text is written until twelve research angles each have a scope file: lineage and institutions; makers and technique; the works as made concepts; the internal grammar; objects and workshops; space; the experience and the audience; money, labour and ownership; record, media and handover; routes and reach; afterlives and products; scholarship and archives. Three reviews follow, by readers who have not seen the plan: curriculum fit, subject gaps, and writing.

A comparable concept needs eight to twelve research passes, the three reviews, a blueprint, and about 40,000 words of learner text at full depth. The grouping page of the old template stays, as the record layer.

Content beside its research, versioned

Each concept now has one folder, topics/<topic>/, with research/, planning/ and learner/ side by side. Ballet moved there with its history kept; a notice stays at the old path.

  • Every learner file carries its version, status and sources. The sources are the research files it was written from, each fingerprinted as it stood at the time.
  • Text cannot change without a new version. Each earlier version is kept as a snapshot, and the file lists what changed in each.
  • Stale research is flagged. If a research file under a page changes, the check warns until the writer bumps the page or confirms it still holds.
  • Generated indexes list every file in a topic with its size and last change, and every learner file with its version and status.
  • The check runs on every push. The shoe page is the first file under it: version 0.3.0, in review, with 0.1.0 and 0.2.0 kept.

Release tags (ballet.the-shoe@0.3.0) are made locally, but this session's connection to GitHub refuses tag pushes. The version record does not depend on them; the tag can be pushed from your machine.
