# Board review, round 1: "The shoe" (Ballet, 1 of 4)

Reviewer: dance historian (Romantic ballet; Ballets Russes). Read: the learner page, both screenshots, `atlas/data/register/units/ballet.yaml`, `BRIEF.md`, and the two research passes. Four claims checked at source (Spitalfields Life 2013; Heritage Crafts; Wikipedia "Grishko"; CSM 1990; 4dancers 2012).

## Verdict

**2 / 5. Not yet citable.** The page's register discipline is better than most teaching pages, but its one load-bearing historical claim (who began the maker's mark, when and why) rests on no source at all, its one labour figure is mislabelled, and the thesis "the hands are still unnamed" is sustained by withholding some twenty names the project's own research holds. A student citing this would repeat a company legend as fact and a dressing-room figure as a shoe-sewing figure.

## Ranked issues

### HIGH

**1. The maker's-mark origin story is unsourced and stated flat.**
> "Frederick Freed began the marks after he opened his shop in Cecil Court in 1929, so a returned shoe could be traced to the hand that made it."

No source in the register says who began the marks, when, or why. I checked: Spitalfields Life 2013 says makers are "identified by the symbols they impress upon the shoes" and nothing about origin; 4dancers 2012 says only "their own personal stamp"; Freed's own history page does not mention the system (pass2_verify, line 79: "not found ... makers' marks"); the *Christian Science Monitor*'s 1990 factory visit ("A Pointe Well Made", https://www.csmonitor.com/1990/0111/pfreed.html) says "Each maker has his own stamp" and no more. The register has no record for this claim; `bal-freed-founded` covers the shop only. This sentence carries the page's thesis and it is the one sentence a historian would ask for the footnote on. The "returns" rationale reads like a company anecdote retold by journalists. Note also that a maker's mark is not peculiar to Freed: the AFP piece on Grishko says "each Grishko shoe carries the maker's number" (pass2_body, 1.2), so "even that is unusual" is wrong.
*Fix:* "Freed's makers each stamp a symbol on the sole and dancers order by it. Who began the practice, and when, the company does not say." Add a register record with `confidence: not found` for the origin, or find a primary (a Freed catalogue, a Fonteyn-era interview, the 1947 company registration file). Drop "even that is unusual" or document it.

**2. The 42-hours figure is passed off as a count of shoe labour, and "the only count" is an unprovable negative.**
> "their union said they spent about forty-two hours a month preparing for the stage against an allowance that covered six. That is a union's figure in a dispute. It is also the only count of this labour anyone has made."

Euronews (20 Dec 2023) reports the union's 42 hours as make-up, dressing and undressing; the research note itself says "pointe-shoe sewing not itemised" (pass2_body, 1.5). The paragraph is about sewing ribbons, darning and breaking in; the figure does not measure that. "The only count anyone has made" is unverifiable and almost certainly false (dancer time-use surveys exist; the page has not looked).
*Fix:* "their union put the time dancers spend getting ready for the stage, make-up and dressing included, at about forty-two hours a month against an allowance covering six. Shoe preparation is inside that figure somewhere; nobody has counted it separately." Source: https://www.euronews.com/2023/12/20/paris-opera-ballerinas-to-go-on-strike-over-christmas-period

**3. The second learning outcome is not met: the page promises three makers' names and gives none.**
> "explain why a ballet's makers are rarely named, and name three of them"

The page names Frederick Freed, Salvatore Capezio, Nikolay Grishko and Eliza Minden (founders, one an economics professor), Linnette Roe (a shoe-room supervisor) and "Crown" (a mark). No maker. Meanwhile pass2_body 1.6 holds sixteen named Freed workers from the 2013 visit; the brief names Michael Cripps (mark "J", making since 1979); the CSM in 1990 named George Gorgiou, 25 years at the bench, whose father made shoes for Fonteyn before him; the AFP named Sergei Murza, Olga Monakhova (27 years) and Irina Sobakina at Grishko; the *Moscow Times* (2011) named Roman Kukushkin, former Bolshoi Theatre shoemaker. The page withholds all of them and ends "The hands are still unnamed." That is a rhetorical move against the project's own evidence.
*Fix:* Name three (Gorgiou 1990; Crown/Cripps 2013; Murza 2020), with the journalist who named each, and restate the thesis as "the institutions do not name them; journalists have" (see Thesis, below).

**4. "Counted" is wrong, and the error has propagated into the register.**
> "a journalist visited the Hackney factory in 2013 and counted forty-one pairs a day at his bench."

The article says Crown is a maker "whose daily output is forty-one pairs". He was told, not counting. The register's `bal-crown-pairs-day` editor note repeats "Counted by the journalist". A page whose third outcome is to tell a record from a self-report should not upgrade a self-report into an observation. Source: https://spitalfieldslife.com/2013/05/16/at-freed-of-london-ltd-ballet-theatrical-shoemakers/
*Fix:* "was told he makes forty-one pairs a day"; correct the register note.

**5. The closing sentence is false about Moscow and silent about the real Russian story.**
> "Nothing here comes from the workshops of Paris, Moscow or St Petersburg, which publish their work and not their names."

The project has Moscow names (issue 3). More important, the Russian story a historian would insist on is that the Imperial and then Soviet theatres made shoes in their own workshops; the post-1991 firms (Grishko 1988/89; R-Class, founded 1991 by the Bolshoi's own shoemaker Kukushkin) were born out of those workshops. Grishko is not the Moscow equivalent of Freed; the Bolshoi's shop is, and the page does not know it exists.
*Fix:* Replace with: "The Bolshoi and the Mariinsky made their shoes in-house; the firms came out of those workshops after 1991. Their makers' names appear in one AFP dispatch and one *Moscow Times* piece, nowhere institutional." Source: https://www.themoscowtimes.com/2011/10/26/russian-ballet-shoes-leave-their-footprint-in-the-west-a10432

### MEDIUM

**6. The pointe chronology hedges the wrong thing and omits the two earliest witnesses.**
> "Dancers were rising onto the tips of their toes in the early 1820s, Amalia Brugnoli in Vienna among the earliest known, in soft slippers held by strength alone; the date is contested, and who did it earliest keeps moving."

Brugnoli's 1823 Vienna appearance (in Armand Vestris's *La Fée et le Chevalier*) is not what is contested; priority is. The register's own alternates record Geneviève Gosselin on pointe in Paris c. 1813–15 (Castil-Blaze), and the earliest *image* of pointe is Waldeck's 1821 lithograph of Fanny Bias in *Flore et Zéphire*; neither reaches the page. The whole sentence rests on Wikipedia and *Pointe Magazine*. The scholarly source is Sandra Noll Hammond, "Searching for the Sylph: Documentation of Early Developments in Pointe Technique", *Dance Research Journal* 19/2 (1987–88), https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/dance-research-journal/article/searching-for-the-sylph-documentation-of-early-developments-in-pointe-techninque/573B15DC8BF3CA8D0899B1BC76EEBAFF, with Ivor Guest, *The Romantic Ballet in Paris*. Hammond also shows early pointe was a momentary pose or promenade, not sustained dancing, which is the real distinction between the 1820s and Taglioni.
*Fix:* "Gosselin in Paris by about 1815, Bias in a lithograph of 1821, Brugnoli in Vienna in 1823: the dates are documented; who was 'first' depends on what counts as pointe, a held pose or a danced phrase." Mark Gosselin `probable`, Bias `documented` (the print), Brugnoli `documented`; mark the *priority* contested.

**7. Taglioni's shoes on 12 March 1832 are lore, presented as record.**
> "Marie Taglioni danced *La Sylphide* at the Paris Opéra on 12 March 1832 in darned slippers with a leather sole and no block"

The premiere date is documented. What she wore that night is not; this is the standard account (Barringer and Schlesinger) and the register marks it `probable`, but the page drops the hedge. Shoes attributed to Taglioni survive (a pair is reported at the Bibliothèque-musée de l'Opéra, BnF); ask the holder before describing them. Also the Carnavalet lithograph "being fetched" is the Chalon *Souvenir d'adieu* of 1845 (V&A dates it 8 Sept 1845), a farewell print thirteen years after the premiere: say so when it arrives, or a student will read it as an 1832 document.
*Fix:* "by the usual account, in darned satin slippers with a leather sole and no block"; caption the print as 1845.

**8. "From Italy, half a century later" is the textbook line, and "safe" is an anachronism.**
> "The block that made it safe came half a century later, from Italy."

The hardening of the box was gradual (1880s–1900s), carried into Russia by the Italian virtuose (Zucchi 1885, Brianza, Legnani 1893) whose stiff shoes the Russians at first derided, and finished by Pavlova's reinforced box and inset sole in the 1900s, which the research note has and the page drops. "Safe" imports a modern frame; nobody in 1890 described the box that way. The quiz feedback narrows the register's "1870s to 1890s" to "probably, the 1870s" with no warrant. Only source: *Pointe Magazine*.
*Fix:* "The box hardened by stages between the 1880s and the 1900s, carried from Milan to St Petersburg by Italian virtuose and reworked by Pavlova; the Italian attribution is the standard account, not yet seen at source." Quiz: "since the late nineteenth century".

**9. Two absolutes about materials are overdrawn.**
> "wood was never used, and a moulded plastic shank arrived with one maker only, probably in 1993; most companies still dance in paste."

"Never" is a universal negative drawn from one Freed maker's remark about Freed's block. "One maker only" is false in the present tense: Bloch's TMT B-Morph (thermoplastic) and others followed Gaynor Minden. The "probably" is on the wrong fact: the April 1993 launch is documented by the company and Lemelson-MIT; what is murky is the patent story (1990, 1991 on a 1988 priority; 1998 for the liner), which the page hides behind "its foam liner was patented in 1998".
*Fix:* "there is no wood in a Freed block, and no maker's account mentions any; a moulded thermoplastic shank came in with Gaynor Minden in 1993 and others have since copied it; most companies still dance in paste."

**10. The firm dates: Capezio was checked at source; Grishko's year is split; the page says otherwise.**
> "their own dates are probably right but not yet checked at source: Capezio's shop opposite the Metropolitan Opera from 1887, Grishko in Moscow from 1988"

pass2_verify (line 75) fetched capezio.com/about-us: "Established 1887", shop "near" the old Met, not "opposite". The register (`bal-capezio`: "Company page not fetched") is behind the verify pass. Grishko: AFP 2020 says 1988; Wikipedia and *Moscow Times* 2011 say 1989; the register logs the alternate and the display drops it.
*Fix:* "Capezio near the old Metropolitan Opera from 1887 (the firm's own date); Grishko in Moscow in 1988 or 1989, depending on who reports it." Update `bal-capezio` to `documented (company)`, `bal-grishko` display to "1988 or 1989".

**11. The Freed headcount range is narrower than the record, and undated.**
> "How many makers Freed has depends on who you ask and in which year, somewhere between twenty and thirty."

CSM 1990: "approximately 40 makers". Freed's own site (pass 1): 28 across three factories. The range over time is 20–40 and the page should date the figures, which is exactly what its third outcome teaches.
*Fix:* "about forty in 1990 by one visitor's count, twenty to thirty in 2012, twenty-eight by the firm's own site". Add the 1990 figure to `bal-makers-count`.

**12. "Most theatres name no shoemaker, cutter or dyer at all" is unverified.**
No programme has been checked. ROH programmes carry production credits; the V&A record for the Matisse costume names "the Paris atelier of Mme Muelle" (pass2_verify, line 56). The honest claim is narrower: the *dancers* and *designers* are on the poster, the workshop heads are sometimes in the small print, the bench is never.
*Fix:* Scope it, or check three programmes (ROH Collections holds them) and say what you found.

**13. Women's labour and the gender of the trade are invisible.**
The page writes "she" for the dancer and lets the reader assume the maker is a man, without saying so. At Freed the 2013 record shows the makers at the bench were men and the finishing, binding and pairing rooms were women (Sheila Goodman, Varsha Bahen, Rashimi Patel). The sewing of ribbons is women's unpaid labour by institutional design, and the Opéra strike is a women's labour dispute. Pointe as a women's technique is itself historical: men danced on pointe in the grotesque tradition, and Nijinska's memoir claims her brother could. A unit that reaches the Ballets Russes should say that pointe is half the trade, and that Nijinsky's shoes (soft, character) had makers too.
*Fix:* One paragraph: who sits at which bench, and who sews at home.

**14. The Opéra's economy is treated as timeless; it has a history the page could teach.**
"What a company pays" is a twentieth-century arrangement. In the nineteenth-century Opéra the corps bought its own shoes out of wages that Guest and Marian Smith (*Ballet and Opera in the Age of Giselle*) document; the Opéra's suppliers (*fournisseurs*) are in its ledgers (Archives nationales, AJ13). "Unnamed" is not "unrecorded": the names are in account books nobody has read for this purpose. That is the historian's version of the thesis, and it is stronger.

**15. Gamba and the pre-1929 trade are missing.**
Freed worked at Gamba, the "leading manufacturer" he left (4dancers; Freed's own page); Heritage Crafts dates the UK craft's origin to the 1920s. The page makes Freed the beginning; he was a defector from an older firm. One sentence.

**16. Citability: the sources are a run-on list with no anchoring.**
A student cannot tell which sentence rests on which source. Add inline markers or per-paragraph source notes drawn from `where_used`.

## Three changes that would most improve it

1. **Name the makers and re-aim the thesis.** Put Gorgiou (1990), Crown/Cripps (2013) and Murza (2020) on the page with the journalist who named each; change "The hands are still unnamed" to "The institutions do not name them. Journalists have, three times in thirty years, and the Opéra's ledgers hold names nobody has read." This meets outcome 2 and turns a slogan into a historical claim.
2. **Source the maker's-mark origin or hedge it, and fix the 42-hours sentence.** These are the two sentences a specialist will catch on first read; both can be corrected in an afternoon.
3. **Replace the chronology's sources.** Swap Wikipedia and *Pointe Magazine* for Hammond 1987–88, Guest, and *The Pointe Book* at first hand; add Gosselin and the 1821 Bias lithograph; move "contested" from Brugnoli's date to the question of priority; hedge Taglioni's shoes; caption the Chalon print as 1845.

## Is the thesis defensible?

As a claim about institutional record (posters, programmes, company pages), yes, and worth teaching. As stated on the page ("the hands are still unnamed"), no: it is sustained by withholding names the project holds, by an unsourced origin legend for the one naming device it praises, and by ignorance of the in-house workshops where most of the world's pointe shoes were made for a century. The defensible version is sharper: *the trade names its makers by mark and number; the institutions do not name them at all; the names exist, in journalists' notebooks and in the Opéra's ledgers, and nobody has collected them.* That is a history-of-making thesis. The current one is a caption.

## What to keep

- The construction paragraph ("Paper, paste and satin"): accurate, concrete, well sourced to a factory visit, and the quiz distractor (wood) is the right one.
- The rule that every count names its counter and date, and the label "the company's own numbers, told to donors and journalists". This is the page's best habit; apply it to the maker's mark and the strike figure too.
- The Freed output spread (150,000 to 330,000) shown as a spread. Correct and instructive.
- Outcome 3 ("tell a date a record supports from a figure a company gives about itself"): the right thing to teach; make the page obey it.
- "What this page could not show you": right instinct, wrong content (issue 5). Keep the section, rewrite the Moscow sentence, add the 1845 date to the print.
- Register structure (`alternates`, `rule`, `editor_note`): the apparatus is sound; it is the display strings that drop the hedges the records carry.
