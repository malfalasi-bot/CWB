# Inclusion and ethics review · "The shoe" (Ballet, 1 of 4) · round 1

Reviewer: inclusion and ethics seat (labour and gender in the performing arts; community-review protocols). Read: `build/units/ballet/the-shoe.md`, `dist/shot-1440.png`, `BRIEF.md`, `atlas/data/register/units/ballet.yaml`, `pass2_body.md` §4–5, `pass2_verify.md` §4; and, on the web, the Spitalfields Life 2013 factory essay, the Spitalfields Life series "The Pointe Shoe Makers of Hackney" (2013; republished 25 Jan 2018 and 5 Jun 2025), the Heritage Crafts Red List entry, and the AFP/Grishko coverage. 21 October 2026.

## Verdict

**3 / 5.** A page that teaches the right lesson about naming and then fails its own test: the man it holds up as unnamed has been named in print since 2013, the women on the factory floor are not in the page at all, and "the hands are still unnamed" is a sentence the course wrote while holding a list of twenty-six names. The ethic is good; the facts under it are wrong, and wrong in the direction of the erasure the page is about. Three fixes make it a 4.

## Issues, ranked

### HIGH

**H1. "Crown" is not unnamed. The page says he is.**
> "The maker known as Crown had made shoes for twenty-four years when a journalist visited the Hackney factory in 2013 and counted forty-one pairs a day at his bench. His name is not in the record. His mark is on thousands of feet."

What is wrong: the same publication the page cites ran a portrait series, "The Pointe Shoe Makers of Hackney" (Patricia Niven, photographs; Sarah Winman, interviews; first published 16 July 2013, republished 2018 and June 2025), in which the maker with the Crown mark is named as **Ray**, twenty-six years at Freed, born locally, forty pairs a day. The series also names Taksim ("Anchor", born in Cyprus, came to Britain thirty years before), Fred ("F"), Daniel ("Butterfly"), Alan ("Triangle") and Darren Plume (manager of the London makers). The research notes list the series URL (pass2_body §6.2, `?p=93791`) as "best available record of named makers" and never read it for names. The register record `bal-crown-years` says "the maker is named only by his mark", which is false. So the page takes a real person who sat for a portrait and told an interviewer, "I never expected someone writing down what I'm saying," and uses him as an emblem of anonymity. That is not the record's erasure; it is the course's, and the page sets it in the rhetorical centre.

Fix: name him. "The maker whose mark is a crown is Ray; he had made shoes for twenty-four years when a journalist first visited in 2013, and sat for a portrait that summer. His name reaches print because a photographer and a novelist went to Hackney. It reaches no company document." Correct the register (`bal-crown-years`, `bal-crown-pairs-day`, add a source for the series). If the course has a policy of not naming workers who did not consent to appear in it, say so on the page and apply it to everyone, Linnette Roe and Darcey Bussell included; do not apply it silently and only to the man at the bench.

**H2. The women are not in the page, and the mark system is gendered, and the page does not say so.**
> "so a returned shoe could be traced to the hand that made it"
> "The sixteen people a journalist named at the Hackney factory in 2013, from the hand laster to a finisher of forty years, appear in no company document."

What is wrong: in the 2013 record the marked "makers" are all men, on piecework, each with a symbol. The binding room, finishing and pairing are where the women are: Ozel Ahmed (supervisor, theatrical shoes, twenty years), Sheila Goodman (pointe-shoe finisher, forty years), Varsha Bahen (finisher), Rashimi Patel (pairing). No woman's hand has a mark. The mark credits one stage of the shoe and that stage is the men's; the women's stage is credited to nobody, in the factory and in this page. The page's only woman worker is "a finisher of forty years", unnamed, in the section about what could not be shown, although her name is in the source. Sheila Goodman and her husband Philip started on the same day forty years before the visit. The page withholds a name it has. The Spectator's 2019 headline, "the big burly blokes who make infinitely precise pointe shoes by hand", is the mythology; the page reproduces its shape (men make, women wear) without noticing it.

Fix: one paragraph in "The mark on the sole": who in the building gets a mark and who does not; the binding and finishing rooms; Ozel Ahmed and Sheila Goodman by name. Then the thesis sentence can be true: "The mark is the one place a maker's name reaches the stage, and it is a man's name; the woman who finishes the shoe leaves nothing on it."

**H3. "The hands are still unnamed" and "Moscow publishes its work and not its names" are false by the course's own research.**
> "Every one of those is a firm. The hands are still unnamed."
> "Nothing here comes from the workshops of Paris, Moscow or St Petersburg, which publish their work and not their names."

What is wrong: pass2_body §1.6 and §5.1 name Sergei Murza (craftsman), Olga Monakhova (sewing, twenty-seven years) and Irina Sobakina (deputy head of production) at Grishko, Moscow (AFP, 2020); Roman Kukushkin, former Bolshoi shoemaker; Yelena Zaitseva, head of the Bolshoi's costume division with 130 staff (TASS, 2016); Mark Suffolk, Leicester; Sophie Simpson, Freed fitter. The page has these and says the hands are unnamed. It is true that the firms do not name them; it is not true that nobody has. The distinction is the whole lesson of the unit (brief, "the makers almost never are [named]"; pass2_body §5.2, "the pattern is consistent enough to teach as a finding"), and the page blurs it into a lament. The Paris line is accurate (the Opéra names outside suppliers only, not employees); the Moscow line is not.

Fix: "Every one of those is a firm. The firms name their founders. A journalist in Moscow in 2020 wrote down three names at Grishko, Sergei Murza, Olga Monakhova, Irina Sobakina; Grishko's own pages give none." Rewrite the closing section to say what the record lacks, what the course has and did not use, and what rights withhold, as three different things (see M6).

**H4. A journalist's number is attributed to the company.**
> "The company's own pages put its output anywhere from 150,000 to 330,000 pairs a year."

What is wrong: 330,000 is the Spectator Australia (2019), not Freed; 150,000 is Spitalfields Life (2013), not Freed. The register (`bal-freed-output`) has the 333,000 filed under the Moscow Times source with a note "Spectator Australia 2019", which is a mis-keyed source. Freed's own figures are "over 250,000" and "nearly 250,000" (its pages) and the 2015 film's 48,000 + 112,000 = 160,000. Telling a learner that the company contradicts itself by a factor of two, when the spread is between four outside writers and the company, is unfair to the firm and teaches the wrong thing about sourcing on the page whose third learning objective is exactly this.

Fix: "Freed's own pages say about 250,000 pairs a year; its 2015 film says 160,000; journalists have printed 150,000 and 333,000. Nobody outside the firm has counted." Re-key the register source.

### MEDIUM

**M1. Gender is carried by the pronouns and never stated.**
> "sometimes slit by the dancer to bend where she wants. She stands on the platform"
> "The dancer sews her own ribbons and elastics"

Pointe is a gendered technology: the shoe is worn almost entirely by women, and the unpaid sewing, darning and breaking-in is therefore women's unwaged work laid on top of a wage. "She" is accurate, but as a silent default it reads as a style choice, and it hides the strongest labour point on the page. The 42-hours figure from Paris covers make-up and dressing, which also fall more heavily on women. Men now dance on pointe (Trockadero since 1974; contemporary work; NYCB's 2022 agreement moved to gender-neutral contract language, pass2_body §4.5), which is worth a clause. Fix: one sentence after "The factory supplies the shoe and nothing else": "Pointe is danced almost entirely by women, so this unpaid hour is women's." Keep "she"; explain it once.

**M2. The geography of making is a geography of head offices.**
> "Capezio's shop opposite the Metropolitan Opera from 1887, Grishko in Moscow from 1988"

The page maps the firms by founding city and the labour by one London factory. Where pointe shoes are made now is largely not in those cities: Grishko reports over 500 staff across Moscow, the Czech Republic and North Macedonia, with a plant at Demir Hisar that the Macedonian government announced as nearly 300 jobs; Bloch (Sydney, 1932) and the volume brands manufacture offshore. (Note to the chair: the "factory in Bosnia" in my brief is not in any source I can find; the Grishko plants are Czech and Macedonian.) "Two businesses in the whole country" and "Britain's list of endangered crafts" frame the craft as a national heritage in decline while the world's shoes are being made by people the page does not look for. The page admits the London bias but files it as a record failure, not a choice. Fix: one sentence: "Most of the world's pointe shoes are not made in these cities: Grishko's are made in Moscow, the Czech Republic and North Macedonia, by about five hundred people, none of them on its website." Page 3 (the company) should carry the wider map the brief promises; this page should at least point at it.

**M3. "Critically endangered" is left to imply the wrong cause.**
> "Heritage Crafts, which keeps Britain's list of endangered crafts, calls pointe-shoe making critically endangered: two businesses with two or more makers in the whole country."

Set directly after the unnamed makers and the disputed output figures, the listing reads as "nobody values this, so it is dying." The listing body itself says the opposite on demand: "plenty of customers and trade is buoyant"; the risks it names are materials sourcing, price, and the closure of independent dance shops after COVID. It is a UK listing (two UK businesses), not a statement about the craft worldwide. The reader should be told what "endangered" measures and whose failure it is (supply chain and retail, not the firms, not the record). Fix: "Heritage Crafts lists it as critically endangered in the UK: two businesses, twenty-one to fifty people, three apprentices. Not for lack of customers, the listing says, but of materials and shops."

**M4. The maker's pay is the one number the page could have asked for and did not.**
> "These are the companies' own numbers, told to donors and journalists, and nobody has audited them."

The page is good on the £50 and the $780,000 as fundraising and press figures. It then stops. Freed's makers are on piecework (Spitalfields Life 2013); forty-one pairs a day is a piece-rate day. The page gives the price of a pair, the budget of a company and the unpaid hours of a dancer, and says nothing about what the person at the bench is paid per pair, nor that no source gives it. That is the class politics of the £50 shoe: the appeal asks the public to buy consumables for a publicly subsidised company, with the dancer as the sympathetic face, and the maker's rate nowhere. Also, "nobody has audited them" is not quite fair: the Royal Ballet and Opera files audited accounts; what nobody has checked is the shoe line. Fix: "The makers are paid by the pair. What a pair pays them is not in any source this page could find." And "nobody outside the companies has checked them."

**M5. The body is absent from the page where the body meets the shoe.**
The page's own thesis is "the body, the craft and the money meet in it," then gives craft and money and defers the body to page 4. Deferral is a fair structural choice (the spine goes outward from the foot), but the shoe is where ankle and foot injuries, the commonest time-loss sites at the Royal Ballet (Mattiussi et al., BJSM 2021), happen, and the page lets "weightlessness, a creature of air" stand without a cost beside it. One sentence and a forward pointer would do: "Ankle and foot are the commonest injuries that keep a Royal Ballet dancer off stage; page 4 has the numbers." Without it the page romanticises what it set out to de-romanticise.

**M6. "What this page could not show you" mixes four kinds of absence and misreports two.**
It puts in one paragraph: an image withheld by rights (ROH); an image not yet fetched (Taglioni, a production state, not a gap in the record); names the institutions withhold (Paris); names the course had and chose not to print (the sixteen, the Grishko three). The CARE/Local Contexts habit is to say who holds what and why: the holder's restriction, the course's own choice, the record's silence. As written it lets the course's omissions borrow the dignity of the record's. Rewrite as three short lines: *What rights withhold* (ROH; link-out). *What the firms and houses do not publish* (Freed, the ROH, the Opéra: roles, no names). *What this page has and did not use* (the names from Hackney and Moscow; why, or print them). Drop the "being fetched" line from the published page; it belongs in the build log.

**M7. Dora Freed.**
> "Frederick Freed began the marks after he opened his shop in Cecil Court in 1929"

The register's own record (`bal-freed-founded`) says "Frederick Freed and his wife Dora open the shop"; Spitalfields Life has Dora as a milliner who worked in the factory into the 1970s. The page gives the shop to him alone. Fix: "after he and Dora Freed opened their shop".

### LOW

**L1. Fairness to the named: present tense and age of data.** "His mark is on thousands of feet" is present tense on a 2013 count; Ray may have retired. "Had made shoes for twenty-four years ... in 2013" is right; keep the past tense throughout for him. Likewise "a finisher of forty years" was forty years in 2013.

**L2. The learner is asked to "name three" makers, and the page names one, by a symbol.** Objective 2 is not met by the page as built (Crown by mark; Linnette Roe is a shoe-room supervisor, not a maker; the Italians and Gaynor Minden are firms or unnamed). H1–H3 fix this for free.

**L3. "in the whole country"** carries a nationalist note the listing does not. "In the UK" is enough.

**L4. The Paris dispute gives the union's figure and labels it; management's position (reorganise the day, finish earlier) is in the research and would cost half a sentence.** Not required, but it is the page's own standard.

**L5. The colophon** discloses AI drafting and says "no human has yet read this page"; good, keep, and add the two Spitalfields Life series posts to the sources once H1 is fixed.

## The three changes that would most improve it

1. **Name Ray, and name Sheila Goodman and Ozel Ahmed.** Correct "His name is not in the record" to what is true: his name is in a photographer's record and in no company's. Add the paragraph on who gets a mark (men, piecework) and who does not (the binding and finishing rooms, mostly women). This turns the page's emblem from an error into the unit's thesis in one object.
2. **Separate the firms' silence from the course's.** Rewrite "the hands are still unnamed" and the closing section so the reader can tell what the record lacks, what rights withhold, and what the course had and left out; print the Grishko names; move "being fetched" to the build log.
3. **Put one sentence each on gender, geography and pay.** Pointe is women's technique and the unpaid hour is women's; most of the world's pointe shoes are made in places this page does not visit; the makers are paid by the pair and no source gives the rate. Three sentences, and the £50 shoe and the donor appeal acquire their politics.

## What to keep

- The thesis and its placement: "The dancer is on the poster. The designer is in the programme. The maker is a symbol on a sole." Exactly right, and worth the page.
- The labelling discipline on money: "a figure it publishes to raise money for them"; "a union's figure in a dispute"; "the companies' own numbers, told to donors and journalists." This is the best practice in the unit and the third learning objective is well chosen.
- The ROH object: a pair signed by the dancer, catalogued with "maker: not given." Make the point explicit (the catalogue names the wearer and not the maker), and it is the page in miniature.
- Honesty about the pointe-origin dispute ("who did it earliest keeps moving") and about Gaynor Minden's 1993 as a launch, not a patent.
- The quiz: one question, placed where it corrects the one thing everyone believes (wood). Keep it.
- The existence of a "what this page could not show you" section. Fix its contents; keep the habit.
- The colophon's candour.
