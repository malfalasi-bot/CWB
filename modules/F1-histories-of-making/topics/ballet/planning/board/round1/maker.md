# Maker's review: "The shoe" (Ballet, as a history of making, 1 of 4)

Reviewer: theatrical shoemaker and costume maker, twenty years in a ballet company's wardrobe and shoe room, now teaching at a drama school. Read: `build/units/ballet/the-shoe.md`, `dist/shot-1440.png`, `research_notes/Ballet/pass2_body.md` sections 1, 2 and 5. I checked four claims against the sources the page cites (Spitalfields Life, May and July 2013; 4dancers 2012; Freed's own catalogue page) and against the bench.

## Verdict

**2 / 5. Not yet.** The tone is right, the money section is the most honest I have read in a teaching text, and the page is careful about who counted what. But the one paragraph of construction is thin and in one place wrong, the page's central anecdote ("His name is not in the record") is false on the evidence of the page's own source, and it promises the learner three makers' names and gives none. I would not hand this to an apprentice until the bench paragraph is rebuilt and the Crown sentence is fixed; after that it would be a 4.

## Ranked issues

### High

**H1. The sole press is not a pointe-shoe step.**
> "The leather sole goes on at about four thousand pounds of pressure, and a night in a warm room dries the paste hard."

The research note itself flags the 4,000 lb figure as "(Freed theatrical dept)". In the Spitalfields piece the press has a named operator, Ali Aksar, in the theatrical (character shoe) department, and the same piece says the Ballet Department "has no machinery": the makers work at individual benches with three tools, a hammer, long-nosed pliers and a broom handle. A pointe shoe's sole is pasted on and hammered down at the bench by the maker who built the block, then edge-stitched (those are the stitches you can see through the pleats on a Freed). The page has lifted a figure from the wrong room and attached it to the wrong shoe. Fix: cut the press from the pointe paragraph entirely, or move it to a sentence about the character shoes made in the next room, which would in fact be a good way to show the learner that "Freed" is two trades under one roof.

**H2. "His name is not in the record" is untrue, and the page's own source list disproves it.**
> "The maker known as Crown had made shoes for twenty-four years when a journalist visited the Hackney factory in 2013 and counted forty-one pairs a day at his bench. His name is not in the record. His mark is on thousands of feet."

Two months after the May 2013 factory visit, the same journalist published "The Pointe Shoe Makers of Hackney" (Spitalfields Life, 16 July 2013; listed in pass2 §6.2 as "best available record of named makers" but evidently not read for names). It gives Crown's name, Ray, with a portrait, "I have been here for twenty-six years", "I make forty pairs a day", "dancers come back and stay with you for life", "People wait weeks to get a shoe from me". The series runs to at least ten parts and names Taksim (Anchor, fifteen years, "I make Jane Taylor one hundred pairs of shoes a year"), Fred (F), Daniel (Butterfly, four months in, taught by Taksim), Alan (Triangle). It also records that it takes "two to three years to become a fully trained Pointe Shoe Maker", that dancers' specifications arrive at the bench on dockets, that the makers are on piecework, and that the quality controller, Darren Plume, checks every shoe each morning after the overnight bake. The journalist did not "count" forty-one pairs either; the maker told him. Fix: "Ray, who stamps a crown, had made shoes for twenty-four years when a journalist visited in 2013; he said he made forty-one pairs a day. Freed's own pages do not name him." That is a truer and sharper sentence, and the thesis survives it: the firm does not name him; a blogger with a camera did.

**H3. The page promises three makers' names and delivers none.**
> "explain why a ballet's makers are rarely named, and name three of them;"

The body names Frederick Freed (a founder), Linnette Roe (a shoe-room supervisor), Darcey Bussell (a dancer) and the designers of nothing. Not one shoemaker, cutter, laster, binder or finisher is named, though pass2 §1.6 lists sixteen from one visit and §5.1 adds Sophie Simpson (Freed fitter), Mark Suffolk (the second of Heritage Crafts' "two businesses", Leicester, founded 2000), Roman Kukushkin (ex-Bolshoi shoemaker) and three at Grishko. The page even says "two businesses with two or more makers in the whole country" without naming the second, which is a maker's own name over the door. Fix: a short named list on the page, by room: Jimmy Fenn, pattern cutter; Worral Thomas, hand laster; Ray, pointe-shoe maker, mark Crown; Sheila Goodman, pointe-shoe finisher, forty years; Sophie Simpson, fitter; Mark Suffolk, Leicester. Then the "could not show you" section can honestly say the company's own documents name none of them.

**H4. The construction is out of order and missing the parts a learner can actually see.**
> "The block is layers of paper and hessian, a coarse jute cloth, stuck with flour-and-water paste and covered in satin ... The shoe is made inside out on a wooden foot-shaped form called a last, then turned the right way round."

"Covered in satin" tells the learner the block is a lump that gets wrapped. It is the other way about: the satin upper with its cotton lining is pulled over the last inside out, and the block is built up inside that shell, between satin and lining, layer of hessian, paste, paper, paste, hammered to shape with the platform flattened square. The pleats, the one feature of a pointe shoe anyone can see from a metre away, are not mentioned at all: they are the margin of the upper gathered under the toe and hammered flat before the sole goes on. The insole is not mentioned (it is tacked to the last first; the three nails you can feel in the heel of a Freed are where it is fixed). The shank is described two paragraphs later as if it were a separate topic, when it goes in after turning. A bench order a learner can hold: last; insole tacked to the last; upper on inside out; pleat and hammer the toe; build the block; sole pasted and hammered on; off the last and turned; shank in; oven overnight; then to other hands, binding room for the drawstring casing, finishing, pairing, stamping. Write that as one paragraph and the quiz, the mark and the dancer's labour all land better.

### Medium

**M1. "A night in a warm room" is an oven.**
Both sources say ovens, baked overnight; 4dancers gives about 70 °C. On the floor this matters: an under-baked block is a shoe that dies in an act, and the quality controller's morning check exists because of it. Say oven, say the check.

**M2. "Wooden" last is unsupported.**
Nothing in pass2 says wooden; the caption says only "Shoe lasts numbered with sizes". Lasts in a working factory are as often plastic now. What a learner needs about the last is not its material but that there is one per size, width and style on a rack beside the bench, and that a "special" is built on a standard last with the maker altering block, vamp, wings, platform and heel to the docket. Cut the adjective or source it.

**M3. Sizes, widths, specials and fitting are absent.**
The page never says what a shoe comes in. Freed's own catalogue: UK 1 to 8½ in half sizes; widths medium, X, XX, XXX; special orders can specify the maker, length, width, block, platform, vamp, side and heel measurements, insole and drawstring, and Freed says two-thirds of its Classic output is made to a dancer's specification "accurate to within 3 mm". That is the fact that explains the whole mark system: a dancer is not buying a size, she is buying a maker's hands plus a docket. No fitter appears either, though one is named in the research and fitting is the first job any apprentice in a shoe room learns. One paragraph fixes it.

**M4. The shank sentence uses the wrong verb and no reason.**
> "sometimes slit by the dancer to bend where she wants"

Dancers score, cut down ("three-quarter"), or break a shank at the demi-pointe; nobody says slit. And the page never says why any of the dancer's work is done: the maker builds to a general shape, and the dancer bends the shank where her foot bends, crushes the wings so she can roll through the foot, darns or glues the platform for grip and to stop the satin shredding, scrapes the sole, sews ribbons at the angle her ankle needs. Thirty to forty-five minutes of sewing per pair, before breaking in. Without the why, "on her own time" is a labour-relations point without a craft behind it.

**M5. Why a shoe dies is not on the page.**
> "Nothing about the shoe is permanent. A pair lasts about ten to twenty hours of dancing by the usual estimate; a principal can finish a pair in one performance."

The numbers ring true, but the unit on the floor is pairs per week by rank, not hours, and a working dancer rotates several pairs at different stages of softness. The cause is simple and teachable: a flour-paste block is a dry biscuit, and sweat and stage heat soften it; the shank cracks where it has been bent a thousand times; the platform wears through. This is why dancers alternate pairs and dry them, why some shellac or glue a dying box, why a Gaynor Minden sells on "lasts longer", and why the insecticide in the paste exists at all. Two sentences.

**M6. Freed's output is misattributed.**
> "The company's own pages put its output anywhere from 150,000 to 330,000 pairs a year."

The 150,000 is Spitalfields Life, the 333,000 is the Spectator; neither is a company page. Freed's own catalogue page says "nearly 250,000 pairs of Classic Pointe Shoes" a year and its 2015 film gave 48,000 standard plus 112,000 custom. For a page whose third objective is telling a record from a company claim, this is a self-inflicted wound. Fix: "Freed says about 250,000; visitors have reported anything from 150,000 to 333,000."

**M7. The origin of the mark is dated without a record.**
> "Frederick Freed began the marks after he opened his shop in Cecil Court in 1929, so a returned shoe could be traced to the hand that made it."

The purpose is right (Ray says the same: symbols so faults can be traced). The attribution to Frederick Freed and the date are not in any source in pass2; Freed's history page does not describe the system. Say "Freed's makers have stamped a symbol on the sole for as long as anyone there remembers; the firm's own history does not date it."

**M8. "most theatres name no shoemaker, cutter or dyer at all" is true of the object and false of the building.**
From inside a company: the head of costume, the running wardrobe, the cutters, the dyer and the wig room are in the staff list at the back of every programme and on the company website. What is never done is to credit a shoe or a tutu to the hands that made it, and outside contractors (a Freed maker, the two men making tutus on an Essex farm) are never named by the house. The anonymity has reasons the page could give: the mark belongs to the firm, not the man, so a dancer who follows a maker who leaves is a commercial risk; the makers are on piecework and waiting lists, and some prefer it; and dancers themselves scratch the mark off the sole to keep a maker to themselves (Spitalfields, July 2013). The thesis should be: named by role in the staff list, never by object; outside hands never at all.

**M9. The quiz is answered two paragraphs above it.**
"Before you read on, choose one" arrives after "There is no wood in it. The block is layers of paper and hessian". Move the question above the "Paper, paste and satin" section, or change it to something the paragraph has not given away: which part the dancer stands on, or what the pleats are.

### Low

**L1.** "wood was never used" is a strong negative for a craft with a long undocumented past; "no maker on record builds a block from wood" is safe.

**L2.** "a moulded plastic shank arrived with one maker only ... most companies still dance in paste": Gaynor Minden is now in most big companies on some feet, and other firms have followed with synthetics. Companies do not dance in a shoe; dancers do, and they choose. "Most professional dancers still wear paste shoes" is defensible; the rest is not.

**L3.** "At Freed of London, which supplies the Royal Ballet": chiefly. Royal Ballet dancers wear what fits them.

**L4.** Bloch (Sydney, 1932) is in the research and missing from the firms list; Suffolk is the other British maker and missing too.

**L5.** The heritage sentence drops the hopeful half: the same listing counts three apprentices at Freed. A teacher wants that line; it is the difference between endangered and extinct.

**L6.** The screenshot: the page reads cleanly at 1440, but it is 3,250 pixels of text about a physical object with no object in it. Even a line drawing of a shoe cut in half would carry more than the quiz does.

## The three changes that would most improve it

1. **Rebuild the bench paragraph in working order** (H4, H1, M1): last, insole, upper inside out, pleats hammered, block built up between satin and lining, sole pasted and hammered, turned, shank in, oven overnight, checked, bound, finished, paired, stamped. Cut the press. Name the three tools. Say the maker makes forty pairs a day on piecework and works in batches, twenty on the rack at a time, which is the honest answer to "what does the maker do in an hour": about six shoes.

2. **Fix Crown and name the hands** (H2, H3): Ray, mark Crown, named in print in July 2013; a list of six named people by room; Mark Suffolk as the second business. Then the "could not show you" section becomes true: the firm's own pages name none of them.

3. **Add one paragraph on sizes, specials, fitting, and death** (M3, M4, M5): what a shoe comes in, what a docket specifies, what the fitter does, why the dancer alters it, and why water kills it. That paragraph is the one that lets a learner tell a good shoe from a bad one.

## Could a learner tell a well-made shoe from a bad one after reading this?

No. The page gives no criterion a hand or eye can use. Six that a first-year apprentice learns in a week: stand the shoe on its platform on a table, it should stand on its own, platform flat and square to the shank; pleats even, tight and lying flat, no gaps and no paste bleed on the satin; block symmetrical, wings matching, the same resistance under the thumb on both sides; sole centred with even stitching; shank flush inside with no ridge under the arch, nails seated at the heel; the pair matching each other (pairing is a job; Rashimi Patel's). Put those on the page and the learner has something to do with the next pointe shoe they pick up.

## What the page should show

- A shoe cut in half lengthways: satin, block layers, lining, insole and shank, sole, pleats. The single most useful image in this unit; a drawing will do.
- The underside of a new Freed: pleats, edge stitching, the stamped mark, the heel nails. This is the "mark on the sole" the page talks about and never shows.
- A maker's bench: the rack of numbered lasts, the paste pot, hammer, pliers, broom handle, twenty shoes in progress. Spitalfields Life's Patricia Niven portraits (2013, 2019 and the ten-part series) are the best record and can be linked if not reproduced.
- The oven racks and the morning check.
- A docket with a dancer's specification beside the shoe made to it.
- A prepared pair next to an unprepared one: ribbons on, platform darned, shank bent, satin cut back. The RBO film "How Royal Ballet dancers prepare their pointe shoes" shows exactly this.
- A dead shoe beside a new one.
- Films: Freed's 2015 introduction film; the 2021 Business Insider factory film; the RBO preparation film.

## What to keep

- The opening: "the body, the craft and the money meet in it." That is the right thesis for a making course and I have never seen it put so plainly.
- "What a company pays, by its own account": the refusal to launder fundraising numbers into facts, and the sentence "no other part of ballet's economy is counted in public at all". Keep every word.
- "The factory supplies the shoe and nothing else." True, and the hinge of the dancer-labour argument.
- The purpose of the mark: fault-tracing first, ordering by maker second. Right.
- The Paris Opéra figure handled as "a union's figure in a dispute". Right.
- "The idea came before the technology": Taglioni in darned slippers, the Italian box fifty years later. Right, and the half-century gap is the thing a learner should remember.
- The weekly paste. Add the insecticide; it is the one detail that makes a learner understand this is a flour shoe.
- "What this page could not show you." Keep the section; make its claims true (see H2, H3) and it becomes the most honest paragraph in the unit.
