# Repertory benchmark: telling an art form's history through its works

X3 pass, 21 November 2026. Benchmark for the whole-history repertory and timeline that will sit beside the thirteen-chapter ballet unit (`../BLUEPRINT.md`). Rules from `../../research/repertory/BRIEF_REPERTORY.md`: claims marked **documented**, **probable**, **contested** or **argued**; URLs only where a search or fetch returned them.

**Recommendation in one paragraph.** Build **"The Repertory"** as an Atlas layer, not a second unit: one whole-history timeline (ten claim-named eras, six lanes: works, who paid, bodies and training, stage and design, music and media, the world outside, with a debates strip above) that opens every chapter at its era; 148 work cards at tiered depth with fixed heads drawn from the dossiers; production records on the ROH model (work, production, performance); era cards, thread walls, argument cards, myth cards and design cards; and seven short guided routes over the same cards, starting with a 45-minute tour of the line for every learner and including an optional fifty-work chronological route. The evidence behind it: a frame taught early and used everywhere (de Groot-Reuvekamp 2017; Shemilt; Howson and Shemilt 2011), parallel timelines (Korallo and others 2012), comparison across cases (Gentner and others 2003; Alfieri and others 2013), multiple sources and perspectives (Nokes and others 2007; Reisman 2012; Wansink and others 2018), narrative over exposition (Mar and others 2021) and the seductive-details warning (Harp and Mayer 1998; Sundararajan and Adesope 2020). Details in §5.3.

Sources examined this pass: 11 books and references (§1), 6 exhibitions and holders (§2), 11 platforms and tools (§3), about 30 studies (§4). Studies marked probable were not re-checked at source in this pass.

### 1.1 Kirstein, *Movement and Metaphor* (1970)

**Structure.** Two parts. Part 1 discusses the five elements "proper to the spectacle" of theatrical dance: choreography, music, mime, costume, decor. Part 2, the longer part, analyses "in a structured outline fifty ballets selected from the four hundred years" (Kirkus 1970) [documented: https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/a/lincoln-kirstein-2/movement-and-metaphor-four-centuries-of-ballet]. Each work is discussed under the same four heads, "priority, precedent, politics and plot or pretext" (Kirkus 1970) [documented]. The four successions that organise the whole are *ballet de cour*, *ballet d'action*, *ballet romantique* and *ballet russe* [documented, Kirkus]. The Dover reissue is titled *Four Centuries of Ballet: Fifty Masterworks* (1984), and the Balanchine Foundation's bibliography notes chapters on *Apollon Musagète*, *Orpheus* and *Agon* [documented: https://balanchine.org/balanchine-catalogue/media]. It was edited at Praeger by Nancy Reynolds, a former NYCB dancer, who persuaded Kirstein to publish a manuscript he had kept unpublished [documented: Yale UP blog 2023, https://yalebooks.yale.edu/2023/02/10/why-dance-matters-ii-balanchine-first-thoughts-retrieved; NYPL oral history *MGZMT 3-3332].

**What works.** The **fixed heads per work** are the single most transferable idea in this benchmark. "Priority" (what it did earliest), "precedent" (what it took from), "politics" (who paid and why) and "plot or pretext" (the premise) are four questions a learner can ask of any work, so fifty entries teach one method fifty times. The thematic Part 1 gives the vocabulary before the entries use it. A later reference judges it an "excellent chronological introduction with concise information about specific choreographic works, including the music" (Appreciating Ballet's Music bibliography) [documented: https://appreciatingballetsmusic.com/annotated-bibliography].

**What fails.** It is "a brilliant if idiosyncratic analysis" (unsigned biography of Kirstein reprinted on Encyclopedia.com) [documented: https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/literature-and-arts/dance-biographies/lincoln-kirstein]: fifty works chosen by one partisan who ran NYCB, ending in his own company's repertory, so the canon is the author's and the line runs to Balanchine. The canon is Western. The emphasis is "the syntax and structure of choreography" (Kirkus), so designers, makers and audiences appear as context, not as heads. The author claimed it was "not for the general reader" [documented, Kirkus]. **Lesson [argued]:** borrow the fixed heads and the fifty-work scale; replace the single author's canon with a declared selection rule and the register's counter.

### 1.2 Balanchine and Mason, *Complete Stories of the Great Ballets*

**Structure.** The 1977 revised edition (xxix + 902 pp.) has nine parts: (1) "Stories of the great ballets", alphabetical from *Abyss* to *Witch Boy*; (2) "How to enjoy ballet"; (3) "A brief history of the ballet"; (4) "Chronology of significant events in the history of ballet"; (5) "How I became a dancer and choreographer"; (6) "Ballet for your children"; (7) "Careers in ballet"; (8) notes on dancers and choreography; (9) glossary [documented: IUCAT record 13539889, https://iucat.iu.edu/iub/13539889]. *101 Stories* (1975) was an intermediate edition that "adds fifty-one ballets choreographed between 1968 and 1974" [documented: Balanchine Foundation bibliography]. The count of about 400 works is commonly given but not seen at source [probable; see `CENSUS.md` §1.1].

**What works.** The alphabetical spine is a **lookup tool for the audience on the night**: you hold a programme and find the story. Each entry is a scene-by-scene narrative, which is what a first-time viewer needs. Putting the chronology in a separate part is honest about what the book is.

**What fails.** Alphabetical order destroys lineage: *La Bayadère* sits beside *Le Beau Danube*. Entries are synopses, not readings; no disputes, little design, no sources. The selection leans to the repertory of the author's company and its time (for example *Bartók No. 3*, *Biosfera*) [argued, from the contents list]. **Lesson [argued]:** a reference spine (A to Z) and a teaching spine (time) are different instruments; the repertory needs both, as two views of one set of records.

### 1.3 Beaumont, *Complete Book of Ballets* (1937) and supplements

**Structure.** Arranged chronologically **by choreographer**, starting with Dauberval: a short biography, then each of his ballets with a credit block ("Pantomime Ballet in 2 Acts and 3 Scenes. Book: Dauberval. Choreography: Dauberval. First produced: Bordeaux, 1786"), a list of characters and a detailed scene-by-scene synopsis [documented: archive.org scan of the 1938 Grosset & Dunlap edition, https://ia902907.us.archive.org/16/items/in.ernet.dli.2015.234186/2015.234186.Complete-Book_text.pdf]. The three volumes cover "nearly three hundred ballets dating from 1786 to 1954" [documented: Cecchetti International, https://cicb.org/cyril-beaumont]. The 1942 Supplement carries a "Corrigenda" keyed to both the London and American editions [documented: SL NSW record].

**What works.** The **credit block before the story** (form, acts and scenes, book, choreography, first production) is the ancestor of the brief's dossier header. Grouping by maker shows a choreographer's development, and the order still runs through time. Mary Clarke and Clement Crisp's working rule was "Look it up in Beaumont" [documented: cicb.org]. Beaumont's single-work monographs (*The Ballet Called Giselle*, 1944; *The Ballet Called Swan Lake*, 1952) are the deep dives beside the guide [probable: dates from working knowledge].

**What fails.** The choreographer-as-author spine hides the librettist, the composer and the designer, the very credit problem the blueprint's chapter 0 opens on. Women choreographers and non-European works barely appear. The corrigenda show the cost of a printed reference: errors persist across editions. **Lesson [argued]:** keep the credit block; do not make the choreographer the only author of a card.

### 1.4 Homans, *Apollo's Angels* (2010)

Already examined in `../../research/pass2_presentation.md` §2 item 15: twelve chapters by nation-state (France, Denmark, Italy, Russia, the Soviet Union, Britain, America twice) and an epilogue, "The masters are dead and gone", that argues the art is dying [documented there]. **For the repertory:** Homans shows the strength of a single **argued narrative**, and the risk: works appear as evidence for the argument, so a learner cannot enter by a work. Her epilogue is itself a dispute (critics contested it on publication [probable]) and belongs on an argument card, not in the spine [argued].

### 1.5 Au, *Ballet and Modern Dance* (1988; 3rd ed. 2012)

**Structure.** Twelve chronological chapters whose titles name the period's theme: "A Most Obedient Servant"; "The Rise of Professionalism"; "The Development of the Ballet d'Action"; "Ascent and Descent"; "Crystallization and Ferment in Russia"; "First Steps towards a New Form"; "'Astonish Me'"; "Truly Modern"; "The Decentralization of Ballet"; "The Metamorphosis of Growth"; "A Time of Growth"; "Moving into the New Millennium" (revised by James Rutter) [documented: https://www.thamesandhudson.com/products/ballet-and-modern-dance-world-of-art]. 232 pages, 149 illustrations.

**What works.** **Claim-headed eras.** The chapter title says what changed ("The Decentralization of Ballet"), which is exactly the voice guide's rule that headings say the claim. Ballet and modern dance in one line of time shows ballet defined against its neighbour (blueprint lesson 13). Small format and many images make it the most-assigned short survey (probable).

**What fails.** It is a survey of names in compressed paragraphs; no work is taken apart, and works recur only as mentions. The frame is "western dance" (publisher's own description). **Lesson [argued]:** name the timeline's eras by the change they carry, as Au does; do not use the eras as containers for name lists.

### 1.6 Craine and Mackrell, *Oxford Dictionary of Dance* (2000; 2nd ed. 2010)

**Structure.** Over 2,600 A to Z entries on "technical terms, steps, styles, works and countries, in addition to many biographies"; the second edition added 150 entries, 75 of them on individual works and forms, "greater emphasis on international coverage" (China, Eire), and about 100 web links on a companion site [documented: OUP, https://global.oup.com/academic/product/the-oxford-dictionary-of-dance-9780199563449]. Its predecessor, Koegler's *Concise Oxford Dictionary of Ballet* (1977; 2nd ed. 1982), had over 5,000 entries [documented in `CENSUS.md` §1.1].

**What works.** One entry per work with premiere data and a short reading, cross-referenced to people and terms: this is the **index row and short card** of the census (§3.2–3.3). The online version makes it a node set rather than a book.

**What fails.** No path; no comparison; a work's entry cannot tell you what it is like. **Lesson [argued]:** the repertory's Line and Label tiers (16 and 50–60 words) should do what an Oxford entry does, and every card should carry cross-links (people, terms, threads) so the dictionary becomes a network.

### 1.7 Dance Heritage Coalition, *America's Irreplaceable Dance Treasures* (2000)

**Structure.** In autumn 1999 the coalition (founded 1992; office in the Library of Congress) invited nominations "from across the full range of America's dance forms and traditions -- past as well as present and indigenous as well as immigrant" [documented: LoC press release 00-099, 28 July 2000, http://www.loc.gov/today/pr/2000/00-099.html]. More than 900 nominations were "vetted through a three-stage process" by expert committees [documented: https://danceheritage.org/treasures.html]. Five criteria: significant impact on dance as an art form; artistic excellence; enriched the nation's cultural heritage; potential to enhance the lives of future generations; worthy of national and international recognition [documented, both]. The unit is mixed: people (Astaire, Balasaraswati), companies, schools and venues (Dance Theatre of Harlem, Bennington, Jacob's Pillow), dances (the Charleston, the hula) and one work (*The Nutcracker*) [documented, LoC list and DHC essays list]. Formats: an illustrated booklet (2000); a touring exhibition of "fifty still images and a similar number of moving images" on a kiosk (2003 to 2009: NYPL, Jacob's Pillow, San Francisco, Columbus, Hofstra) [documented: danceheritage.org/publications.html; Hofstra]; an online exhibition (2012) of commissioned essays with bibliographies and research resources [documented: artsmeme, 28 Aug 2012]. Twelve treasures added in 2012, thirteen in 2015 [documented: Dance Magazine 2012, 2016].

**What works.** **Published criteria and a counted process** (900 nominated, three stages, 100 chosen): the selection is open to challenge, which is the constitution's "counts with their counter". Mixing works, people, places and dance forms admits social and vernacular dance that a works-only list excludes. Each treasure has a signed essay with a bibliography, so the list is a gateway to scholarship.

**What fails.** "Irreplaceable" and "treasure" are a preservation advocate's words, not a historian's; the list is national by design; it was meant to be "issued every two years" (LoC 2000) and was not extended for twelve years [documented]. Alphabetical order gives no story. **Lesson [argued]:** publish the repertory's inclusion rule, the size of the pool it was drawn from and who chose; let the unit of entry include a few non-works (a venue, a dance form) where the history needs them.

### 1.8 Kobbé, *The Complete Opera Book* (1919) and Harewood's revision (1954)

**Structure.** Kobbé died in 1918 before finishing; the 1919 first edition was titled *The Stories of the Operas, together with 400 of the Leading Airs and Motives in Musical Notation* [documented: Project Gutenberg 40540]. Harewood's 1954 revision kept "Kobbé's original scheme... the composers are grouped under nationality and century so that some sort of rough historical continuity is preserved", in three parts (Before 1800; the nineteenth century; the twentieth century), with premiere and performance details by Harold Rosenthal at the head of each opera [documented: preface, archive.org scan]. Harewood's rule on length: synopsis lengths follow "the complication of plot or musical structure" and "should not be taken as an indication" of value, so *Wozzeck* gets nearly twice *Norma*'s space [documented, same]. Authorship marked "K" and "H" per section [documented: Wikipedia, *The Complete Opera Book*].

**What works.** **Music examples inside the synopsis** tie story to score at the moment it matters (the ballet equivalent: a step, a pose or a design image at the moment of the story). The production data block at the head. The initials that show which editor wrote what (apparatus that admits layered authorship). The explicit note that space is not merit.

**What fails.** Grouping by composer and nation makes the composer the author and the nation the frame; cross-national influence and staging history are invisible. **Lesson [argued]:** state, as Harewood did, that the depth of a card follows the evidence and complexity, not rank; sign every card.

### 1.9 Gombrich, *The Story of Art* (1950)

**Structure.** Twenty-eight chapters from prehistory to the present, each a period and place with a claim in its title ("The great awakening", "Permanent revolution", "A story without end") [documented: Wikipedia; 1951 Phaidon scan]. Gombrich's first self-imposed rule: "I would not write about works I could not show in the illustrations", so that the text would not "degenerate into lists of names" [documented: preface]. His third rule: "resist any temptation to be original in my selection", because familiar masterpieces "may serve as welcome landmarks" for newcomers [documented: preface]. A reviewer noted that "Gombrich takes pains to make comparisons with earlier work", which "helps to give the reader a sense of the continuity" [documented: Narotzky, Project MUSE, https://muse.jhu.edu/article/597877/summary]. Over eight million copies, 30+ languages [documented: Wikipedia].

**What works.** **No work without its image**; **canonical landmarks first** for a newcomer; **every work compared with a predecessor**. These are, in order, the Atlas's evidence rule, the repertory's Tier A, and the comparison-after-example finding (Gentner and others 2003).

**What fails.** "Consistently Eurocentric" (Guardian review, 2006), with Asia reduced to one "Looking Eastwards" chapter [documented: https://www.theguardian.com/books/2006/dec/02/art]. A Korean study of art-history teaching through the book (Ahn 2017, *미술교육논총* 31(2)) finds bias in the selection of plates and over-emphasis on the artist's intention, and proposes presenting one work with interpretations from different perspectives side by side [documented: KCI abstract]. **Lesson [argued]:** keep Gombrich's three rules; answer his failure with the alternative-readings card on every work.

### 1.10 Gardner, *Art through the Ages* (1926; 16th ed.)

**Structure.** Chronological chapters by period and region, now in Western and non-Western volumes. The editor claims to "never [abandon] the traditional list of 'great' works or the very notion of a 'canon'" while giving "patronage, function, and context" space in every chapter [documented: Kleiner preface, concise ed., https://art.pte.hu/sites/art.pte.hu/files/files/menuk/dokument/KI/muvtortenet/gardners-art-through-the-ages_reduce.pdf]. Each chapter ends with **"The Big Picture"**, a bullet summary with small images of the characteristic works. A poster-size **Global Timeline** has "four major horizontal bands corresponding to Europe, the Americas, Asia, and Africa, and 34 vertical columns for the successive chronological periods" with key monuments illustrated [documented: same preface]. Boxed essays fall into five recurring types (for example "Architectural Basics") [documented].

**What works.** **The timeline as a separate, whole-history artefact** with regional lanes, beside chapters that go deep; recurring box types that a reader learns to find (Materials and Techniques, Written Sources, The Patron's Voice and so on, probable from other editions); an end-of-chapter big picture.

**What fails.** The size (c. 1,600 images) and the textbook voice; the regional bands make "Europe" one lane among four while it holds most of the pages [argued]. **Lesson [argued]:** a whole-history timeline with **regional lanes** and **period columns** is the established textbook device for showing simultaneity; the ballet repertory should copy the device and fix its weakness by making the lanes about the work (who paid, what was staged where) and not only geography.

### 1.11 The *1001 Before You Die* format

**Structure.** One work per entry (a page or half-page), "roughly chronological" by decade, a short essay by one of many critics, a fact strip (for albums: running time, producer, key tracks), and a large image [documented: Wikipedia, *1001 Albums You Must Hear Before You Die*; contents "1950s... 2000s", archive.org]. *1001 Albums* has "90 internationally acclaimed music journalists" and "more than 900 iconic images" [documented: Hachette]. The series runs to paintings, buildings, books, films, video games and more [documented: Hachette series list].

**What works.** A large, browsable, **equal-sized** unit of entry; dates as the only spine, so any page is an entry point; many contributors give range.

**What fails.** "The whole premise of the book is humbug" (Matt Price, *The Australian*, 2005) [documented: Wikipedia]: a list that cannot be finished, a must that is a marketing claim; reviewers of *1001 Video Games* found series sequels repeated and handhelds "under-represented" [documented]; *Albums* is mostly "rock and pop albums from the Anglosphere" [documented]. Every entry is the same size whatever its weight, so the format flattens. **Lesson [argued]:** the browsable equal card is right for the Line and Label tiers of 148 works; the "must" framing and an unexplained selection are wrong.

### 1.12 Two further references that bear on the design

- **MacGregor, *A History of the World in 100 Objects*** (BBC Radio 4 and British Museum, 2010): one object per 15-minute episode, in date order, grouped into twenty themed weeks of five [probable: from working knowledge; not re-checked this pass]. It is the clearest precedent for a whole history told through single items in strict time order **with themed clusters**, and for a fixed per-item length.
- **Single-work monographs as deep dives** (Beaumont's *The Ballet Called Giselle* and *Swan Lake*; Wiley's *Tchaikovsky's Ballets*, 1985; Garafola and others on single works) [probable]: references pair a guide (many works, short) with monographs (one work, long), which is exactly the tier structure of Line, Label, Story and Deep.

### 1.13 What the books teach, together [argued]

1. **Three spines recur**: alphabetical (Balanchine–Mason, Oxford, DHC) for lookup; by maker (Beaumont, Kobbé) for lineage of authors; by time with claim-named eras (Kirstein, Au, Gombrich, Gardner, 1001) for teaching. A digital repertory can offer all three as views of one record set; a book must choose.
2. **The best per-work entries use fixed heads** (Kirstein's four Ps; Beaumont's credit block; Kobbé's data head and music examples). The brief's thirteen dossier headings are the same idea at greater depth.
3. **Every canon book is criticised for its selection** (Kirstein's partisanship, Gombrich's Eurocentrism, 1001's Anglosphere). Only the DHC published its criteria and its pool. The repertory should do so.
4. **The strongest histories pair a narrative with a reference**: Gardner's chapters with its Global Timeline; Balanchine–Mason's stories with its chronology part; Beaumont's guide with his monographs. No book in this list does comparisons across works as its main unit; the comparison wall is the gap the course can fill.

### 2.1 V&A, *Diaghilev and the Golden Age of the Ballets Russes* (2010)

**Structure.** More than 300 objects from the V&A and lenders [documented: V&A press release, https://media.vam.ac.uk/media/documents/legacy_documents/press_release/68662_press_release.pdf]. Gallery 1 begins with Diaghilev's life in St Petersburg and "an overview of the dance scene he was set to transform", runs through the Paris seasons to 1914, and ends on "a group of nine costumes designed by Nicolas Roerich" for *The Rite of Spring* [documented: same]. The middle gallery had, in the curator's account, "a strong feeling of backstage both in the decoration (including working period lights)", with sections on music and set design, Goncharova's *Firebird* and Picasso, and "a short film explaining how the V&A acquired much of its collection including footage of the 1968 Sotheby's auction" [documented: Pritchard, in a Venice conference volume, https://iris.unive.it/retrieve/handle/10278/28715/25324/Djagilev%20pp.%20201-296.pdf]. "The third gallery returned to a more chronological approach" with the 1920s works, and "only a relatively small area was given over to the legacy", on fashion and dance [documented: same]. Goncharova's *Firebird* backcloth was shown in "a gigantic room with the huge projection" of an ENB dancer (Begoña Cao) dancing to the score (Abitare review, 2010) [documented: https://www.abitare.it/en/design-en/visual-design-en/2010/11/03/diaghilev-at-victorian-albert-2]. The installation was designed with Tim Hatley, partly on a turntable, "to bring the theatricality and excitement of living artists into the exhibition" (Arts Fuse, 2013) [documented: https://artsfuse.org/84217/fuse-visual-arts-review-seeing-the-wonders-of-diaghilev-and-the-ballets-russes]. A printed **London walk** of 32 stops (theatres, hotels, costumiers, Beaumont's bookshop) took the exhibition into the street [documented: V&A walking tour PDF]. Pritchard notes that "45% of all performances by Diaghilev's company were presented in London" [documented: Pritchard].

**What works.** **The ballet as the cluster** (the *Rite* costumes together; *Firebird* design, cloth and danced projection together), inside a **chronological frame**, so the visitor sees both a work and a sequence. **Scale**: the full-size cloth shows what no catalogue can. **Provenance shown** (the 1968 auction film) as part of the story: how the record survived is itself content. **The walk** puts the history into places a visitor can stand in.

**What fails.** The LRB's Peter Campbell observed that costumes and cloths, "closer to the performances", are "diminished by the absence of the environment they were created for" [documented: https://www.lrb.co.uk/the-paper/v32/n21/peter-campbell/at-the-v-a]. A Londonist review found it relied "too heavily on educational videos, simplistic explanations" and "conventional talking head spots" [documented: https://londonist.com/2010/11/exhibition-review-diahilev-va]. Legacy and the makers of the costumes got little room. **Lesson [argued]:** cluster by work within a time spine; show the record's own survival; give motion its own medium (film) at the point where the object cannot carry it.

### 2.2 NGA Washington, *When Art Danced with Music* (2013)

**Structure.** Adapted from the V&A show, with "some 80 works from the V&A's renowned collection" and about 50 objects from more than 20 lenders, "more than 130" objects in all, "organized chronologically and divided into five sections": The First Seasons (1909–1912); Vaslav Nijinsky–Dancer and Choreographer; a section on the Russian avant-garde designers Goncharova and Larionov (probable title; the press release names their work and the *Firebird* backdrop); The International Avant-Garde; Modernism, Neoclassicism, and Surrealism [documented: NGA exhibition page, https://www.nga.gov/exhibitions/diaghilev-and-ballets-russes-1909-1929-when-art-danced-music; NGA press release 11 Jan 2013]. The free brochure has five essays: Serge Diaghilev; Transformation of Ballet; Revolution in Music; When Art Danced with Music; Legacy [documented: NGA brochure PDF]. Goncharova's 1926 *Firebird* backdrop (51.5 × 33.5 ft) and the *Train Bleu* front curtain, "designed by Pablo Picasso and painted by Prince Alexander Schervashidze", were the largest objects ever shown inside the gallery [documented: press release].

**What works.** The **five-section chronology with a person (Nijinsky) as one section** shows that a time spine can hold a section about a maker without breaking. The press release credits the scene painter beside Picasso, which is the credit line the blueprint asks for (chapter 7). The brochure's essay split (person, dance, music, art, legacy) is a compact model of the "lanes" a whole-history timeline needs.

**What fails.** An art museum's version shifted weight to the visual artists: one reviewer found it "a more conventional museum approach, lacking the dynamism" of London, costumes standing "in static grandeur", and noted that "many of the artisans were females who embellished the fabrics", a labour the labels did not foreground [documented: Arts Fuse 2013]. **Lesson [argued]:** the same objects tell a different history under a different institution; the repertory should show which lane a work's evidence comes from (art museum, theatre archive, company) because the holder shapes the story.

### 2.3 Musée d'Orsay, *Degas à l'Opéra* (2019–2020)

**Structure.** Organised by Orsay and the Orangerie with the NGA, with the BnF, for the Opéra's 350th anniversary [documented: Orsay press kit, https://www.musee-orsay.fr/sites/default/files/exposition/booklet/2021-07/DP_degas_a_l_opera.pdf]. The parcours is thematic, with sections such as "Génétique des mouvements", "Salle, scène, coulisses" and "L'Opéra, laboratoire technique" [documented: https://www.musee-orsay.fr/fr/programme/agenda/expositions/presentation/degas-lopera]. The press kit has a chronology and a work list as separate parts [documented]. The curators' framing: Degas's Opéra "n'est-il jamais exact"; his dance classes and examinations are "des fantasmagories" made in the studio [documented: Orsay page]. Degas himself: "J'en ai tant fait de ces examens de danse, sans les avoir vus, que j'en suis un peu honteux" [documented: press release].

**What works.** **Rooms named for spaces of the house** (auditorium, stage, wings) are a ready-made lane system for the course's chapter 6 (the night and the house). The **source critique on the wall**: a famous image of ballet (Degas's classes) is shown to be invented, which is exactly the "myths against the evidence" head of the dossier.

**What fails.** For ballet history the unit is the painter, not the work; ballets appear only as subjects (*La Source*, through the portrait of Eugénie Fiocre). **Lesson [argued]:** an image card for a work should say whether the image is a record (a photograph of the production), a design (made before) or an artist's invention (made after), because learners take Degas for a record.

### 2.4 BnF Bibliothèque-musée de l'Opéra and the Paris Opéra's online offer

**Structure.** The library and archives were founded in 1866, the museum opened in 1881, both passed to the Bibliothèque nationale in 1935 and to its music department in 1942; the reading room is the salon of the Palais Garnier, temporary exhibitions are in the lower rotunda, the permanent display in the former smoking room [documented: https://www.bnf.fr/en/opera]. The collection holds some 2,500 set maquettes, 3,000 objects (500 paintings) and 3,000 stage jewels [documented: https://www.bnf.fr/fr/galerie-de-la-bibliotheque-musee-de-lopera], and the Ballets Russes designs (Bakst, Benois, Goncharova) and the Kochno fonds [documented: BnF *Rouge* repères page]. Exhibitions mount by genre or building and then theme: *Le Grand opéra, le spectacle de l'Histoire* (2019–20) and *Le Palais Garnier: 150 ans d'un théâtre mythique* (2025–26), the latter with sections such as "Le Palais national" and "Le Palais de la danse" [documented: BnF agenda pages]. The Opéra's own online offer is **POP**, a subscription streaming platform with ballets such as Nureyev's *La Belle au bois dormant* (2 h 44) and MacMillan's *L'Histoire de Manon* [documented: https://www.operadeparis.fr/, 15 Nov 2026]. Gallica holds digitised Opéra material (probable; not examined this pass).

**What works.** An archive inside the building it records: the house, its repertory and its documents in one place. Exhibitions that take the building as the subject (Garnier at 150) show the house as a world (blueprint lesson 8). Streaming gives the work in motion.

**What fails.** The public offer is split three ways (BnF exhibitions, Gallica scans, the Opéra's paid streaming) with no shared work record linking a ballet's designs to its film. Streaming is paid and link-only for the course. **Lesson [argued]:** the repertory's work card is the place where these three kinds of evidence meet; that join is a service no holder provides.

### 2.5 Bakhrushin State Central Theatre Museum, Moscow

**Structure.** The permanent exhibition opens in the founder's study (Aleksei Bakhrushin's desk, the guest album signed by visiting celebrities), then a room on Fyodor Volkov and eighteenth-century theatre, then music theatre, where ballet is shown through portraits of ballerinas (Istomina, Taglioni, Karsavina, Pavlova) and *World of Art* designs [documented: https://www.bakhrushinmuseum.ru/main-exposition/]. The eighteenth-century exhibition "presents the history of theatre in Russia by reigns" and is called "Theatre in Russia", not "Russian theatre", because foreign troupes, ballet masters and designers shaped it [documented: https://www.bakhrushinmuseum.ru/teatr-v-rossii-xviii-veka]. The playbills and programmes department holds about 600,000 items, over 300,000 of them from the pre-revolutionary provinces "from Arkhangelsk to Tiflis" [documented: https://www.bakhrushinmuseum.ru/en/collection/playbills-and-programmes-department]. The online catalogue sorts objects by fund and also under rubrics "Theatres", "Productions", "Persons" and "Authors", with curator albums and virtual exhibitions [documented: https://collectiononline.gctm.ru/about]. Its touring shows are built on single choreographers (*Bolshoi Ballet of Yuri Grigorovich*, Rome 2026, with a section on the designer Simon Virsaladze) [documented: museum news, 19 June 2026].

**What works.** **"Productions" as a first-class rubric** in an object catalogue; a named argument in an exhibition title ("in Russia", not "Russian"); the playbill as the densest record of what was staged where.

**What fails.** Ballet is a sub-room of music theatre in the permanent display; the exhibitions serve a state-directed programme of "cultural diplomacy" (the museum's own phrase) [documented: news, 9 Dec 2025], so the 2024 Berdyansk show framed occupied-territory institutions as "new regions" [documented: news, 20 Mar 2024]. **Lesson [argued]:** a holder's framing is part of the evidence; cite Bakhrushin objects with their holder and date and do not adopt its captions.

### 2.6 Royal Ballet and Opera Collections, performance database

**Structure.** The database aims "to catalogue every performance at the Royal Opera House from 1732 to the present" and is complete for works and productions on the main stage from 1946 to August 2019 and for all main-stage performances from September 1999 [documented: https://rohcollections.org.uk/performances.aspx]. **Records are in three levels**: *work* ("the elements that never change about a work: creators, genre, premiere, and literary source"); *production* ("the team that put the work on stage: producer, director, set and costume designers, lighting designer"), named by the year of its premiere, for example *The Sleeping Beauty (1946)*; and *performance* (the people on stage or in the pit on a given day) [documented: same]. Records "mirror the information found in the theatre's programmes", and each performance names its information source (programme, stage-management report) and timings (for *Les Sirènes*, 12 Nov 1946: "Entire work: 7.41-8.19pm") [documented: performance record 3494]. The archive holds "details of over 13,000 performances" [documented: https://www.rbo.org.uk/about/royal-ballet-and-opera-archive-and-collections].

**What works.** **Work, production, performance** is the right data model for a stage art, and the one the repertory's register should adopt: Ek's *Giselle* is a work; the Paris Opéra's 1993 staging of it is a production; a cast on a night is a performance. Each record names its source. Browse by title with a genre filter, and advanced search by person, character or date.

**What fails.** One house; no narrative, no images in the record; designers sit only at production level, so a learner cannot see a designer across works without searching. **Lesson [argued]:** copy the three levels and the source line; add the narrative and image layers on top; let "the version most often seen today" (the dossier header) be a production record.

### 2.7 What the exhibitions teach, together [argued]

1. **Time spine, work cluster.** Every Diaghilev exhibition chose chronology and then clustered objects by ballet. A whole-history repertory should do the same: the era is the room, the work is the vitrine.
2. **A middle room on the craft.** The V&A broke chronology once, for a backstage gallery on music and design. A repertory can likewise break its timeline for thematic "rooms" (the comparison walls), provided it returns to time.
3. **The holder shapes the history.** The same objects read as theatre at the V&A and as fine art at the NGA. Show the holder on every image and let the lane say where the evidence lives.
4. **Show how the record survived** (the V&A's auction film; Sergeyev's notations in the blueprint's chapter 11). Survival is content.
5. **Data model from the theatre archive** (ROH): work, production, performance, with a source per record.

### 3.1 Jacob's Pillow Dance Interactive

Launched in 2011 with Doris Duke Charitable Foundation support; "more than 550 video clips filmed at Jacob's Pillow from the 1930s to today", with at least 50 added a year, "a clip from every performance from each Festival"; 144,764 unique visitors from 174 countries in 2020 [documented: Jacob's Pillow NEH grant narrative, https://www.neh.gov/sites/default/files/2024-10/FOIA%2023-29%20Jacob's%20Pillow%20Dance%20Festival,%20Inc..pdf; company figures]. Four ways in: **Browse** by era (decades 1930s to 2020s), genre (ballet, contemporary, cultural, hip hop, modern, tap) and artist; **Playlists** curated by Norton Owen; **Themes | Essays** by guest curators (Women in Dance; What is Dance?; Dance and Society; Dance of the African Diaspora; Tap; Men in Dance); and a **Guess** game of "randomly-generated multiple-choice questions" [documented: https://danceinteractive.jacobspillow.org/about; https://danceinteractive.jacobspillow.org/themes-essays]. A reviewer warned that it "was not an archive of complete dances; it was a sampling of many, many pieces, each, often, with just a minute of dance", and that autoplay segues "annoyingly" into the next clip, so she preferred the listings by decade (Sloat, DanceTabs 2021) [documented: https://dancetabs.com/2021/04/an-introduction-and-rummage-through-the-jacobs-pillow-dance-interactive-archive/].

**For the repertory [argued].** The **three facets (era, genre, artist) plus curated paths plus signed themed essays** is the simplest complete model of a works library with paths. The minute-long clip is the right size for a Label card ("see it move"). The guessing game is a retrieval-before-teaching opener that the template forbids (§1.1 of the template); use it, if at all, as a review after a path.

### 3.2 Numeridanse

Launched 24 January 2011 by Charles Picq at the Maison de la Danse, Lyon, co-developed with the Centre national de la danse; "plus de 5 000 œuvres et des centaines d'outils pédagogiques" free in 2025; about 50 contributors who deposit their videos and credits; 502,300 visitors a year on average [documented: Numeridanse press kit 2025, https://numeridanse.com/app/uploads/2025/02/hd-2025-02-dossier-de-presse-refonte.pdf; Ministère de la Culture, 12 Feb 2025]. The "Thématiques" space offers "un webdoc, un parcours ou une exposition virtuelle" to "découvrir le monde de la danse, en comprendre l'histoire, s'informer sur un style" or "préparer des élèves avant d'assister à un spectacle" [documented: CN D, https://www.cnd.fr/fr/catalogue-ressources-eac-mediation/numeridanse-thematiques]. Users make playlists, publish them to a "Bourse aux playlists", and each parcours has its text as a downloadable PDF; the Ministry of Education's training sheet suggests asking pupils to build a playlist "sur un style de danse, un courant, un.e chorégraphe, une chronologie" and to justify their choices [documented: Eduscol PNF memo 2023, https://eduscol.education.gouv.fr/sites/default/files/document/memo-numeridanse-pnfpdf-98289.pdf]. The 2025 version adds a database of artists and organisations and "un maillage entre œuvres, artistes et thématiques" [documented: Ministère].

**For the repertory [argued].** Two things to borrow. (1) **The learner builds a path** (a playlist with reasons) as the Apply task, which is the course's "closing contribution" in another form. (2) **The mesh of works, artists and themes** is the same three-way linking the Atlas cards need (work ↔ person ↔ thread). Numeridanse is contributor-fed, so the canon is whoever deposited; its ballet coverage is French-weighted (probable).

### 3.3 Google Arts & Culture

Examined in full in `../../research/pass2_presentation.md` §§1–3 (items 6–12): vertical panel stories of 30–150 words, one image per panel, bold holder line, "Tap to explore" toggles; the Bolshoi costume-department story is its best making unit. Collections are organised by partner, and a user enters by typing "dance" and choosing a company's collection (*Dance Enthusiast* guide, 2021) [documented: https://www.dance-enthusiast.com/features/day-in-the-life/view/a-beginners-guide-to-dance-on-google-arts-and-culture]. **For the repertory [argued]:** a story is organised by holder, not by work, so the same ballet appears in several unconnected stories; no timeline across partners.

### 3.4 Streaming: Royal Ballet and Opera, Paris Opéra POP, Marquee TV

POP is the Paris Opéra's own subscription platform [documented, §2.4]. The Royal Ballet and Opera's own streaming service was not examined in this pass (its news site failed to load in pass 2; several RBO productions appear on Marquee TV, below). Marquee TV sorts its ballet into "Classical Ballet", "Contemporary Ballet", "Ballet Documentaries", "Inspired by Literature" and seasonal collections such as "Cozy Ballets" and "Festive Favorites", with free short films on making (*Slow TV: Making a Tutu*, 38 min; *Slow TV: Pointe Shoe Preparation*, 31 min; *Mime in Ballet with Cynthia Harvey*, 13 min; *Akram Khan's Giselle: The Creative Process*, 8 min) and an education plan with student and group pricing [documented: https://marquee.tv/collections/ballet-documentaries; http://marquee.tv/collections/english-national-ballet-collection; https://marquee.tv/education]. **For the repertory [argued]:** streamers organise by mood and market (classical, contemporary, cosy, festive), not history; but their **free making-of shorts** are exactly the motion evidence the template reserves video for (§4.3: "only where motion, sound or touch carries the meaning"). Link them from the work card's "see it" line; never host.

### 3.5 NYPL Jerome Robbins Dance Division

"The largest and most comprehensive archive in the world devoted to the documentation of dance" (the division's own description); a Dance Oral History Project since 1974 of "over 600 interviews", accessible on site, with more than 50 full interviews to listen to online [documented: https://www.nypl.org/research/divisions/jerome-robbins-dance-division/oral-history-project-dance]. NYPL Digital Collections lets a user "Search only public domain" and states "nearly 500,000 public domain items" across the library, which "You do not need NYPL's permission to use", with a requested credit line "From The New York Public Library" [documented: https://digitalcollections.nypl.org/about]. Many video and audio items are on-site only (lock icon) [documented: NYPL remote research guide]. **For the repertory [argued]:** the public-domain filter makes NYPL the **best single source of hostable images** for works before about 1930 (prints, photographs, Denishawn); the oral histories are the "witness" voices for twentieth-century works. The interaction model is a finding aid, not teaching.

### 3.6 Bachtrack statistics

Already in `BENCHMARKS.md` L3 and `CENSUS.md` §1.1: annual ranked lists of listed performances (2025: 6,267 dance performances; *Nutcracker*, *Swan Lake*, *Romeo and Juliet*, *Sleeping Beauty*… *Giselle* sixth) [documented there; Bachtrack's own counts, "not exhaustive"]. **For the repertory [argued]:** a **"how often it is danced now" field** on each work card, labelled as Bachtrack's listing count and year, gives the learner a living-repertory signal beside the historical one, and makes visible how few works carry the present repertory.

### 3.7 Synchronous Objects for *One Flat Thing, reproduced* (2009)

Published online 1 April 2009 by William Forsythe, Maria Palazzi and Norah Zuniga Shaw with "more than 30 contributing researchers" at Ohio State's ACCAD and The Forsythe Company; it "flows from dance, to data, to objects" [documented: https://synchronousobjects.osu.edu/; https://accad.osu.edu/synchronous-objects-0]. The dance's counterpoint is decoded into three systems: 25 movement themes, "more than 200 cues", and alignments; spatial data from tracking each of 17 dancers in top and front video; attribute data "built from the dancers' firsthand accounts", with disagreements resolved by the team [documented: https://synchronousobjects.osu.edu/blog/introductory-essays-for-synchronous-objects]. About 20 objects (Movement Density as a topographic landscape; Statistical Counterpoint with linked brushing across a parallel-coordinates, a stage and a time view; a Counterpoint Tool) [documented: Pew Center essay 2013; Cressie interview 2009]. "Millions of unique visitors" (project's own figure) [documented]. It became the prototype for Motion Bank (2010 onward), whose aim is "a collection of digital dance scores" [documented: Kulturstiftung des Bundes; Dance Current 2014].

**For the repertory [argued].** It is the deepest **single-work explorable** in dance, and it shows what a Deep tier can be: the work decomposed into its systems, each system made visible, with the makers' own accounts as data. It is also a warning about cost (three years, 30 researchers) and about a single work: it teaches one choreography and "choreographic thinking", not a history. For the course, one such explorable (a *Giselle* score-and-mime object, or the *Sleeping Beauty* plan of numbers) is the ceiling, not the norm.

### 3.8 Smarthistory

Mission: "engaging, trusted, up-to-date global art history"; 3,700 videos and essays by 850 contributing scholars; 60 million page views in 2025; essays "peer-reviewed"; official art-history partner of Khan Academy and of the College Board for AP Art History; "All of our content is licensed under a Creative Commons license" (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0) [documented: https://smarthistory.org/about]. Entry by curated guides ("The Basics"), syllabi (World Art History Syllabus) and thematic series (ARCHES, Understanding Museums, Creating and Conserving, "Not your grandfather's art history: a BIPOC reader") [documented: https://smarthistory.org/].

**For the repertory [argued].** Smarthistory is the closest working model of what the repertory should be: **one card per work** (essay or two-voice video "on location"), **many paths** over the same cards (period, region, theme, syllabus), **signed and reviewed** entries, and an open licence. The two-voice video ("conversations between experts") is a format for the "Readings and disputes" head. Its NC-SA licence is not compatible with the course's hosting list (CC0, PD, CC BY, CC BY-SA), so its content is link-only; its model is free to copy.

### 3.9 MoMA, *Inventing Abstraction 1910–1925* network diagram (2012–13)

A 16 × 25 ft wall diagram at the entrance, an interactive "Connections" page and a PDF; vectors "connect individuals whose acquaintance with one another during these years could be documented"; red nodes for artists with more than 24 connections [documented: MoMA diagram PDF; Posner, DH101 2016, http://miriamposner.com/classes/dh101f16/2016/10/03/reverse-engineering-inventing-abstraction-moma-exhibition]. Built from a curators' 0/1 spreadsheet of who knew whom among the 80-odd artists, analysed by Paul Ingram and Mitali Banerjee of Columbia Business School, designed in the red-and-black of Alfred Barr's 1936 flowchart; online, a click isolates an artist's ego network with a short biography [documented: MoMA *Inside/Out* 2013; MoMA through Time]. Nijinsky, Laban and Wigman are nodes (dance is in the network) [documented: diagram PDF]. Critiques: the 24-connection threshold is unexplained and the sources of the edges (letters, magazines, catalogues) are not given (Posner); non-Western sources that Barr's chart named are omitted, and economic forces elided (Reiner and Patkowski, cited in *Artl@s Bulletin* 6(3), 2017) [documented: https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1152&context=artlas]. A reviewer argued that Barr's flowchart and the network "must co-operate", because the linear narrative "makes order out of the 'chaos'" (Berkowitz, Artsy 2013) [documented].

**For the repertory [argued].** The network is the right picture for **Diaghilev's committee** (blueprint ch. 7) and for **credit** (who worked with whom across works); the critique is the design brief: every edge needs a source and a type (collaborated on, taught, commissioned, married, quarrelled), the threshold must be stated, and the excluded must be named. Pair the network with a flowchart (lineage) and say they answer different questions.

### 3.10 The Pudding

Visual essays "explaining ideas debated in culture"; the team describes three story structures: "one point, one chart, stacked"; scrollytelling (with its own libraries, Scrollama and enter-view); and "tapping/stepping", for smaller stories [documented: https://pudding.cool/process/how-to-make-dope-shit-part-3]. *How Music Taste Evolved* (2017) lets a reader **play** every Billboard top-5 hit 1958–2016 along a scrubbable timeline [documented: https://pudding.cool/2017/03/music-history/]. *Musical motifs* (December 2025) visualises "how musicals use motifs to tell stories" [documented: pudding.cool home]. Data sets are released in a GitHub repository under the MIT licence [documented: https://github.com/the-pudding/data]. A 2026 design critique notes the cost of pure scrolling: no way to pause, replay or jump, and no table of contents [documented: IXD@Pratt, https://ixd.prattsi.org/2026/09/design-critique-the-pudding-website].

**For the repertory [argued].** The **story-then-explore** pattern (a guided scroll that ends by handing the reader the whole dataset) is the right shape for a whole-history timeline: tell the eras, then let the learner filter the 148 works. The motif essay is a model for a thread wall. Release the register as open data, as The Pudding does. Give every scroll a table of contents and controls.

### 3.11 Timeline tools

- **TimelineJS** (Northwestern Knight Lab): one slide per event from a Google Sheet, with media, credit and caption columns; "end dates will cause TimelineJS to display spans of time (a.k.a. eras)"; a **Group** column puts events "in the same row or adjacent rows" with a label at the left, but "creators cannot control the order in which groups are shown", "overlapping eras are not well-supported", and each era's colour "is not currently configurable"; Mozilla Public License 2.0 [documented: https://timeline.knightlab.com/docs/using-spreadsheets.html; https://timeline.knightlab.com/docs/license.html]. Knight Lab's own advice: "Write each event as a part of a larger narrative" and "Include events that build up to major occurrences" [documented: https://timeline.knightlab.com/].
- **vis-timeline**: items with start and optional end, **groups** with an explicit order, zoom from milliseconds to years, editable items; Apache-2.0 or MIT [documented: https://github.com/visjs/vis-timeline]. The technical fit for lanes the course controls.
- **Histography** (Matan Stauber): every dot a Wikipedia event, a resizable era bar, "Editorial Stories" handpicked across the field [documented: https://histography.io/]. A model of **scale first, story second**, and of the failure of scale: thousands of dots carry no reading.
- **StoryMapJS** (Knight Lab; MIT) for a slide-per-place route; **Neatline** (Omeka) for map plus timeline plus narrative; **Tiki-Toki**, **Sutori**, **Preceden** (freemium) [documented: UW and Tulane library guides]. Map tools must not use OpenStreetMap data under the brief.

**For the repertory [argued].** TimelineJS is a good authoring prototype (the course owner can edit a sheet) but cannot hold the lanes the repertory needs (ordered groups, overlapping eras). The built version needs ordered lanes, overlapping era bands, filters by thread and region, and a deep link to every card; vis-timeline or a hand-built SVG does that.

### 3.12 What the platforms teach, together [argued]

1. **Card plus facets plus paths** is the common winning shape (Pillow, Numeridanse, Smarthistory): one record per work, filterable by era, kind and maker, and threaded by curated, signed paths.
2. **Learners make paths** (Numeridanse playlists with reasons): the best Apply task for a repertory.
3. **One work can be an explorable** (Synchronous Objects), but at research-project cost; budget one.
4. **Networks need sourced, typed edges and a stated threshold** (MoMA's critics).
5. **Story first, then the whole set** (The Pudding; Histography's failure without story).
6. **Licences split the field**: most dance media is link-only (Pillow, Marquee, POP, Google, MoMA, OSU); hostable images come from public-domain archives (NYPL, Gallica, LoC) and CC BY(-SA) sources. Smarthistory's NC-SA is not on the course's list.

## 4. Learning evidence

What the research says about six design questions. Each study is given with what it **supports** for the repertory and how strong the support is. Where a finding was already logged in `BENCHMARKS.md` (L1–L3) it is summarised and cited there.

### 4.1 Chronology and timelines

| Study | Design | Finding | Supports |
|---|---|---|---|
| de Groot-Reuvekamp, Ros and van Boxtel (2017), *Theory & Research in Social Education* | Quasi-experiment, 16 teachers, grades 2 and 5, N = 396 treatment, 392 control | "Timewise", teaching built on "a consistent use of timelines", raised post-test scores on understanding historical time "with a medium effect size" [documented: abstract via Consensus] | A timeline used **consistently across a whole course**, not once, improves sense of period |
| de Groot-Reuvekamp (2017), PhD, Amsterdam | Five studies | Dutch pupils still lacked a sense of historical time despite the "ten-era curriculum (2006)"; gains came from teaching to stated objectives with consistent timelines [documented: abstract] | Named eras alone do not teach period; the eras must be **used** in every lesson |
| de Groot-Reuvekamp and others (2018), *Teaching and Teacher Education* | Same 16 teachers | Highest gains for teachers who implemented the approach as designed with "educative curriculum materials" [documented: abstract] | The timeline must be **built into the materials**, not left to the teacher |
| Foreman, Boyd Davis, Moar, Korallo and Chappell (2008), *Instructional Science* 36(2) | Experiments, undergraduates and children | Undergraduates ordered events better after a virtual "fly-through" timeline than after a "washing line" of images; secondary pupils gained nothing; primary pupils did best with paper images [documented: abstract] | A spatial timeline helps adults; **no medium wins for everyone**; keep a printable, plain version |
| Korallo, Foreman, Boyd Davis, Moar and Coulson (2012a), *Computers & Education* 58(2): 856–862 | Undergraduates; three **parallel** timelines (world history, art, psychology) vs booklets | VE users "better remembering the chronological ordering of all materials and successfully cross-referencing from one domain to another" (p = .033 reported) [documented: UEA abstract; Academia summary] | **Parallel lanes** help learners relate simultaneous events across domains: the direct evidence for a laned ballet timeline |
| Korallo and others (2012b), *Computers & Education* 58(4) | 127 children aged 8–9, UK and Ukraine | Passive fly-throughs hurt this age; "active challenge" helped, but only with prior familiarisation; familiarity "may reduce working memory load" [documented: abstract] | An interactive timeline needs a **task** and a short **orientation** to the medium |
| Shemilt (2000, "The Caliph's coin"; 2009, "Drinking an ocean and pissing a cupful"); Howson and Shemilt (2011); Usable Historical Pasts project (Foster, Ashby and Lee 2008; Lee and Howson 2009) | Theory and classroom studies, England | Most students finish school "without developing usable big pictures of the past" (Chapman's summary of UHP); **frameworks** are "provisional factual scaffolds" that "enable students to organise material as instantiations of, or exceptions to, high-level generalisations" (Howson and Shemilt 2011, quoted by Counsell 2011) [documented: Chapman, UCL Discovery; Counsell 2011, Roehampton reading list] | A whole-history timeline should be taught **early and quickly** as a scaffold, then filled and revised by every chapter and card; it is an instrument, not a summary to memorise |
| Gibson (2018), *Public History Weekly* | Review | Frameworks "can be taught... in a relatively short period of time (several days or weeks), but are expected to be extended, deconstructed, and reconstructed over time"; critique: they can reinforce grand narratives [documented: https://public-history-weekly.org/6-2018-27/frameworks-knowledge-big-picture] | Teach the frame in the first sitting; let disputes break it later |

**Bearing [argued].** The evidence supports (1) a single whole-history frame introduced early, (2) used **in every unit** rather than as a separate lesson, (3) with **parallel lanes** for simultaneous strands, (4) made active by a task (place this work; find what else happened that year), and (5) with a static fallback. The evidence is mostly from schools and short tasks; no study found tests a timeline across a forty-hour adult course (not found in this pass).

### 4.2 Seixas's historical thinking concepts

Seixas and Morton, *The Big Six Historical Thinking Concepts* (Nelson, 2013): **historical significance; primary source evidence; continuity and change; cause and consequence; historical perspectives; the ethical dimension** [documented: The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Historical Thinking Concepts", 2020; Chapman's bibliography]. The model is "rooted in how [historians] tackle the difficult problems of understanding the past" (Seixas and Morton 2013, p. 7, quoted in *Educational Philosophy and Theory* 2020) [documented]. It is a "pragmatic hybrid" of British, German and American traditions (Seixas 2017, *Educational Philosophy and Theory* 49(6)) [documented: abstract]. Comparable frameworks: the English National Curriculum (2013) adds "similarity and difference" and "interpretations"; Wineburg's sourcing, contextualising, corroboration and close reading; van Boxtel and van Drie's historical reasoning [documented: *Current Opinion in Psychology* review 2025, Table 1].

**What each concept asks of a repertory [argued]:**

| Concept | Question a work card or path can ask | Where it lives in the design |
|---|---|---|
| Significance | Why is this work in the repertory, and who decided? | The card's "why it is here" line; the selection rule (§5) |
| Evidence | What survives of it (score, notation, designs, film, reviews), and who holds it? | The card's evidence strip, with holder and licence |
| Continuity and change | What did it keep from earlier works, what did it change? | "Set it beside" pairs; the remake families; era bands |
| Cause and consequence | What made it possible (money, a star, a technology, a state), and what followed? | The "how it came to be" head; lineage arrows on the timeline |
| Perspectives | How did a dancer, a critic, an audience, a culture staged see it, then and now? | "Readings and disputes"; Wansink's three layers (below) |
| Ethical dimension | What harm or exclusion does it carry, and what do we owe now? | Exotic-other and yellowface walls; the repair cases (blueprint ch. 10) |

Note the "similarity and difference" concept (English curriculum) is the one the comparison walls teach directly and is absent from the Big Six.

### 4.3 Comparison and contrasting cases

- **Gentner, Loewenstein and Thompson (2003)**, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 95(2): comparing two cases gave about **twice the transfer** of studying them separately [documented in `BENCHMARKS.md` L1].
- **Alfieri, Nokes-Malach and Schunn (2013)**, *Educational Psychologist* 48(2), meta-analysis of 57 experiments: case comparison **d = 0.50**; stronger when learners look for **similarities** and when the **principle is given after** the comparison (far transfer d = 0.98 in that condition) [documented in L1].
- **Schwartz and Bransford (1998)**, *Cognition and Instruction* 16(4): analysing contrasting cases **then** being told beat being told alone and beat analysing twice without telling [documented in L1].
- **Marton and Pang (2006)**; **Raviv, Lupyan and Green (2022)**: an aspect is discerned only against variation; more variable input generalises better [documented in L3].
- **Rittle-Johnson and Star (2007)**, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 99(3): comparing two solution methods side by side beat studying them one after another, in algebra [probable: from working knowledge; not re-checked this pass].

**Bearing [argued].** The comparison wall is the single best-supported device in this benchmark. The repertory should make **pairs and walls across the whole history** its main teaching unit (not only within a chapter), enter each wall through a pair, ask for similarities first, and name the principle after. A timeline makes the cross-period pair visible (Giselle 1841 beside Masilo 2017); a thread filter on the timeline is a comparison generator.

### 4.4 Multiple perspectives and contested history

| Study | Finding | Supports |
|---|---|---|
| Wineburg (1991), *Journal of Educational Psychology* 83(1) | Historians read documents by **sourcing, corroboration and contextualisation**; students did not [probable: working knowledge; the heuristics are documented as taught in Nokes 2007] | Every reading on a card names its source and date |
| Nokes, Dole and Hacker (2007), *Journal of Educational Psychology* 99(3): 492–504 | 246 students, 8 classes randomised to 4 conditions for 3 weeks: those who read **multiple texts** "scored higher on history content and used sourcing and corroboration more often" than textbook readers [documented: APA abstract] | Several short, attributed sources per work beat one smooth summary, **for content as well as for skill** |
| Reisman (2012), *Cognition and Instruction* 30(1): 86–112 | 236 eleventh-graders, 5 San Francisco schools, 6 months of "Document-Based Lessons" in which students "interrogate, and then reconcile, historical accounts from multiple texts": significant gains on historical thinking, transfer to contemporary issues, **factual knowledge** and general reading comprehension [documented: abstract] | The dispute card (two readings, reconcile) does not cost content knowledge; it adds it |
| Stradling (2003), *Multiperspectivity in History Teaching*, Council of Europe | Multiperspectivity is "a way of viewing, and a predisposition to view" the past from different perspectives; learners must "tolerate discrepancies, contradictions, ambiguities, dissenting voices" [documented: quoted by the CoE Observatory 2022 and by Kropman's chapter] | Show contradictions in the record as content, not as error |
| Wansink, Akkerman, Zuiker and Wubbels (2018), *Theory & Research in Social Education* 46(4) | A temporal model: perspectives of subjects **in the past**, **between past and present** (historians, later stagers), and **in the present** (the learner); multiperspectivity is "the epistemological idea that history is interpretational" [documented: open-access article; CoE Observatory] | Three layers of reading per work: the premiere's people; the later critics and remakers; the learner now |
| Kropman, van Drie and van Boxtel (2022), *European Journal of Psychology of Education* | 104 tenth-graders randomised to a high- or low-multiperspectivity text on the Dutch Revolt: the high group "included significantly more perspectives" in their own summaries [documented: abstract] | The text's own structure transfers to the learner's account: write the card with perspectives built in |
| Systematic review of historical-literacy interventions (2023, ERIC EJ1403644) | All high-weight-of-evidence studies "guided students to think historically", e.g. Reisman; Huijgen and others (2018) on contextualisation by "cognitive incongruity" [documented] | Present a reading that clashes with the learner's assumption (the ballerina's "first" pointe; Degas as record) and make them resolve it |
| Barton and McCully (2007); Goldberg, Schwarz and Porat (2011), cited by Wansink (2018) | Willingness to take another perspective falls when the learner is emotionally tied to the topic [documented as cited] | The ethical-dimension cases (yellowface, apartheid-era stagings) need a protocol and a slower pace |

**Bearing [argued].** Disputes and alternative interpretations should be a **unit of content**, not a footnote: an argument card per dispute (the template's form 13), two readings in equal grammar, the sources, and a reconcile-or-hold task. The three temporal layers give every work card a fixed shape for its readings.

### 4.5 Cognitive load, timelines and browsing

| Finding | Source | Supports |
|---|---|---|
| Interesting but irrelevant "seductive details" reduce recall and transfer; placing them **at the start** made the effect worse, **at the end** reduced it; the damage comes from "priming inappropriate schemas" | Harp and Mayer (1998), *Journal of Educational Psychology* 90(3): 414–434 [documented: abstract] | "Facts worth telling" must be **relevant** to the work's idea and go **after** the core, never as an opener |
| Meta-analyses: Rey (2012), 39 effects, retention d ≈ 0.30, transfer d ≈ 0.48 (as reported by Rey 2014); Sundararajan and Adesope (2020), 58 papers, 7,521 participants, small-to-moderate harm (g ≈ −0.33 overall, as summarised by ACU INSPIRE), worse when learners cannot control pace | [documented: ScienceDirect abstract; PMC review 2022; ACU INSPIRE] | Same; and let the learner control pace (cards, not autoplay) |
| Spatial contiguity, segmenting and signalling (meta-analyses by Ginns 2006, *Learning and Instruction* 16; Rey and others 2019, *Educational Psychology Review* 31; Richter, Scheiter and Eitel 2016, *Educational Research Review* 17) | Cited in Sundararajan and Adesope's reference list [documented as cited; effect sizes not re-checked] | Label on the image, not in a legend; one era or one card per screen; signal the lane and the thread by colour **and** label |
| Primary children learned chronology from VEs only after familiarisation, which "may reduce working memory load" | Korallo and others (2012b) [documented] | A one-screen orientation before the interactive timeline |
| Browsing a large hypertext can raise load and lower comprehension for novices; learner control has near-zero average benefit | DeStefano and LeFevre (2007), *Computers in Human Behavior* 23; Karich, Burns and Maki (2014), *Review of Educational Research* 84 [probable: working knowledge, not re-checked this pass] | Novices need **guided paths** first; the open explorer is for after a path, or for returning learners (the expertise-reversal effect, Kalyuga and others 2003 [probable]) |

**Bearing [argued].** A whole-history timeline of 148 works is a high-element display. To keep load down: show **eras and lanes first** with a handful of landmark works (Gombrich's "welcome landmarks", §1.9), reveal the rest by filter or zoom, one card open at a time, labels in place, and a guided path before free exploration.

### 4.6 Narrative versus encyclopedic structures

- **Mar, Li, Nguyen and Ta (2021)**, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 28(3): 732–749, meta-analysis of 37 studies, 150 effect sizes, more than 33,000 participants: narrative texts were "more easily understood and better recalled than essays", **g = 0.55** [0.31, 0.79]; memory g = 0.72, comprehension g = 0.48; adults g = 0.44; the advantage held when content and difficulty were controlled [documented: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8219577]. One commentator reports that the authors "warn explicitly against forcing all instructional material into narrative form" [documented as reported by a secondary source; not checked in the paper this pass].
- **Shemilt (2000)** and **Counsell (2011)**: "overview-depth interplay" (Banham's principle): many small memorable stories nested in an overview give pupils "confidence to play with new narrative connections" [documented: Counsell 2011].
- **Reference works** (Oxford, Balanchine–Mason, DHC) are encyclopedic by design: they serve lookup, not learning (§1.13).

**Bearing [argued].** The narrative advantage is robust and favours the blueprint's history-first chapters and the dossier's "How it came to be" story. But a story is one path; a learner who needs to compare or to look something up needs the encyclopedic layer. The design answer is **narrative paths over an encyclopedic set of cards**: every card opens with a short story (Story tier, 150–250 words), and the cards are reachable both by story paths and by facets.

### 4.7 What the evidence adds up to [argued]

1. **A frame first, used everywhere** (Timewise; Shemilt; Howson and Shemilt): one whole-history timeline, taught in the first sitting, returned to in every chapter.
2. **Parallel lanes** for simultaneous strands (Korallo 2012a).
3. **Comparison across periods as the core activity** (Gentner 2003; Alfieri 2013; Schwartz and Bransford 1998).
4. **Disputes as content, from several sources, with three temporal layers** (Nokes 2007; Reisman 2012; Wansink 2018; Kropman 2022).
5. **Stories over cards; facts after the core; guided before free** (Mar 2021; Harp and Mayer 1998; Sundararajan and Adesope 2020).
6. **Gaps**: no study found tests a works-based repertory or a whole-history timeline in adult design education; outcome evidence for "recurring case" and "explorer" designs is weak (see `BENCHMARKS.md` L1). The design should be tested with five learners per round (Nielsen and Landauer 1993, in L4) before it is built at scale.

## 5. Recommendation

### 5.1 What has to be decided

The course owner wants, beside the thirteen-chapter unit, a whole history of ballet through its works: a timeline, deep dives on most famous works, reference points, facts, design and art knowledge, and comparisons, contradictions and alternative readings across the whole history. The material largely exists: 148 works in `register.yaml` (35 tier A, 48 tier B, 65 tier C), about 97 dossiers in `dossiers/D1`–`D8` at 1,100–1,700 words, 24 threads, 18 debates and the myth register in `X1`, ten eras of the stage picture and the designers in `X2`. The decision is **where this sits and how a learner moves through it**, without duplicating the blueprint's chapters (which already tell the history) and without breaking the template's rules (history first, one worked example, records apart from teaching).

### 5.2 Four designs

**Design A. A companion unit: "Four centuries in fifty works".** A second unit, about 15–20 hours, ten era-chapters in date order, five works taken apart in each under fixed heads (Kirstein's model, §1.1), a wall at the end of each era.
- *Strengths.* A complete, finishable course with a clear promise; narrative order (Mar and others 2021, g = 0.55); easy to explain; the dossiers convert almost directly.
- *Costs.* About 50,000–70,000 learner words; a second set of chapters to version and review (template §7).
- *Risks.* It re-tells the history the thirteen chapters already tell, against the template's rule that units "never re-tell the history" (§4.2); two parallel narratives drift apart; the fixed fifty becomes a canon with an author's bias (Kirstein's and Gombrich's critics, §§1.1, 1.9); adds 15–20 hours to a 41-hour unit, and each boundary between parts is a door out (MITx data, `BENCHMARKS.md` L2).
- *Evidence.* Narrative advantage (Mar 2021); overview-depth interplay (Counsell 2011). Against: weak evidence for parallel courses; attrition between parts (Moreno 2019 in L2).

**Design B. A timeline layer threaded through the chapters.** No new unit. One whole-history timeline with lanes opens every chapter at its era; each work mentioned is a dot that opens its card; walls stay inside chapters.
- *Strengths.* The best-evidenced use of a timeline: one frame, used consistently in every lesson (de Groot-Reuvekamp 2017; Shemilt; Howson and Shemilt 2011); parallel lanes (Korallo 2012a); cheap in words.
- *Costs.* Timeline build and data (about 300–500 dated events); card tiers for works the chapters name.
- *Risks.* Works not named in a chapter have nowhere to live; the "deep dives on most of the famous ballets" are not delivered; cross-history comparisons stay chapter-bound; no way in for a learner who wants a work, not a chapter.
- *Evidence.* Timewise; Korallo; frameworks research. Silent on deep dives.

**Design C. An Atlas repertory explorer.** A browsable set of 148 work cards with facets (era, region, thread, maker, designer), a zoomable timeline, a collaboration network and the thread walls; no paths, entered from anywhere.
- *Strengths.* Delivers breadth and deep dives; reference use (Oxford, Balanchine–Mason, §1.6, 1.2); the model of Smarthistory, Jacob's Pillow and Numeridanse (§3.12); reusable by every F1 unit; open data like The Pudding.
- *Costs.* Card tiers for all 148 works (about 70,000 words at the census plan's tiers); facets and network data with sourced, typed edges.
- *Risks.* Browsing without a path overloads novices and has near-zero average benefit from learner control (DeStefano and LeFevre 2007; Karich and others 2014, probable); Histography's failure of scale without story (§3.11); facts become seductive details if not tied to the work's idea (Harp and Mayer 1998); a network invites the MoMA critique (§3.9).
- *Evidence.* Card-plus-facets is common practice with little outcome evidence; the cognitive-load evidence warns against it as a first experience.

**Design D (recommended). A hybrid: "The Repertory", an Atlas layer with one timeline, many cards and a few guided routes.** The explorer of C, with the timeline of B as its spine and as the opening frame of every chapter, plus a small number of **routes** (guided paths over the same cards), one of which is the fifty-work chronological journey of A, written as a route, not a unit.
- *Strengths.* Each piece of evidence gets its device: the frame used everywhere (Timewise; Shemilt); parallel lanes (Korallo); comparison across periods as the core activity (Gentner; Alfieri); stories on every card and guided before free (Mar; Sundararajan and Adesope); disputes as content with three temporal layers (Nokes; Reisman; Wansink). No second narrative competes with the chapters, because routes are sequences of cards, not new chapters. One record set serves three spines: by time, by thread, A to Z (§1.13).
- *Costs.* The cards (largest cost, but they are needed under any design that delivers deep dives); the timeline build; route writing (short: a route is an introduction, links and transitions, about 1,000–2,000 words); the selection rule and counter.
- *Risks.* Scope creep (148 Deep cards is not needed; see tiers below); the timeline becomes decoration if chapters do not use it (Timewise found gains only with consistent use, de Groot-Reuvekamp 2018); network edges without sources (fix: typed, sourced edges only).
- *Evidence.* The sum of §4.7.

### 5.3 The recommended design in detail

#### Name and promise

**The Repertory: ballet's history in its works.** One sentence on the first screen: *"Four hundred and forty years of ballet on one line of time: open any work to see how it was made, what it borrowed, how it looked, who argued about it, and what was made after it."* Through-question (shared with the unit): how do you turn ideas, bodies and things into a world other people can enter, and keep it alive after you?

Above the lanes, a thin **Debates strip**: each argument card is pinned twice, at the time it concerns and at the time it was argued (for example the "who made *Giselle*" credit question, 1841, and its scholarly debate, twentieth century), which makes Wansink's "between past and present" layer visible on the line. Disputed dates are drawn as ranges with the confidence word (as the London Museum leaves its date range visible, `pass2_presentation.md` §2.21).

**Behaviour** (from §§3.11, 4.5): opens on the ten era bands with three to five landmark works each (Gombrich's "welcome landmarks"); the other works appear on zoom or by filter (thread, region, kind, tier, designer); one card opens at a time beside the line; labels sit on the marks, not in a legend; colour is always doubled by a label; a deep link for every era, work and filter state, so a chapter can open the timeline "at E5, thread T05". A one-screen orientation ("how to read this line") comes first (Korallo 2012b). A **printable poster** version, like Gardner's Global Timeline (§1.10), is the static fallback (Foreman 2008). Build: prototype in TimelineJS from a sheet (MPL 2.0), then the course build on vis-timeline (Apache-2.0 or MIT) or hand-made SVG, because TimelineJS cannot order its groups or overlap eras (§3.11).

#### Paths through it

| Route | What it is | Length | Order |
|---|---|---|---|
| **R0 The line in one sitting** | The ten eras, one landmark work each, the six lanes explained; ends by asking the learner to place one work they know | 45 minutes | Chronological. It is chapter 0's lineage walk, made on the timeline, and the first thing every learner does (frame first: Shemilt; Gibson 2018) |
| **R1 Four centuries in fifty works** | Five works per era at Story tier, with the Deep tier one click away; an era card between eras; one pair at the end of each era | 12–15 hours | Chronological (Kirstein's fifty, with a published selection rule and at least one work per era from outside western Europe and North America where the record allows) |
| **R2 Thread routes** (one per wall, starting with five: two worlds; the white act; the exotic other and its answers; the remake families; a woman against family and patriarch) | Pair, then wall, then principle, then the exception; works from three centuries and three regions | 1–2 hours each | Across time, by comparison (Gentner; Alfieri) |
| **R3 The stage picture** | `X2`'s ten eras of design as a route through design cards and the works' "Design and art" heads | 3 hours | Chronological within lane 4 |
| **R4 Arguments** | Six debates from `X1` (story or form; who is the author; authenticity and reconstruction; Orientalism; race and access; is ballet dying) as argument cards with their works | 3 hours | By question |
| **R5 The world stage** | National and regional works (`D8`, T14 and T08): one form, many states | 2–3 hours | By region, then time |

A route is a sequence of existing cards with a short introduction (150 words), one transition sentence per step, and a closing say-back; it is not a chapter, so it adds no history the cards do not hold.

#### How works, debates and design knowledge appear

- **Works**: as dots on lane 1, as cards at their tier, as members of walls and routes. Each card ends with **"Set it beside"** (two to four works from other periods or places), which is the cross-history comparison generator; the timeline's thread filter shows the same pairs in time.
- **Debates**: as argument cards (equal grammar, named holders), as the "Readings and disputes" head on every work card (at least two readings, each with author, title, year), as the Debates strip on the timeline, and as route R4. Myths as myth cards linked from the work they concern.
- **Design and art**: as lane 4 and its ten-era band, as the "Design and art" head (concrete: a colour, a fabric, a device), as design cards for designers and recurring ideas, as route R3, and on every image as a tombstone caption with a **kind label**: *record* (photograph of the production), *design* (made before), *artist's image* (made after, like Degas, §2.3).
- **Facts worth telling**: last on each card, five to eight, each relevant to the work's idea and with its confidence word (Harp and Mayer 1998).
- **Evidence and licences**: every image hosted only if CC0, PD, CC BY or CC BY-SA (NYPL public domain, Gallica, LoC, Wikimedia Commons with verified licence); everything else link-only (company films, Marquee, POP, Jacob's Pillow, Smarthistory, MoMA, OSU).

#### How it links to the rest of the program

- **F1**: other units link to work cards and walls as tiles (`review/R1_curriculum_fit.md` §2.1); the field atlas receives the timeline's era and lane model as a pattern other topics can reuse (an opera or a fashion topic would use the same six lanes, relabelled).
- **Method and practice areas** (template §6): work cards' "What a maker can take from it" feed M1.11a (premise and bibles); the remake families feed M4 (remaking); the divertissement, season and costume-family cards feed M3 (collection logics); lane 4 feeds S1 (space and experience) and S3 (wearables).
- **Open data**: `register.yaml` plus a new `timeline_events.yaml` published as a data file (The Pudding's practice, §3.10), with the selection rule and its counter.

#### Selection rule and counter (constitution: counts with their counter)

A work is in the repertory if it meets at least one published criterion, recorded in its "Why it is here" strip: (1) earliest known instance of a device that lasted; (2) in the living repertory (Bachtrack top 30 in any year of the last decade); (3) a remake family of three or more productions; (4) a design or credit case the course teaches; (5) a regional counterweight, so that every era holds at least one work made outside western Europe and North America where the record allows. The counter on the first screen: **148 works of an estimated 20,000–50,000 premiered since 1581** (`CENSUS.md` §1.2, argued), with who chose and when, after the DHC's practice (§1.7).

#### Data additions

- `register.yaml`: add `era` (E1–E10), `danced_now` (Bachtrack count and year, labelled), `inclusion` (criteria 1–5), `evidence` (what survives, holder, licence), `productions` (ids).
- New `timeline_events.yaml`: id, date or range, confidence, lane (1–6), era, title (under 12 words), source, links (work, person, card). Estimate 300–500 events.
- New `edges.yaml` for the collaboration network (only if built): from, to, type (commissioned, designed for, danced in, taught, remade, quarrelled), date, source. No edge without a source (MoMA critique, §3.9).

#### Build order and size

1. **Timeline prototype and R0** (TimelineJS from a sheet; 10 era cards; 50 landmark events; the 35 tier A works at Label tier). Test with five learners (Nielsen and Landauer 1993, in L4): can they place a work, read a lane, find a pair?
2. **Work cards**: Label for all 148; Story for tiers A and B (83); Deep for the 35 tier A works. About 35,000 words.
3. **Thread walls R2** (five) and **argument cards R4** (six). About 8,000 words.
4. **R1** route text (about 2,000 words over existing cards) and the 15 extra Deep cards it needs (about 13,000 words).
5. **Course build** of the timeline (lanes, filters, deep links, poster); then R3, R5, R6.
6. Collaboration network last, and only with sourced, typed edges.

Total learner text: about 55,000–60,000 words, most of it the cards that any design delivering deep dives would need; the routes add little. Learning time: R0 45 minutes for everyone; R1 12–15 hours for those who choose it; others 1–3 hours each.

#### What this design does not do

It does not create a second unit, it does not make the fifty works a closed canon, and it does not host media the course cannot license. It does not claim evidence it lacks: no study tests a works-based repertory with a laned timeline for adult design learners; the first build is a test.

#### Open questions for the course owner

1. Is R1 (the fifty-work route) wanted as a certificated path with its own say-backs, or as an optional route? The evidence favours optional (attrition at boundaries, L2).
2. Ten eras or eight (the register's present `period` field has eight values)? This file recommends ten, because E5/E6 and E8/E9 carry different changes; the register field would be remapped.
