# Ballet, pass 2: the dancer's body as a technology, and the crafts and labour around it

Research notes for F1 "Histories of Making". Compiled 20 October 2026 from web sources fetched on that date.

Conventions used throughout:

- **Confidence notes.** *documented* = stated by the institution, manufacturer, patent office or peer-reviewed paper that owns the fact; *probable* = stated by a reputable secondary source (trade press, museum blog, newspaper) with no primary record checked; *contested* = sources disagree or the claim is marketing; *not found* = searched, nothing located.
- **Counts always name the counter and the date.** Company pointe-shoe and tutu figures are fundraising or press numbers unless a paper or annual report is cited; they are flagged as such.
- "Earliest known" is used throughout; "first" appears only inside direct quotations from sources.

## 1. The pointe shoe

### 1.1 Construction and the traditional ("turn shoe") method

The traditional pointe shoe is a satin slipper whose toe is built up into a rigid *box* from layers of fabric, paper and paste, with a stiffened *shank* under the arch. It is assembled inside-out on a last and then turned right-side-out ("turn shoe" method); the pleats under the toe are the gathered margin of the satin upper where it meets the sole.

| Part | What it is / how made | Materials | Source and confidence |
|---|---|---|---|

Freed: "There's no wood in the block of a pointe shoe." | Freed factory photo-essay, Spitalfields Life, 16 May 2013 https://spitalfieldslife.com/2013/05/16/at-freed-of-london-ltd-ballet-theatrical-shoemakers/ (documented, as a factory visit; the author is not Freed); Wikipedia "Pointe shoe" https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointe_shoe (probable) |

Earliest known pointe work (for the chronology lecture): Amalia Brugnoli is described as dancing on full pointe in Vienna in 1822–23 (Wikipedia gives both years; *contested*) https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amalia_Brugnoli ; Marie Taglioni danced *La Sylphide* (Paris Opéra, 12 March 1832) in darned, leather-soled satin slippers, which Pointe Magazine (Julia Rosica, 4 Aug 2020) calls the first full-length ballet on pointe https://pointemagazine.com/history-of-pointe-shoes/ (*probable*; the premiere date is *documented* via the Australian Ballet blog https://australianballet.com.au/blog/ghosts-in-the-gaslight). The Italian reinforced box of newspaper, flour paste and pasteboard dates from the 1870s–90s (Pointe Magazine, *probable*). Pavlova's hardened box and internal leather sole, early 1900s (Pointe Magazine and Wikipedia, *probable*).

### 1.5 The dancers' own preparation labour and the shoe rooms

- Dancers sew their own ribbons and elastics, darn or glue platforms, and break shoes in; the factory supplies the shoe only. Sandra Madgwick (former BRB principal) refers to "the hours spent stitching ribbons and elastics" but does not quantify them, BRB story, 5 Feb 2024 https://brb.org.uk/stories/my-first-ballet-shoes-sandra-madgwick (documented statement, no number).
- Paris Opéra dancers' 2023 dispute put a number on preparation labour generally: union representative Matthieu Botto said dancers spend about 42 hours a month preparing (make-up, dressing, undressing) against an allowance covering about 6 hours, Euronews, 20 Dec 2023 https://www.euronews.com/2023/12/20/paris-opera-ballerinas-to-go-on-strike-over-christmas-period (probable; union figure, pointe-shoe sewing not itemised).
- **NYCB shoe room.** Supervisor Linnette Roe (former Pacific Northwest Ballet principal), 2018; earlier "ballet shoe supervisor" Angel Betancourt, 2007. Roe: "If the shoe isn't right, the dancer can't do her job." Sources as in 1.4 (probable).
- **Royal Opera House shoe room.** No named shoe-room staff located on rbo.org.uk; the RBO film "How Royal Ballet dancers prepare their pointe shoes" (YouTube id IdFW5ArdTC8, mirrored at https://racer.lt/play/how-royal-ballet-dancers-prepare-their-pointe-shoes-IdFW5ArdTC8-1808932) shows dancers' own preparation. Named ROH shoe staff: *not found*.
- Darcey Bussell's signed worn pair is catalogued by ROH Collections https://rohcollections.org.uk/Magnify/Costumes5687.aspx (documented object).

### 1.6 Freed's maker's-mark system and named makers

- System: each pointe-shoe maker works at an individual bench on piecework and stamps a personal symbol on the sole; dancers order by maker. Symbols reported: Crown, Wine Glass, Fish (Spitalfields Life 2013, documented by observation); "letters, a key, a heart, and a Maltese Cross" (4dancers, probable); Wikipedia: "a letter or symbol stamped on the sole of custom-ordered shoes" (probable). Freed's own history page does not describe the system (checked).
- Output per maker: "Crown makes 41 pairs a day" (Spitalfields Life 2013); "about 30 to 40 pairs a day" (4dancers); "31 pairs a day, or 35" (Spectator 2019); "about 50 shoes a day" (Wikipedia). Headcount: "20 to 30 cobblers" (4dancers); "24 artisans" (Business Insider, 2021, title only; article blocked); "26 shoemakers" (Wikipedia). All *contested* between sources; none is Freed's own published number.
- Heritage Crafts (UK) counts "2 businesses employ two or more makers", "21–50 professionals", "3 apprentices ... at Freed of London" https://www.heritagecrafts.org.uk/pointe-shoe-making/ (documented by the listing body; date of count not given on page).
- Named Freed people (Spitalfields Life, visit of May 2013, republished 18 Jan 2019 https://spitalfieldslife.com/2019/01/18/at-freed-of-london-ltd-ballet-theatrical-shoe-makers/ ): the pointe-shoe maker known only by his mark **"Crown"** (24 years making); **Ozel Ahmed** (supervisor, theatrical department); **Jimmy Fenn** (designer and pattern cutter); **Ronny Taylor** (lasting-room foreman); **Ken Manu** (heel moulder); **Sanjay Sanjawah** (panel trimmer); **Ali Aksar** (sole-press operator); **Jerry Kelly** (production director); **Worral Thomas** (hand laster); **Charlie Johnson** (side laster); **Tony Collins** (machine operator, c. 40 years); **Luthu Miah** (supervisor, binding room); **Varsha Bahen** (finisher); **Rashimi Patel** (pairing); **Sheila Goodman** (pointe-shoe finisher) and **Philip Goodman** (chargehand), both c. 40 years. These are a journalist's record, not Freed's: *probable* for names and roles.
- Other named shoemakers: **Roman Kukushkin**, former Bolshoi Theatre shoemaker, founder of Russian Class (1991) (Moscow Times 2011, probable); **Sergei Murza** (craftsman), **Olga Monakhova** (sewing workshop, 27 years), **Irina Sobakina** (deputy head of production), all Grishko Moscow (AFP/Moscow Times 2020, probable); **Mark Suffolk**, founder of Suffolk Dance pointe-shoe factory, Leicester, 2000 (Heritage Crafts, documented); **Sophie Simpson**, Freed pointe-shoe fitter (Moscow Times 2011, probable).

## 2. The tutu's engineering

### 2.1 Romantic bell versus classical plate

| Type | Form | Earliest known | Layers and materials | Source and confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Romantic (bell) | Fitted bodice, light floating skirt to mid-calf, modelled on fashionable dress | Marie Taglioni's *La Sylphide* costume, 1832, designed by Eugène Lami | 3–5 layers of tulle/gauze (Australian Ballet); tarlatan (open-weave starched cotton) was the main tutu fabric until the early 20th century (London Museum) | London Museum blog, Kathy Kincel, 17 Apr 2025 https://www.londonmuseum.org.uk/blog/anna-pavlova-ballet-swan-costume-evolution-tutu/ (documented, museum); Australian Ballet blog, Heather Bloom, 10 May 2024 https://australianballet.com.au/blog/how-to-make-a-tutu (documented, company) |

### 5.2 Note on naming

Across the institutional pages fetched, every designer is named (Tim Hatley, Toer van Schayk, Bob Crowley, Mara Blumenfeld, Karinska, Eugène Lami), every dancer quoted is named, and makers are named in only three institutional sources: BRB (four costume staff, 2026), DNB (three, 2023), and the London Museum (a historical maker, 2025). Freed's own website names its founder and famous customers but not one of its c. 25 makers, who are known publicly only by stamped symbols and through a journalist's 2013 visit. The Paris Opéra's heritage story about its artisans names its outside suppliers and no employee. The ROH, with the largest costume and shoe operation in the UK, names roles, not people. Dancers, by contrast, are named even in fundraising copy (Céline Gittens on the BRB appeal page). The pattern is consistent enough to teach as a finding: the body is named; the hands that equip it are usually not.

## What is solid enough to teach, and what is PR

**Solid (peer-reviewed or primary):** the Royal Ballet injury rates (Mattiussi et al., BJSM 2021: 3.9/3.1 medical-attention and 1.2/1.1 time-loss injuries per 1,000 h, women/men, 123 dancers, 417,693 h) and workload (Shaw et al., JDMS 2022: 19–27.5 h/week by rank, principals 28 and artists 113 shows a season); the Allen et al. 2012 rate of 4.4 injuries per 1,000 h in 52 dancers; the Savoy's electric stage on 28 Dec 1881 (*The Times*, next day); the Palais Garnier 5% rake (Opéra's own page); the Minden patent record (US 4,901,453 of 1990, US 5,035,069 of 1991, US 5,740,618 of 1998), which also shows that the "1993 patent" is a launch date, not a grant date; the ministers' letter of 23 Dec 2019 preserving retirement at 42 for Paris Opéra dancers recruited before 2022; the AGMA agreement dates and percentages; the Heritage Crafts count of two UK pointe-shoe businesses; and the tutu hours given by named institutional staff (DNB 38 h per tutu; London Museum 30–40 h for a hand-sewn skirt; BRB two weeks for a Kitri tutu).

**Company PR or fundraising, usable only with that label:** every pointe-shoe count and budget (NYCB $780,000 / 9,000–11,000 pairs; RBO "over 6,000 pairs" at "about £50"; BRB "almost 4,000 pairs", £260,000, £65 a pair, "20 pairs a month"), all of which come from appeal pages or staff quoted in press and none from an audited report; Freed's annual output, which ranges from 150,000 to 333,000 pairs depending on who is quoted; Gaynor Minden's "three to six times longer"; the Australian Ballet's $5,000–10,000 tutu cost; the Paris Opéra's €8 million strike loss, which is the institution's own figure in a political dispute.

**Contested or undocumented at source:** the 1698 origin of the Opéra pension (reported by AFP, no primary cited); the barre and mirror dates (the IADMS paper itself says the mirror's history "is not clearly documented"); the 1948 Marley date (told by rival flooring firms); Brugnoli's 1822 or 1823; the gas-fire cause of the 1873 Le Peletier blaze. The Freed makers' names rest on one journalist's visit; they are the most valuable thing in these notes for the "unnamed makers" argument precisely because nothing institutional corroborates them.
