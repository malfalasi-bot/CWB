21 November 2026. Pass X1 under `BRIEF_REPERTORY.md`. Status: in progress.

Confidence words: **documented** (seen at source, or in a named source), **probable** (secondary or working knowledge), **contested** (both readings given), **argued** (an interpretation). Register ids refer to `register.yaml`; threads T01 to T24 to `CENSUS.md` §4.

## Part 1. The great debates

Eighteen debates that run across the whole history. Each gives the question in plain words, the positions with the people who hold them, the works that carry the debate across at least three centuries or regions (register ids), what the evidence can and cannot settle, and what a learner should take away. Positions are written in the same grammar on purpose: "X holds that..." for each side. Where a position is our summary of a body of opinion rather than one author's thesis, it is marked **argued**.

### 1.1 Story or form? Narrative against abstraction

**The question.** Should a ballet tell a story, or is dancing to music enough?

**Positions.**
- *Ballet must tell a story.* Jean-Georges Noverre, *Lettres sur la danse et sur les ballets* (Lyon and Stuttgart, 1760), holds that dance must express passions and action, and that steps without meaning are mere skill **[documented, title and thesis; probable, wording]**. Gasparo Angiolini, his rival, held the same aim and disputed only who got there first (*Lettere di Gasparo Angiolini a Monsieur Noverre*, 1773) **[probable]**. Michel Fokine, letter to *The Times*, 6 July 1914, holds that every step must serve the drama and the period, not a stock vocabulary **[documented in W4]**. Soviet *drambalet* (Rostislav Zakharov, Leonid Lavrovsky, 1930s to 1950s) holds that ballet must be realist theatre with psychology and plot, as socialist realism required **[probable]**.
- *Ballet is music made visible and needs no plot.* Fyodor Lopukhov's *Dance Symphony* (*Velichie mirozdaniya*, Petrograd, 1923) holds that dance can unfold like a symphony **[probable]**. George Balanchine, "Notes on Choreography", *Dance Index* (February to March 1945), holds that choreography is its own art of movement in time **[documented, bibliographic in Stoneley 2007; content probable]**. Yuri Grigorovich's *Stone Flower* (1957) and *Legend of Love* (1961) bring "symphonic" dance back into Soviet story ballet, which Christina Ezrahi, *Swans of the Kremlin* (2012), reads as a thaw-era revolt against drambalet **[probable]**.
- *The opposition is itself historical.* Susan Leigh Foster, *Choreography and Narrative: Ballet's Staging of Story and Desire* (1996), holds that "story" and "pure dance" are categories each period builds for its own uses **[probable]**.

**Carriers.** don-juan-angiolini-1761, medee-et-jason-1763, bayadere-1877 (the Shades as proto-abstraction), les-sylphides-1909, serenade-1934, fountain-bakhchisarai-1934, romeo-juliet-lavrovsky-1940, symphony-in-c-1947, stone-flower-1957, agon-1957, in-the-middle-1987, woolf-works-2015. France, Austria, Russia, USSR, USA, Britain; 1761 to 2015.

**What the evidence can and cannot settle.** It can settle what makers wrote and what audiences were given (scenarios, programmes, notations). It cannot settle which is "better", and it shows that the line is porous: plotless works keep traces of story (*Serenade*'s fallen girl), and story ballets contain long stretches of pure dance (every *divertissement*).

**Take-away.** Treat story and form as two dials, not two camps. A world can be built from rules of movement and music as firmly as from a plot.

### 1.2 Authenticity, reconstruction and revision

**The question.** Can a lost ballet be brought back as it was, and should it be?

**Positions.**
- *Recover the original from the documents.* The Mariinsky's 1999 *Sleeping Beauty* (staged by Sergei Vikharev from the Stepanov notations of the Sergeyev Collection, Harvard Theatre Collection) holds that the 1890 text can be recovered beneath later accretion **[documented in W3]**. Millicent Hodson and Kenneth Archer (Joffrey Ballet, Los Angeles, 1987; Hodson, *Nijinsky's Crime against Grace*, 1996) hold that Nijinsky's lost *Rite* can be rebuilt from Rambert's annotated score, Valentine Gross's pastels, Roerich's designs and reviews; Hodson calls it a "puzzle" filled "between the last known movement and the next known movement" **[documented, NYPL/All Arts 2024; Launay 2003]**. Alexei Ratmansky's *Sleeping Beauty* (ABT, 2015) and his later Petipa stagings hold that notation recovers style as well as steps (lower legs, smaller jumps, more mime) **[documented in W3]**.
- *A ballet lives only in bodies; every staging is a new work.* Robert Joffrey held that "there is really no such thing as a 're-creation'" **[documented, quoted NYPL exhibition text via All Arts, 2024]**. Hanna Järvinen, in *Studies in Costume and Performance* (2020 to 2021, open-access manuscripts), holds that the 1987 *Rite* is a "so-called reconstruction" that has acquired "a hegemonic status" and misreads the surviving costumes **[documented]**. A *New Yorker* critic in 2001 ("The Lost Nijinsky") found it "tame, folkloric" **[documented]**. Richard Taruskin, *Text and Act* (1995), holds for music that "authenticity" is a modern taste projected onto the past **[probable]**.
- *Revision was always the tradition.* Petipa revised his own and others' ballets; *Giselle*, *La Bayadère* and *Swan Lake* reach us through St Petersburg revisions of 1884, 1900 and 1895 **[probable; documented in W1]**. Marian Smith, *Ballet and Opera in the Age of Giselle* (2000), and Doug Fullington's work with her on the Justamant and Sergeyev *Giselle* sources hold that versions are the object of study, not a fall from an original **[probable]**.

**Carriers.** sylphide-1832 (Pierre Lacotte's 1972 "re-creation"), giselle-1841, pharaohs-daughter-1862 (Lacotte, Bolshoi 2000), bayadere-1877, swan-lake-1877-1895, sleeping-beauty-1890, rite-of-spring-1913, sleeping-beauty-ratmansky-2015. France, Russia, USA; 1832 to 2015.

**What the evidence can and cannot settle.** Notation records positions, counts and floor patterns; it rarely records weight, tempo, style, the principal's variations as danced, or the lighting. Surviving costumes constrain what bodies could do (Järvinen's point). No document settles which "original" is meant: premiere night, the author's last revision, or the version the audience loved.

**Take-away.** "Reconstruction" is a design decision with a stated evidence base. Ask of any revival: which sources, which gaps, who filled them, and what was the aim.

### 1.3 Who is the author?

**The question.** Is a ballet the work of the choreographer, or of a team that includes librettist, composer, designer and dancers?

**Positions.**
- *The choreographer is the author.* The programme, the copyright and the estate say so: the George Balanchine Trust licenses "Balanchine" works; Fokine's heirs license Fokine **[documented in G4 and scope 05]**. Much ballet history is written as a chain of choreographer-masters (Homans, *Apollo's Angels*, 2010, organises chapters by masters and national schools) **[documented in S file]**.
- *Authorship is shared or misattributed.* Roland John Wiley, *The Life and Ballets of Lev Ivanov* (1997), holds that the lakeside acts of the 1895 *Swan Lake* are Ivanov's, though the poster leads with Petipa **[probable]**. Ivanov also made most of *Nutcracker* (1892) after Petipa fell ill **[probable]**. Librettists shaped the world: Vernoy de Saint-Georges co-wrote *Giselle* with Gautier; Ivan Vsevolozhsky, director of the Imperial Theatres, wrote the *Sleeping Beauty* scenario and designed its costumes **[probable; documented in W1]**. Alexandre Benois claimed the *Schéhérazade* scenario that the poster gave to Bakst, a dispute Lynn Garafola, *Diaghilev's Ballets Russes* (1989), records **[probable]**. Who had the idea for the *Rite* (Roerich or Stravinsky) is contested in W2.
- *The dancer is a co-author.* Sally Banes, *Dancing Women* (1998), holds that the dancer's performance can contradict and enlarge the role as written **[documented]**. Anthea Kraut, *Choreographing Copyright* (2015), holds that the law's idea of a single author has excluded dancers, women and Black artists from credit **[probable]**. Pierina Legnani's 32 fouettés (1893 to 1895) and Pavlova's shaping of *The Dying Swan* are often cited as dancer authorship **[probable]**.

**Carriers.** ballet-de-la-nuit-1653 (the king as maker), giselle-1841, swan-lake-1877-1895, sleeping-beauty-1890, nutcracker-1892, dying-swan-1905, scheherazade-1910, rite-of-spring-1913, red-poppy-1927, white-haired-girl-shanghai-1965 (collective credit in a Cultural Revolution "model work"), rites-page-1997 (Bangarra and the Australian Ballet, with cultural consultants). France, Russia, China, Australia; 1653 to 1997.

**What the evidence can and cannot settle.** Posters, contracts, rehearsal notes and letters can show who did what on given days. They cannot settle what counts as authorship, which is a legal and moral choice that changes with the period (a 1704 Paris privilege dispute between Beauchamps and Feuillet over notation is the earliest known case in ballet's line, in scope 01).

**Take-away.** Read a credit list as a claim. For a world-builder, name every role that made the world, including the ones the poster leaves out.

### 1.4 "Ballet is woman": the ballerina against the danseur

**The question.** Is ballet an art that centres and empowers women, or one that displays them for a male gaze? And why did the male dancer fade and return?

**Positions.**
- *The ballerina is ballet's centre and its agent.* George Balanchine, interviewed by John Gruen (*The Private World of Ballet*, 1976): "In ballet, however, woman is first" **[documented, quoted in Daly 1987]**. Sally Banes, *Dancing Women: Female Bodies Onstage* (1998), holds that dancing is itself agency: a role written as passive "may [be saturated] with agency" by the dancer's prowess **[documented, via Manning 2024]**.
- *Ballet displays women as objects of a male gaze.* Ann Daly, "The Balanchine Woman: Of Hummingbirds and Channel Swimmers", *TDR* 31.1 (1987), holds that "Balanchine glorified Woman because her Beauty pleased him, pleased the cavalier, and pleased the spectators' male gaze" **[documented]**. Gelsey Kirkland, *Dancing on My Grave* (1986), and Alice Robb, *Don't Think, Dear* (2023), describe control of dancers' bodies and lives **[probable; Robb documented via Spectator 2022]**.
- *The position moved.* Susan Manning, introducing her 1997 essay "The Female Dancer and the Male Gaze" in *Dancing on the Fault Lines of History* (2024, open access), says her own earlier dismissal of ballet "now seems a relic of 1980s feminist dance studies" **[documented]**.
- *On the man.* Lynn Garafola, "The Travesty Dancer in Nineteenth-Century Ballet", *Dance Research Journal* (1985 to 1986), holds that Paris in the 1840s to 1870s pushed men off the stage and cast women as men (Franz in *Coppélia*, 1870, was danced by Eugénie Fiocre) **[probable]**. Ramsay Burt, *The Male Dancer* (1995), holds that homophobia shaped how the returning male dancer was made to look "manly" **[probable]**. Peter Stoneley, *A Queer History of the Ballet* (2007), reads "Ballet is Woman" as Balanchine's answer to charges that ballet was effeminate **[documented]**.

**Carriers.** sylphide-1832, giselle-1841, coppelia-1870, sleeping-beauty-1890, spectre-de-la-rose-1911, apollon-musagete-1928, serenade-1934, jewels-1967, spartacus-1968, swan-lake-bourne-1995. France, Russia, USA, USSR, Britain; 1832 to 1995.

**What the evidence can and cannot settle.** Cast lists, pay records and repertory counts can settle who danced, who was paid more and who choreographed (the Dance Data Project counts choreographers by gender today). The meaning of a lift or a solo for its dancer and its audience is interpretation.

**Take-away.** Two readings can both be true: the role, the dancer and the system around her are three different things. Look at each separately.

### 1.5 The body ideal

**The question.** Is ballet's ideal body a technical necessity or a cultural norm that excludes and harms?

**Positions.**
- *The ideal follows from the technique.* The selection systems of the Vaganova Academy and the School of American Ballet hold that turnout, proportion, instep and flexibility are preconditions for the vocabulary **[probable]**. The "Balanchine body" (long legs, small head, thin) was promoted as the American modern dancer's form (Stoneley 2007 summarises this) **[documented]**.
- *The ideal is a norm that excludes and harms.* Kirkland (1986) and later memoirs describe starvation and injury **[probable]**. Angela Pickard, *Ballet Body Narratives* (2015), holds that the ideal is learned and policed in training **[probable]**. Pap Ndiaye and Constance Rivière, report on diversity at the Paris Opéra (2021), ask the school to rethink its "stringent physical criteria" **[documented in scope 02]**. Brenda Dixon Gottschild, *The Black Dancing Body* (2003), holds that ideals of line and shape are racialised **[probable]**.
- *The ideal changes.* Romantic ballerinas were shorter and rounder, Soviet extensions were higher, and today's "six o'clock" extension would have looked vulgar in 1900 **[argued]**. Shiryaev's films of 1906 to 1909 show bravura that undercuts claims that old dancers were weaker (theartsdesk review, 2010) **[documented, secondary]**.

**Carriers.** sylphide-1832, swan-lake-1877-1895, dying-swan-1905, apollon-musagete-1928, jewels-1967, in-the-middle-1987, giselle-masilo-2017. France, Russia, USA, Germany, South Africa; 1832 to 2017.

**What the evidence can and cannot settle.** Photographs, films, school intake rules and medical studies settle how bodies have changed and what training costs. Whether a given ideal is "needed" for an art depends on what you want that art to be.

**Take-away.** A design system that names one body as default excludes every other. Ask who the system was measured on.

### 1.6 Orientalism and the exotic other

**The question.** When ballet stages "the East", "Africa" or "the Americas", is it a fantasy that serves power, a form of cultural exchange, or both? And should such roles be changed?

**Positions.**
- *It is Orientalism in Said's sense.* Edward Said, *Orientalism* (1978), holds that the West invents an East to know and rule it; dance scholars apply this to *La Bayadère*, *Le Corsaire* and *Schéhérazade* **[documented in W4]**. Hanna Järvinen, "Ballets Russes and Blackface", *Dance Research Journal* 52.3 (2020), holds that the Golden Slave and the Blackamoor are racist stereotypes that recur in revivals, exhibitions and pedagogy **[documented, abstract]**. Phil Chan with Michelle Chase, *Final Bow for Yellowface* (2020), and the pledge Chan founded with Georgina Pazcoguin (2017), hold that caricatures in *Nutcracker*'s "Tea" must go **[documented]**. Ndiaye and Rivière (2021) recommend ending "blackface, brownface and yellowface" at the Paris Opéra **[documented]**.
- *It is also intercultural sharing, or theatre that should be left as made.* Jennifer Fisher, "'Arabian Coffee' in the Land of the Sweets", *Dance Research Journal* (2003 to 2004), holds that the *Nutcracker* divertissements can be "intercultural dance sharing" **[documented, editors' summary in scope 01]**; critics answer that she ignores who borrows from whom **[documented in scope 01]**. In December 2019 the Bolshoi's director Vladimir Urin said the theatre "will not engage in such a discussion" of blackface in its *Bayadère* **[documented, RFE/RL in pass2_verify]**. Isabelle Fokine, rights holder for *Petrushka*, in 2020 proposed recasting the Moor as a steppe "Warrior", a change from inside the estate **[documented in pass2_verify]**.
- *Russia's Orient is its own case.* Richard Taruskin and others hold that Russian Orientalism also looked inward, to the empire's own Caucasus and Central Asia (*Fountain of Bakhchisarai*, *Shurale*, *Gayane*) **[probable]**.

**Carriers.** indes-galantes-1735, corsaire-1856, pharaohs-daughter-1862, bayadere-1877, nutcracker-1892, scheherazade-1910, petrushka-1911, fountain-bakhchisarai-1934, the-kabuki-1986, rama-hari-1980 and cleopatra-cairo-2019 (made from inside the culture depicted). France, Russia, USA, Philippines, Egypt; 1735 to 2019.

**What the evidence can and cannot settle.** Archives settle what was put on stage (make-up books, costume designs, photographs), who made it and whether the culture shown was consulted. They cannot settle intent, and they show the same image read as homage in one city and insult in another.

**Take-away.** Borrowing creates obligations. Pair every staged "other" with a work made by the people shown, and ask who was in the room.

### 1.7 Class, court and state

**The question.** Is ballet by nature an aristocratic art, or can it be a people's art? Who has used it, and for what?

**Positions.**
- *Ballet is aristocratic in its bones.* Jennifer Homans, *Apollo's Angels* (2010), holds that classical dance was "a vision and defense of nobility, not as a social class but as an aesthetic and way of life" **[documented, quoted in reviews, Gramilano 2010]**. Mark Franko, *Dance as Text: Ideologies of the Baroque Body* (1993), holds that court ballet was political action in costume **[probable]**. The *Ballet de la nuit* (1653) staged the young Louis XIV as the sun dispelling the Fronde's night (BnF Essentiels; Vexilla Galliae 2020) **[documented]**.
- *Ballet can be, and was made, a people's art.* After 1917 the Bolshoi and Mariinsky were kept open, re-audienced and given revolutionary subjects (*Red Poppy*, *Flames of Paris*) **[probable]**; Ezrahi (2012) holds that the Soviet state both used ballet and was resisted from inside it **[probable]**. Emily Wilcox, *Revolutionary Bodies: Chinese Dance and the Socialist Legacy* (2018), holds that China built ballet into revolutionary "model works" (*Red Detachment of Women*, 1964; *White-Haired Girl*, 1965) **[probable]**. Cuba made Alicia Alonso's company a national institution after 1959 (Lester Tomé, in W3) **[probable]**.
- *Ballet serves whoever pays, including markets and the Cold War.* Naima Prevots, *Dance for Export* (1998), holds that US tours were cultural diplomacy **[probable]**; Soviet tours did the same in reverse **[probable]**.

**Carriers.** balet-comique-1581, ballet-de-la-nuit-1653, sleeping-beauty-1890, red-poppy-1927, flames-of-paris-1932, bright-stream-1935, red-detachment-1964, white-haired-girl-shanghai-1965, spartacus-1968, giselle-alonso-1945-1963, raise-the-red-lantern-2001. France, Russia, USSR, China, Cuba; 1581 to 2001.

**What the evidence can and cannot settle.** Budgets, ticket prices, audience surveys and edicts settle who paid and who attended. They do not settle whether the form itself carries class, or only its institutions.

**Take-away.** The same vocabulary has served a king, a commissar and a box office. Separate the form from the patron before judging either.

### 1.8 Nation and folk

**The question.** When ballet uses "folk" dance to make a national style, is it collecting the people's dance or inventing a nation for the stage?

**Positions.**
- *National ballet draws on real folk sources.* Bournonville's *Napoli* (1842) is built from what he saw in Naples **[probable]**; the Ballets Russes' Russian works drew on Roerich's ethnography and Stravinsky's folk sources, which Richard Taruskin, *Stravinsky and the Russian Traditions* (1996), calls "neonationalism" **[documented in W4]**. Soviet republics' "national ballets" (Baku 1940, Kazan 1945) were built with folk informants **[probable]**.
- *National folk on stage is a constructed tradition.* Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger, eds, *The Invention of Tradition* (1983), hold that national traditions are often recent and made **[documented, title; argument probable]**. Anthony Shay, *Choreographing Politics: State Folk Dance Companies, Representation and Power* (2002), holds that state ensembles stage an idealised peasant for political ends **[probable]**.
- *Ballet is itself a folk dance.* Joann Kealiinohomoku, "An Anthropologist Looks at Ballet as a Form of Ethnic Dance" (1970), holds that ballet is the ethnic dance of a particular Western culture **[documented, cited in scope 06]**.

**Carriers.** napoli-1842, don-quixote-1869, coppelia-1870, firebird-1910, les-noces-1923, laurencia-1939, maiden-tower-1940, gayane-1942, shurale-1950, estancia-1952, fish-beauty-1959, cesmebasi-1965, rama-hari-1980, shim-chung-1986. Denmark, Russia, USSR, Argentina, China, Turkey, Philippines, Korea; 1842 to 1986.

**What the evidence can and cannot settle.** Field notes, informant names and early films can show what was taken and changed. They cannot settle whether a staged nation is "authentic", because all staging selects.

**Take-away.** A national style is a design brief. Credit the sources by name and say what was changed for the stage.

### 1.9 Race and access

**The question.** Is ballet open to every body that trains, or does its institution keep some people out?

**Positions.**
- *The technique is universal; the doors were the problem.* Arthur Mitchell, the earliest known Black principal at New York City Ballet, founded Dance Theatre of Harlem with Karel Shook in 1969 to show that Black dancers could excel in classical ballet **[probable]**. Misty Copeland, *Life in Motion* (2014), and her promotion as American Ballet Theatre's first Black woman principal (30 June 2015) argue the same through a career **[probable]**.
- *Exclusion is built into the look of the form.* Ndiaye and Rivière (2021) hold that the corps' uniformity and "le ballet blanc" have meant whiteness, and ask to "stop whitening skins" **[documented in scope 01]**. The pink tights and shoes called "flesh" were a designed default; brown and bronze pointe shoes reached the market only with Gaynor Minden (2017) and Freed with Ballet Black (2018) **[documented in scope 03]**. Dance critic John Martin's claim in 1963 that Black dancers were anatomically unsuited is the record of a prejudice, refuted **[documented in scope 01]**.
- *Ballet is already partly African American.* Brenda Dixon Gottschild, *Digging the Africanist Presence in American Performance* (1996), holds that Balanchine's American style absorbed Africanist elements (off-centre hips, syncopation) **[probable]**.

**Carriers.** agon-1957 (Mitchell and Diana Adams), revelations-1960 (modern dance, boundary case), giselle-alonso-1945-1963, creole-giselle-1984, swan-lake-masilo-2010, giselle-masilo-2017, flight-pattern-2017, common-grounds-acogny-2021. USA, Cuba, South Africa, Senegal and Germany; 1957 to 2021.

**What the evidence can and cannot settle.** Rosters, school admissions, product catalogues and contracts settle who was hired and what was sold. They do not settle what a "classical" look must be.

**Take-away.** Defaults are design. Changing the colour of a shoe changed who could look "finished" on stage.

### 1.10 Queer readings

**The question.** Has ballet been a queer space all along, and can its classics be read, or remade, as queer?

**Positions.**
- *Ballet has a queer history.* Peter Stoneley, *A Queer History of the Ballet* (2007), and Kevin Kopelson, *The Queer Afterlife of Vaslav Nijinsky* (1997), hold that ballet's audiences, patrons and makers (Diaghilev's circle above all) formed a queer culture **[documented, Stoneley; Kopelson probable]**.
- *The classical structure is heteronormative.* The pas de deux of man and woman, partnering roles and casting rules encode one kind of couple (Daly 1987; Burt 1995) **[probable]**.
- *Remaking reveals, reading-in distorts.* Matthew Bourne's male swans (1995), Mats Ek's *Swan Lake* (1987), Mark Morris's *Hard Nut* (1991) and Dada Masilo's *Swan Lake* (2010) remake the classics with queer desire visible **[documented in W3]**. Against this, a critic or maker may hold that calling a work "the gay *Swan Lake*" narrows it; Bourne has been reported as resisting the label **[probable, not verified this pass]**.

**Carriers.** spectre-de-la-rose-1911, apres-midi-faune-1912, les-biches-1924, bright-stream-1935 (travesty comedy), bolero-bejart-1961, swan-lake-ek-1987, hard-nut-1991, swan-lake-bourne-1995, swan-lake-masilo-2010. France, USSR, Belgium, Sweden, USA, Britain, South Africa; 1911 to 2010.

**What the evidence can and cannot settle.** Letters, memoirs and casting can show the lives and the desires around a work. They cannot fix a single meaning for a dance on stage.

**Take-away.** A canon can be re-read by who is allowed to desire whom. That is a world-building lever.

### 1.11 Is ballet dying, or changing?

**The question.** Is classical ballet in terminal decline, or in one of its periodic renewals?

**Positions.**
- *Ballet is dying.* Jennifer Homans, *Apollo's Angels* (2010), epilogue "The Masters Are Dead and Gone": "After years of trying to convince myself otherwise, I now feel sure that ballet is dying" **[documented, quoted Dance Informa 2011]**. Robert Gottlieb, *New York Review of Books* (9 December 2010), thinks the odds are that she is right **[documented]**.
- *Ballet is changing, as it always has.* Claudia La Rocco, *Slate* (15 November 2010): "I've never been able to wrap my head around the critical impulse to proclaim an art form kaput" **[documented]**. Reviewers noted that Homans herself narrates repeated deaths and revivals (Ballet Alert forum, 2010; Rogoff and Horwell in scope 01) **[documented]**. Alastair Macaulay replied in the *New York Times*, 5 January 2011 **[documented as existing, not read]**. Homans later said: "I hope I am proven wrong" (interview quoted on Ballet Alert, 2010) **[documented, secondary]**.

**Carriers.** sylvia-1876 and excelsior-1881 (the "decline" of Paris and the rise of Italian spectacle), parade-1917 (ballet as avant-garde), push-comes-to-shove-1976, in-the-middle-1987, alice-wheeldon-2011, woolf-works-2015, giselle-khan-2016, flight-pattern-2017, like-water-for-chocolate-2022. France, Italy, USA, Germany, Britain, Canada; 1876 to 2022.

**What the evidence can and cannot settle.** Attendance, the number of new full-length works and their revival rates (Bachtrack's annual statistics) can be counted. "Dying" is a judgement about quality and meaning that numbers do not settle.

**Take-away.** The elegy is a genre of ballet writing. Read it as a position in a debate, written by an insider, not as a fact.

### 1.12 Popularity against prestige: the economics of *Nutcracker*

**The question.** Does a popular hit pay for art, or crowd it out?

**Positions.**
- *The popular repertory funds the rest.* Dance/USA's survey for 1991 found *Nutcracker* averaging 40% of annual income for companies that performed it (*Los Angeles Times*, 6 December 1992) **[documented]**; its 2017 survey found holiday revenue averaging 48% of season revenue (median 55%) and about 15% of total revenue **[documented, Dance/USA 2018]**. New York City Ballet's executive director said its run brings "about 45% of our total ticket revenue" (NPR, 22 December 2025) **[documented]**. Boston Ballet's Mikko Nissinen: "*Nutcracker* is the only production that produces any revenue" (*Dance Magazine*, 2020) **[documented]**.
- *Dependence is a risk and a creative cost.* Dance/USA's own 2018 summary warns about "dependence"; *Dance Magazine* (2018) hoped for "similar enthusiasm for non-*Nutcracker* performances" **[documented]**. COVID-19 in 2020 showed the risk when runs were cancelled **[documented, Dance Magazine 2020]**.
- *Popularity is a ritual worth studying.* Jennifer Fisher, *Nutcracker Nation* (2003), holds that local *Nutcrackers* are community rituals that adapt to place **[probable]**.

**Carriers.** excelsior-1881 (a mass spectacle at La Scala), nutcracker-1892, cinderella-zakharov-1945, fille-mal-gardee-ashton-1960, hard-nut-1991, swan-lake-bourne-1995, like-water-for-chocolate-2022. Italy, Russia, USSR, Britain, USA; 1881 to 2022.

**What the evidence can and cannot settle.** Survey data settle the money (as company-reported figures). They do not settle whether the money buys risk elsewhere or just more *Nutcracker*.

**Take-away.** Every world needs a hit that pays for its experiments. The question is whether the hit is allowed to change.

### 1.13 The white act and its meanings

**The question.** What does the corps of identical women in white mean: the Romantic ideal, a fantasy of dead women, a picture of whiteness, or industrial discipline?

**Positions.**
- *It is the Romantic ideal of the spirit.* Gautier and the Romantic critics held that the sylph and the Wili were poetry made visible **[probable]**. Marian Smith (2000) traces the white act to opera, beginning with the dead nuns in *Robert le diable* (1831) **[probable]**.
- *It is a male fantasy of the unattainable or dead woman; or a community of women with power.* Banes (1998) holds that the Wilis form a female community with real power over men **[documented, Banes table of contents and abstract]**.
- *It is a picture of whiteness.* Ndiaye and Rivière (2021) devote a section to "le ballet blanc" **[documented in scope 01]**; Dance Theatre of Harlem's *Creole Giselle* (1984), Akram Khan's *Giselle* (2016) and Dada Masilo's *Giselle* (2017) remake who is allowed to be the corps **[documented in W3]**.
- *It is a technology of uniformity.* Gas light (Paris Opéra from 1822), white gauze and identical bodies produce one image from many workers **[argued]**.

**Carriers.** robert-le-diable-nuns-1831, sylphide-1832, giselle-1841, bayadere-1877, swan-lake-1877-1895, les-sylphides-1909, serenade-1934, creole-giselle-1984, giselle-khan-2016, giselle-masilo-2017. France, Russia, USA, Britain, South Africa; 1831 to 2017.

**What the evidence can and cannot settle.** Designs, lighting records and casting settle what the image was made of. Its meaning is plural, and the readings are not mutually exclusive.

**Take-away.** A uniform group image is one of the strongest tools in a designer's kit. Ask who it includes by sameness and who it leaves out.

### 1.14 Tradition against innovation in the classroom

**The question.** Should ballet be taught by a fixed syllabus that preserves a style, or by teachers who change the method to serve new choreography and new bodies?

**Positions.**
- *A codified syllabus preserves style and safety.* The Cecchetti method (Cyril Beaumont and Stanislas Idzikowski's manual, 1922; Cecchetti Society, 1922), the Association of Operatic Dancing (1920; Royal Academy of Dance from 1936), Agrippina Vaganova's *Basic Principles of Classical Ballet* (1934) and the Bournonville schools (set by weekday, compiled after his death) hold that a graded, repeatable method keeps a lineage alive **[probable]**.
- *The method must serve the choreography.* Balanchine's teaching at the School of American Ballet (from 1934) changed speed, the fifth position and the arms to fit his ballets (Suki Schorer, *Suki Schorer on Balanchine Technique*, 1999; Merrill Ashley, *Dancing for Balanchine*, 1984) **[probable]**. William Forsythe's *Improvisation Technologies* (CD-ROM, 1999) teaches ballet as a system to be extended **[probable]**.
- *Global syllabi erase national styles.* Homans (2010) laments the loss of distinct national styles (Laura Jacobs's summary, *Wall Street Journal*, quoted Gramilano 2010) **[documented, secondary]**.

**Carriers.** napoli-1842 (Bournonville style), sleeping-beauty-1890 (Petipa and Cecchetti), les-sylphides-1909, four-temperaments-1946, symphony-in-c-1947, in-the-middle-1987. Denmark, Russia, Britain, USA, Germany; 1842 to 1987.

**What the evidence can and cannot settle.** Manuals, films of class and exam syllabi show how methods differ. Which method is "correct" depends on which repertory you want to dance.

**Take-away.** A teaching method is a design system for bodies. It encodes a repertory; changing one changes the other.

### 1.15 Ballet and modern dance: rivals or kin?

**The question.** Is modern dance ballet's opposite, or one branch of the same tree?

**Positions.**
- *Rivals.* Isadora Duncan and later modern dancers defined themselves against ballet's shoes, corsets and codes **[probable]**. Lincoln Kirstein, *Blast at Ballet* (1938), polemicised for an American ballet against both European tradition and its critics **[probable]**. The Soviet press attacked "formalism" in both **[probable]**.
- *Kin.* Graham and Balanchine shared *Episodes* (NYCB, 1959) **[probable]**; Twyla Tharp's *Push Comes to Shove* (1976) put a modern choreographer and Baryshnikov together at ABT **[documented in register]**; Forsythe's *In the Middle* (1987) and Bausch's *Rite* (1975) are performed by ballet companies **[documented in register]**.

**Carriers.** apres-midi-faune-1912, rite-of-spring-1913, green-table-1932, la-coronela-1940, revelations-1960, sacre-bausch-1975, push-comes-to-shove-1976, in-the-middle-1987. France, Germany, Mexico, USA; 1912 to 1987.

**What the evidence can and cannot settle.** Repertory lists show constant traffic between the two. The boundary is a matter of labels and institutions, not steps.

**Take-away.** "Ballet" and "modern" are brands as much as techniques. The register's boundary cases are kept on purpose.

### 1.16 Russia, the canon and the war

**The question.** When a state uses its classics as propaganda, should others stop performing them?

**Positions.**
- *Suspend the Russian repertory.* Ukraine's culture minister Oleksandr Tkachenko called in December 2022 for allies to halt Tchaikovsky and other Russian composers until the war ends, while saying this was not "cancelling Tchaikovsky" (*Guardian*, 7 December 2022) **[documented]**. The National Opera of Ukraine states that removing Russian composers from its repertory is "fundamental" (6 December 2022) **[documented]**; Ukrainian theatres replaced Tchaikovsky in *The Snow Queen* with Offenbach and Strauss (ZN.ua, 2023) **[documented]**.
- *Keep the works, refuse the state.* Alexei Ratmansky left Moscow in February 2022 mid-rehearsal and later said: "Pushkin, Chekhov, Tchaikovsky is great culture but it is being used as a propaganda tool" (*Guardian*, 25 August 2023); he still stages Tchaikovsky in the West **[documented]**. The Royal Opera House cancelled a Bolshoi residency (2022) while UK companies kept *Nutcracker* **[documented, Guardian 1 March 2022]**.
- *This is old.* Soviet and Western tours (Bolshoi in London 1956; NYCB in the USSR 1962) and the defections of Nureyev (1961), Makarova (1970) and Baryshnikov (1974) made ballet a Cold War front **[probable]**.

**Carriers.** swan-lake-1877-1895, sleeping-beauty-1890, nutcracker-1892, flames-of-paris-1932, spartacus-1968, sleeping-beauty-ratmansky-2015. Russia, USSR, Ukraine, Britain, USA; 1877 to 2022.

**What the evidence can and cannot settle.** Statements, repertory changes and contracts can be dated. Whether art "belongs" to a state is a political judgement.

**Take-away.** A classic's meaning depends on who performs it, for whom, and when.

### 1.17 Where did ballet begin, and where is its centre?

**The question.** Is ballet a French and Russian line with branches abroad, or an art with many centres?

**Positions.**
- *A line from Italy and France through Russia to New York.* Homans (2010) organises the history by national epicentres, France and Russia above all; reviewers note the global story is missing (Nicifero's Project MUSE review, 2012) **[documented in scope 01]**.
- *Italian before French.* Fifteenth-century Italian dancing masters (Domenico da Piacenza; Guglielmo Ebreo's treatise, translated by Barbara Sparti, 1993) and Catherine de' Medici's Italian circle predate the French court ballet; Mark Franko, *The Dancing Body in Renaissance Choreography* (1986) **[documented in scope 01]**.
- *Many centres.* Emily Wilcox (2018) for China, Lester Tomé for Cuba, Joann Kealiinohomoku (1970) for ballet as one ethnic dance, and UC Santa Cruz's course framing of a "ballet diaspora" hold that ballet is polycentric **[documented in scope 06]**.

**Carriers.** balet-comique-1581, napoli-1842, excelsior-1881, white-haired-girl-matsuyama-1955, fish-beauty-1959, cesmebasi-1965, rama-hari-1980, shim-chung-1986, cleopatra-cairo-2019. France, Denmark, Italy, Japan, China, Turkey, Philippines, Korea, Egypt; 1581 to 2019.

**What the evidence can and cannot settle.** Dates of institutions and works settle chronology. "Centre" is a choice of where to look from.

**Take-away.** A map of the canon is an artefact with an author. Redraw it from somewhere else and see what moves.

### 1.18 Live or recorded: is the filmed ballet the ballet?

**The question.** Is ballet only real in the theatre, or do film, television and streaming make or keep the work?

**Positions.**
- *Performance is defined by disappearance.* Peggy Phelan, *Unmarked: The Politics of Performance* (1993), holds that performance becomes itself through disappearing; a recording is something else **[probable]**.
- *Liveness is itself made by media.* Philip Auslander, *Liveness* (1999), holds that the "live" is defined against recording and shaped by it **[probable]**. The record supports this: Alexander Shiryaev filmed dances and made puppet animations of Mariinsky ballets in 1906 to 1909, after the theatre refused him permission to film its dancers; the films were used later in restaging the *Bayadère* Hindu dance (Bioscope 2008; Wikipedia summary of Yultyeva) **[documented, secondary]**.

**Carriers.** dying-swan-1905 (filmed by Pavlova), bayadere-1877 (Shiryaev's puppet film), swan-lake-1877-1895 (the 1991 broadcast), romeo-juliet-lavrovsky-1940 (1954 film), nutcracker-1892 (television), woolf-works-2015 (cinema relays). Russia, USSR, Britain, USA; 1905 to 2015.

**What the evidence can and cannot settle.** Films survive and can be dated. Whether a filmed ballet is "the work" is a philosophical question, but much of what we know of steps after 1900 comes from film.

**Take-away.** Plan the record as part of the work. Many ballets survive only because someone filmed them.

## Part 2. Myths and the record

Thirty-eight popular stories set against the evidence, in rough chronological order. Each gives the story as usually told, what the record shows, and a verdict on the story: **documented** (the story is true as told), **probable** (likely true, or true in part), **contested** (sources disagree; both readings given) or **argued** (the story is an interpretation, not a fact). Where the verdict is "false as told", the corrected statement carries its own confidence word.

1. **"Ballet began in France."** The record: the word and the art come from Italian *ballo* and *balletto*; fifteenth-century Italian masters (Domenico da Piacenza, Guglielmo Ebreo) wrote dance treatises before the French court spectacles; the *Balet comique de la Royne* (Paris, 15 October 1581) was devised by an Italian, Baldassare de Belgioioso (Balthasar de Beaujoyeulx). France codified and institutionalised ballet (1661, 1669, 1700). **Verdict: false as told; "codified in France from Italian roots" is documented** (scope 01; Franko 1986; Sparti 1993).

2. **"The *Balet comique de la Royne* (1581) was the first ballet."** The record: it is the earliest known court ballet with a unified dramatic action that was also published (1582 print, IMSLP); court dance spectacles came before it, such as the *Ballet des Polonais* (1573) **[probable]**. **Verdict: probable as "earliest known unified, printed court ballet"; false as "first ballet"** (G2; scope 01).

3. **"Catherine de' Medici brought ballet to France."** The record: Catherine patronised Valois court festivals and Italian artists, and the 1581 ballet was performed for a wedding in her circle; but the Valois spectacle tradition was French and Italian together, and she did not import a finished art. **Verdict: contested** (scope 01; Homans 2010 ch. 1).

4. **"Louis XIV got the name 'Sun King' from dancing the sun in the *Ballet de la Nuit* (1653)."** The record: he did dance the rising sun on 23 February 1653 (Benserade's verses survive). The sun device with the motto *Nec pluribus impar* was presented at the carrousel of 5 to 6 June 1662, which Louis himself named in his *Mémoires* as where he took up the device; BnF Essentiels says he "definitively" won the nickname there; a medal with the motto may date from 1658; Mazarin is said to have chosen the sun emblem after the Fronde (Sabatier 2017, cited in *CRCV* 2025); the emblem itself was older royal imagery. **Verdict: contested** (BnF Passerelles credits 1653; BnF Essentiels and histoire-image.org credit 1662; Swiss National Museum blog 2023 credits 1653).

5. **"Louis XIV founded the Paris Opéra Ballet in 1661."** The record: in 1661 he founded the Académie royale de danse, a body of dancing masters; the opera academy came in 1669 (Perrin) and passed to Lully in 1672; the Opéra's dancers trace their line to the opera institution, not to the 1661 academy. **Verdict: false as told; probable as corrected** (scope 01; Swiss National Museum blog conflates the two).

6. **"Beauchamps invented the five positions."** The record: they are attributed to Pierre Beauchamps by tradition and by the Feuillet dispute; the earliest printed description is in Feuillet's *Chorégraphie* (1700) and Rameau's *Le Maître à danser* (1725). **Verdict: probable as attribution; not documented as invention** (scope 06; Swiss National Museum blog 2023 "credited with").

7. **"Marie Camargo shortened the skirt and invented the heel-less slipper."** The record: Camargo (Paris Opéra, from 1726) is credited with raising her hem and dropping heels to show beaten steps (English National Ballet, 2024; Lancret's portraits c. 1730). Contemporary verification of exact changes is thin. **Verdict: probable.**

8. **"Marie Sallé danced *Pygmalion* in a muslin shift, without panniers (London, 1734)."** The record: the *Mercure de France* reported that she appeared "without panniers, without skirt", in a muslin robe draped like a Greek statue (quoted on Provence 7). **Verdict: documented, as a report** (pygmalion-salle-1734).

9. **"Noverre invented the story ballet."** The record: John Weaver's *The Loves of Mars and Venus* (Drury Lane, 1717) told a myth in dance and gesture and printed a scenario **[documented in G1]**; Hilverding and Angiolini worked on *ballet d'action* in Vienna at the same time as Noverre; Angiolini disputed Noverre's priority in print (1773). **Verdict: false as told; Noverre was its most influential theorist (documented)** (W4; G1).

10. **"Marie Taglioni was the first to dance on pointe."** The record: Amalia Brugnoli is named on pointe in 1822 or 1823 (English National Ballet gives 1823, *La Fée et le Chevalier*); Geneviève Gosselin is given as 1813 or 1815; Sandra Noll Hammond and Edward Fairfax argue that rising to the toes has earlier roots and was not only female. Taglioni made pointe expressive of the supernatural in *La Sylphide* (1832). **Verdict: false as told; "Taglioni made pointe poetic" is argued** (scope 02; ENB 2024).

11. **"Taglioni's admirers in St Petersburg cooked and ate her shoes."** The record: the story first appears in London's *Morning Post*, 3 January 1855, thirteen years after the 1842 tour it describes (ABC Classic, 2018). **Verdict: contested; treat as legend** (no contemporary Russian source found in this pass).

12. **"Taglioni designed the tutu, and the word comes from 1832."** The record: the bell-shaped white skirt for *La Sylphide* (1832) is credited to the painter Eugène Lami; the Paris Opéra itself says the tutu "first appeared during the 1830s" with this costume. The word *tutu* for a dancer's skirt is attested only in 1881 (Charles Nuitter, Opéra librarian, cited in Larchey's slang dictionary, 1883), from baby talk for "bottom" (*cucu*, *tutu*). **Verdict: costume probable (attribution to Lami secondary); word documented 1881** (CNRTL; Opéra de Paris 350 years; Britannica).

13. **"*Giselle* was invented by Gautier alone."** The record: Gautier told the story himself in an open letter to Heine (*La Presse*, 1841), compressing the timeline; the libretto was co-written with Vernoy de Saint-Georges, who built the plot; Adam wrote the music; Jean Coralli was credited with the choreography, but Jules Perrot made all Grisi's dances without credit. The Wilis came from Heine's *De l'Allemagne* (1835). **Verdict: false as told; the shared authorship is documented** (W1; Guest 2008).

14. **"Fanny Elssler was 'pagan' and Taglioni 'Christian'."** The record: the pairing is Gautier's critical formula of 1837 to 1838 **[probable]**, widely repeated as if it described the dancers rather than a critic's taste. **Verdict: argued (a critic's reading).**

15. **"Emma Livry burned to death on stage."** The record: on 15 November 1862, at a rehearsal of Auber's *La Muette de Portici*, her skirt caught a gas jet; she died eight months later, on 26 July 1863, at Neuilly. A decree of 27 November 1859 required fireproofing; she had signed a waiver refusing treated skirts because they yellowed and stiffened the gauze ("they are so ugly") **[probable; Musée d'Orsay documents the accident and death; the waiver via Clarín 2024 and fr.wikipedia]**. **Verdict: false as told (not on stage, not instantly); the corrected story is documented in outline.**

16. **"The Romantic ballerinas were frail and technically limited."** The record: Shiryaev's films (1906 to 1909) and accounts of Elssler and Grisi show strength and speed; the "frail" image is partly the roles and the lithographs. **Verdict: argued (against the story)** (theartsdesk 2010).

17. **"*Swan Lake* failed in 1877 because the music was too symphonic."** The record: some critics found the score "too noisy, too Wagnerian"; the sharpest criticism fell on Reisinger's choreography, the décor and the dancers (Laroche: he had "never seen a poorer presentation"); Karpakova replaced much of the score with interpolations. The ballet stayed in the Moscow repertory until 1883, with 33 or 41 performances in different counts; the *New York Times* (2015) calls it "by no means the fiasco that used to be supposed". **Verdict: contested; "failed" is too strong, "because of the music" is false as the main cause** (Petipa Society; BSO; NYT 2015; PBT guide).

18. **"Tchaikovsky was the first great composer to write a ballet."** The record: Lully, Rameau, Gluck (*Don Juan*, 1761), Beethoven (*Prometheus*, 1801), Adam and Delibes all came before. **Verdict: false.** What is new is the symphonic scale of his ballet scores **[argued]**.

19. **"Russian ballet was made by Russians."** The record: the Imperial school was founded by Jean-Baptiste Landé (1738); its great ballet masters were Didelot (Swiss-French), Perrot, Saint-Léon, and Petipa (born Marseille 1818, arrived 1847); its virtuosity was shaped by Italians (Legnani, Cecchetti). **Verdict: false as told; Russian dancers and audiences made it Russian (argued).**

20. **"Odette and Odile were always danced by one ballerina."** The record: Karpakova may have danced both in 1877, but the ballet is believed originally to have called for two dancers; the double role became standard with Legnani in 1895. **Verdict: contested** (Wikipedia summary of Wiley).

21. **"Petipa invented the 32 fouettés for *Swan Lake*."** The record: Pierina Legnani first performed them in St Petersburg in *Cinderella* (1893); Petipa then built them into the Act III coda for her (1895). **Verdict: false as told; documented as corrected** (PBT guide 2022).

22. **"Coppélia's Franz was always a man."** The record: at the 1870 Paris premiere Franz was danced *en travesti* by Eugénie Fiocre; the first Swanilda, Giuseppina Bozzacchi, aged 16, died on her 17th birthday, 23 November 1870, of smallpox and fever during the siege of Paris, after the Opéra closed on 31 August and stopped paying salaries **[probable, Wikipedia citing Treccani]**. **Verdict: false as told** (Garafola 1985).

23. **"*Nutcracker* was a hit in 1892."** The record: reviews of the premiere (18 December, Old Style 6 December 1892) were mixed, with complaints about the libretto and the children; its place as a Christmas institution is a mid-twentieth-century American creation: abridged Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo version 1940, full-length by Willam Christensen in San Francisco on 24 December 1944, Balanchine's in February 1954. **Verdict: false as told; the US chronology is documented** (Dance Data Project 2025; Dance Magazine 2020).

24. **"Ballet lost its edge in the West after 1870 and was saved by the Russians."** The record: Paris and London kept ballet alive in opera houses and music halls (the Alhambra and Empire, London, 1880s to 1910s) and Milan had *Excelsior* (1881); the Ballets Russes reframed it as art. **Verdict: argued** (Homans 2010 supports the decline story; G2 and G3 complicate it).

25. **"The Ballets Russes brought Russian ballet from Russia."** The record: Diaghilev's company never performed in Russia; it was a Paris-based touring company drawn from Imperial dancers on leave and later émigrés. **Verdict: probable as corrected** (W2).

26. **"Pavlova invented the modern pointe shoe."** The record: Pavlova widened the platform and added a leather insole to her shoes; the Bata Shoe Museum says she is "credited with dancing in the first modern pointe shoes", and that she had Salvatore Capezio make them on her US tours; she was criticised for "cheating". Italian makers had already hardened boxes. **Verdict: contested** (Bata Shoe Museum; ENB 2024).

27. **"Pavlova refused to dance *Firebird*."** The record: she disliked Stravinsky's score and left the Ballets Russes; Karsavina created the role in 1910 (Petipa Society). **Verdict: probable.**

28. **"Nijinsky could hang in the air."** The record: many witnesses said he seemed to pause (Rupert Brooke: he "stopped still in the air"); the classmate Bourman wrote that he seemed "to linger two or three seconds". Explanations: the *Spectre* window sill was low, he was caught behind the set by four men so no one saw the landing, and the conductor held the last chord; Nijinsky himself resented being praised as a leaper ("I am not a leaper; I am an artist", quoted LRB 2013). **Verdict: the impression is documented; the physics is illusion (probable)** (LRB 2000 and 2013; *Brain* 2018; Wikipedia summary).

29. **"The 1939 photograph shows Nijinsky's last leap, a sign of recovery."** The record: it was staged at the Swiss sanatorium by his wife Romola for the press while Serge Lifar danced; Nijinsky was gravely ill (Fallow Media essay). **Verdict: probable as staged publicity.**

30. **"*The Rite of Spring* caused a riot (Paris, 29 May 1913)."** The record: the hall was very loud, with whistling and shouting, and some reports describe blows and a challenge to a duel; Carl Van Vechten's "forty people expelled" comes from a man who attended the second performance (*Paris Review* 2013); Esteban Buch found the relevant police file missing (BBC 2013); the dancers took curtain calls and the evening went on; the word "riot" was first attached to the event in 1922 (Levitz, "Racism at *The Rite*", 2017, as reviewed in *MTO* 2018); the four later Paris shows and London were calm. **Verdict: contested; "uproar" is documented, "riot" is a later frame.**

31. **"Diaghilev told Cocteau 'Astonish me'."** The record: the line comes from Cocteau's own retelling **[probable]**. **Verdict: probable as Cocteau's account; not independently documented.**

32. **"Pavlova's last words were 'Get my Swan costume ready'."** The record: the line is reported by Victor Dandré, her partner and manager, in *Anna Pavlova: In Art and Life* (1932); she died at the Hôtel des Indes, The Hague, at 12.30 a.m. on 23 January 1931 of pleurisy (*New York Times* report, same day). No second witness's account was found. **Verdict: contested (single interested source).** The claim that she wished her ashes taken to Moscow has no evidence (Wikipedia summary).

33. **"The pavlova dessert was invented in Australia."** The record: a "pavlova" appears in New Zealand print in 1926 to 1927 (a Davis gelatine jelly) and meringue versions in New Zealand from 1928 to 1929; Bert Sachse's Perth claim is 1935. The OED (2010) and Helen Leach, *The Pavlova Story* (2008), favour New Zealand for the name; ABC (2024) gives Australia the modern cake form. **Verdict: contested** (BBC 2010; ABC 2024; National Portrait Gallery 2014).

34. **"Balanchine said 'Ballet is woman'."** The record: the exact three words are not found in a primary source in this pass; Gruen's 1976 interview has "In ballet, however, woman is first"; Jennifer Homans (NYU interview) calls the short form "probably apocryphal, but it certainly was his view". **Verdict: probable as paraphrase.**

35. **"Fonteyn and Nureyev received 89 curtain calls."** The record: Vienna State Opera, 15 October 1964, premiere of Nureyev's *Swan Lake*; Guinness records it as the most for a ballet (Nureyev Foundation; Guinness). **Verdict: documented (as a record held by Guinness and the Foundation).**

36. **"Soviet television played *Swan Lake* on a loop for three days in August 1991."** The record: the ballet (a 1983 Bolshoi film with Natalia Bessmertnova) had been scheduled; the head of state broadcasting, Leonid Kravchenko, said the schedule was approved on 5 August; Viktor Oskolkov, who ran the programme directorate, told *Komsomolskaya Pravda* (August 2026) that he put it on twice on 19 August to fill dead air; Janice Ross (2015) and many others describe a continuous loop. That the ballet was also shown when Brezhnev (1982), Andropov and Chernenko died is widely reported (Radio Svoboda 2016; Ross 2015). **Verdict: contested; "twice on 19 August" now has a named first-hand source.**

37. **"Copeland was the first Black principal in American ballet."** The record: she was American Ballet Theatre's first Black woman principal (30 June 2015) **[probable]**; Arthur Mitchell was a principal at New York City Ballet from 1962; Raven Wilkinson danced with the Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo from 1955; Lauren Anderson was a Houston Ballet principal from 1990. **Verdict: false as told; true for ABT women (probable).**

38. **"Ballet dancers are all thin because ballet requires it."** The record: body ideals changed by period and company (Romantic, Imperial, Balanchine); the "Balanchine body" was a choice promoted in New York (Stoneley 2007). **Verdict: argued (against the story); see debate 1.5.**

**Pattern across the myths [argued].** Most myths have one of four shapes: a "first" that hides earlier makers (items 1, 2, 9, 10, 18); a single author who hides a team (5, 13, 21); a dramatic night that hides a long run (17, 23, 30, 36); and a death or a body made into a symbol (15, 22, 28, 32). Teach the shapes, and learners can test new stories themselves.
