# X2: Design and art across the whole history of ballet

21 November 2026. Repertory pass X2 (see `BRIEF_REPERTORY.md`). Status: complete for this pass (21 November 2026); for the human check.

**What this file is.** A history of how ballet has looked, written for a design student who will build a world of their own. It does not repeat the scope files: `../scope/04_stage_design.md` already holds the scene-painter chain (Sapunov, Golov, Shervashidze, Polunin), the Parade curtain's conservation, the museum and market, the Thurrock workshop and Rosenthal; `../scope/03_objects_makers_materials.md` holds the shoe, the tutu's construction, Karinska's workshop and the makers' names; `../products/PRODUCTS_AND_COLLECTIONS.md` holds the fashion-house, jewellery, poster and company-identity record. Where those files carry a fact, this one points to them (marked "see 04", "see 03", "see P") and builds on it.

**Confidence words** (from the brief): **documented** (seen at source this pass, or in a named source), **probable** (secondary or working knowledge, not re-checked), **contested** (two readings given), **argued** (an interpretation, ours or a named author's). Short forms in tables: [D], [P], [C], [A]. Register ids are in `code`.

**Thesis offered to the unit [A].** Ballet has changed its look about once a generation, and each change came from one of three pressures: a new light source (candle, gas, electricity, projection, LED), a new kind of designer let in (court ornamentist, scene-painting workshop, easel painter, sculptor and architect, fashion house, video and light artist), or a new idea of the body (hooped, corseted, freed, geometric, bare). A maker can read any ballet's look by asking which of the three moved last.

## Part 1. Eras of the stage picture

Ten eras, court to now. Dates are working boundaries, not walls; each era's look survives into the next (the Romantic tutu is still on stage today).

### Era 1. Court spectacle and the machine stage (1581–c.1680)

- **Visual language.** Allegory made visible: gods, virtues, planets and continents as costumed figures, the king at the centre. A mixed spectacle of verse, song, dance and machines in a hall or a court theatre, often with the audience on three sides and the dancers performing geometric figures to be read from above [P; see W1 §1].
- **Technologies.** Movable perspective scenery (wings and shutters converging on a vanishing point); machines for flying gods and changing scenes; Giacomo Torelli's chariot-and-pole system, which changed all wings at once from below stage (Venice 1641, Paris c.1645) [D, see 04 §1.1]; candle and oil light, so most action happened in a lit forestage.
- **Materials and colour.** Silk, cloth of gold and silver, embroidery, plumes, masks; pasteboard and canvas for scenery. Gold and silver read best by candlelight, so metallic thread and spangles were functional, not only rich [A].
- **Designers.** Jacques Patin (*Balet comique*, 1581); Torelli; Henri de Gissey (costumes, *Noces de Pélée et de Thétis*, 1654; designs for the *Ballet de la Nuit*, 1653, whose Rising Sun drawing is catalogued under an unnamed "Maître du ballet de la Nuit") [D, see 04]; Carlo Vigarani.
- **Signature images.** Louis XIV as the Rising Sun, *Ballet royal de la Nuit* (1653; BnF Estampes; PD) `ballet-de-la-nuit-1653`; the engravings by Israël Silvestre after François Francart of Torelli's sets for *Les Noces de Thétis* (1654; BnF; PD) [D, see 04]; the printed livret of the *Balet comique* (Ballard, 1582) with its engraved hall plan [D, see 04].
- **What changed and why.** Dance was one element of a political spectacle. Because print was how these events reached people who were not there, the engraving became the design record, and the look of court ballet that survives is the look of its prints [A, see 04 §1.1].

### Era 2. The Opéra and the ornamental costume (c.1680–1760)

- **Visual language.** Symmetrical palace and garden sets; the costume as a stage version of court dress. Men wore the *tonnelet* (a stiffened short skirt) over a Roman-style cuirass, with plumed headdresses; women wore boned bodices and wide skirts, later over panniers. Character types (Turk, Chinese, shepherd, fury) were signalled by ornament laid on one shared silhouette [D: Britannica "Costume in Baroque opera and ballet"].
- **Technologies.** The Palais-Royal theatre's machinery inherited from Vigarani; from the 1720s the Italian *scena per angolo* (angled perspective) of Bibiena and, at the Paris Opéra, Servandoni, which replaced Bérain's symmetry with diagonals [D, see 04 log].
- **Materials and colour.** Heavy brocade and embroidery under Bérain, then lighter silks, appliqués and pastel satins in the 1720s (Claude Gillot's *Les Éléments*, 1721) [D: Britannica]; block printing and stencilling to imitate embroidery cheaply (a Paris Opéra pattern book of the 18th century; costumes "painted in silver fish-scale pattern" in a Warsaw inventory) [D: Wodarska-Ogidel, ICOM Costume 2025].
- **Designers.** Jean Bérain (1640–1711), Opéra decorator from 1680; Jean Bérain the Younger; Claude Gillot; Jean-Baptiste Martin (Opéra designer from 1748) [D: Britannica].
- **Signature images.** Bérain's hand-coloured etchings of costumes for Lully (the Sun in *Phaéton*, 1683; a triton for *Persée*, 1682), Musée Condé, Chantilly [D: Château de Chantilly on Google Arts & Culture; PD artworks, photos link-only]; Gillot's designs for *Les Éléments* (Gallica, PD) [P].
- **What changed and why.** The professional stage (the Académie royale de musique, 1669) took ballet from the courtiers, and design became an office: one *dessinateur* dressed every production, so the house had one look [D for the "dessinateur unique" system: CNCS *Habiller l'Opéra*, see 03 log]. Marie Sallé's muslin dress and loose hair for *Pygmalion* (London, 1734) is the earliest well-known protest against that look from a dancer's side [P; register `pygmalion-salle-1734`].

### Era 3. Reform and the costume of character (1760–1820)

- **Visual language.** Noverre's *ballet d'action* wanted costume to tell the story and to free the body. Boquet's designs kept the rococo silhouette but loosened it; after about 1770 his drawing style became freer [D: Grove via CESAR]. By the 1790s, neoclassical dress (high waist, light muslin, flesh-coloured tights) arrived with the Revolution's taste for antiquity [P].
- **Technologies.** Wires and flying for Didelot's *Flore et Zéphire* (London, 1796), where dancers were lifted on wires so that Zephyr seemed to fly [P; register `flore-et-zephire-1796`]; the Argand oil lamp (1780s) brightened the stage [P].
- **Materials and colour.** Silk and printed linen; pale, "pastel" palettes; muslin and tricot (knit) for the body [P].
- **Designers.** Louis-René Boquet (1717–1814), *dessinateur des habits* to the court from 1764 or 1765 and the Opéra's costume designer from 1758–59 [D: CESAR, citing Jordan 2005 and Grove]. His designs travelled with Noverre to Stuttgart, Vienna, Warsaw, Stockholm and London [D: CESAR].
- **Signature images.** Noverre's two-volume gift to Gustav III of Sweden (1791), with 147 Boquet costume sketches for 19 ballets, National Library of Sweden, digitised by the Library of Congress / World Digital Library [D: LoC item 2021668154; PD]; Boquet's Medea for *Médée et Jason* (1763), Warsaw University Library [D: Wodarska-Ogidel 2025] `medee-et-jason-1763`.
- **What changed and why.** For the first time a choreographer wrote about costume as an obstacle (Noverre, *Lettres*, 1760) [D: Collins & Jarvis 2016]. The argument that costume should serve the dancing, not the court, begins here and runs to the leotard [A]. Petra Dotlačilová's thesis (Stockholm, 2020) is the best recent study of how reform was actually made in the workshop [D: DiVA record].

### Era 4. The Romantic stage: gas, gauze and the white act (1820–1850)

- **Visual language.** Moonlit ruins, Scottish glens, German forests; a real world in Act I and a spirit world in Act II (the *ballet blanc*). The spirits are women in white, so the scene is a contrast of a painted, coloured, daytime world with a pale, misty one [P; see W1].
- **Technologies.** Gaslight at the Paris Opéra from *Aladin* (1822), which could be dimmed from a central valve so the auditorium could be darkened [D, see 04 §1.2]; gauzes that become transparent when lit from behind; traps and wires; and the electric arc for single effects, such as a sunbeam or sunrise [C on the date: Britannica and Luminaris both give 1846 for the Opéra's earliest arc effect, but the Duboscq account Luminaris quotes ties the debut to *Le Prophète*, which premiered in 1849].
- **Materials and colour.** White cotton tarlatan and gauze for the bell-shaped skirt (later called the Romantic tutu); pale pink silk tights; painted canvas in deep blue-greens for night [P; see 03 §1.4].
- **Designers.** Pierre-Luc-Charles Ciceri (sets, the Opéra's chief from 1815 or 1818 [C]); Eugène Lami (costume of *La Sylphide*, 1832 [C, attribution debated]); Paul Lormier (*Giselle* costumes, 1841); Louis Daguerre (with Ciceri on *Aladin*) [D, see 04].
- **Signature images.** Ciceri's watercolour of the cloister for the nuns' ballet in *Robert le diable* (1831, Gallica) `robert-le-diable-nuns-1831`; the lithographs of Taglioni as the Sylphide (Chalon, Lane, 1830s; PD, many copies at V&A and NYPL) `sylphide-1832`; the Wilis' white act of *Giselle* (1841) `giselle-1841`.
- **What changed and why.** Light, not paint, made the second world. Because gas could be dimmed and directed, the stage could be dark and the dancers could glow, and that made the white act possible [A, see 04 concept 7]. The cost was fire: dancers' gauze skirts caught on gas jets, and Emma Livry died of burns after her costume caught fire at an Opéra rehearsal in 1862; she had refused a fire-retardant treatment that stiffened the fabric [P].

### Era 5. The grand ballet and the Imperial workshops (1850–1905)

- **Visual language.** Very large narrative spectacles with many scenes, processions and apotheoses: Egypt (*La Fille du Pharaon*, 1862), India (*La Bayadère*, 1877), a Perrault fairy court (*The Sleeping Beauty*, 1890). Archaeology and costume history were used as sources; Ponomarev, for example, was described in 2007 as an "ethnographer-bibliophile" who studied Greek and Moorish dress for *Le Corsaire* and used only three colours, red, blue and white, in many shades [D: Vremya novostei, 22 Aug 2007, quoting Bolshoi restorer Yelena Zaitseva].
- **Technologies.** Gas, then electricity: incandescent light reached the Paris Opéra in about 1880–81 [D: Britannica says "within one year" of Edison's 1879 lamp; Luminaris records the Opéra lit electrically during the 1881 Exposition], with fully electrified theatres in Munich, Stuttgart and Vienna by 1883 [D: Britannica]; panoramic moving cloths; transformation scenes; the hooped "plate" tutu shortening towards the 1890s [P, see 03].
- **Materials and colour.** Machine net; silk; metallic braid and paste jewels; stencilled and hand-painted fabrics [P; Mariinsky workshop account, see 03 log].
- **Designers.** In Paris: Charles Cambon, Édouard Desplechin, Antoine Lavastre (sets), Alfred Albert (costumes) for *Coppélia* (1870) [register `coppelia-1870`]. In St Petersburg the scenery was divided by act among workshop painters (Levogt, Andreyev, Bocharov, Shishkov for *Sleeping Beauty*) and costumes were drawn by Ivan Vsevolozhsky, the director of the Imperial Theatres, and by Evgeny Ponomarev (1852–1906), head of the costume department from 1887 [D: SPb Theatre Library calendar; Mariinsky credits *La Bayadère* costumes to Ponomarev].
- **Signature images.** Vsevolozhsky's costume designs for *Sleeping Beauty* (1890; St Petersburg State Theatre Library and Theatre Museum; PD, licence of reproductions unclear) `sleeping-beauty-1890`; the 1895 *Swan Lake* lakeside sets (Bocharov, Ivanov) `swan-lake-1877-1895`.
- **What changed and why.** Ballet in Russia became a state industry with its own departments, and design was made by committee. Peter Koppers argues that for *Sleeping Beauty* Ponomarev "more or less traced" Vsevolozhsky's designs [D: Koppers, *Essays on the Imperial Ballet*, 2025; argued]. Konstantin Korovin's arrival in Moscow (1899) and his work with Alexander Gorsky on *Don Quixote* (1900, with Golovin) put a single painter in charge of a production's colour; Korovin also proposed replacing corseted costumes with looser tunics [D: Churakova, Tretyakov Gallery Magazine 2012]. The cool reception of that *Don Quixote* (the designers were not called out to take applause) shows how new the idea was [D: Koppers 2025].

### Era 6. The painter's stage: the Ballets Russes and its circle (1909–1929)

- **Visual language.** The ballet as a painting set in motion, with one easel painter designing set, costume and often curtain. Three looks in twenty years: (a) Russian decorative and Orientalist colour (Bakst's emerald, orange and violet; Benois's 18th-century and fairground Petersburg; Roerich's prehistoric Russia; Golovin's and Goncharova's folk ornament), 1909–14; (b) the Paris avant-garde (Cubism in *Parade*, 1917; Fauve and decorative Matisse, 1920), 1917–24; (c) Constructivism, Surrealism and neo-Romanticism (*La Chatte* and *Le Pas d'acier*, 1927; *Ode*, 1928; Rouault, de Chirico), 1925–29 [P; see W2].
- **Technologies.** Mostly painted cloths (the continental method of painting on the floor in distemper) [D, see 04]; electric light coloured with gels; projection in *Ode* (1928) [D, see 04]; new materials such as celluloid, oilcloth and steel (*La Chatte*) [D, see 04].
- **Materials and colour.** Stencilled and painted silk and cotton rather than embroidery, because paint was quicker and read the same from a distance [D: NGA Teacher's Notes 1999, see 03]; colour as shock (Bakst's *Schéhérazade* greens and pinks, 1910).
- **Designers.** Bakst, Benois, Roerich, Golovin, Korovin, Anisfeld, Sudeikin, Goncharova, Larionov, Picasso, Matisse, Derain, Braque, Laurencin, Gris, Miró and Ernst, Gabo and Pevsner, Yakulov, Tchelitchew, de Chirico, Rouault, Chanel [D for those in register; see Part 2].
- **Signature images.** Bakst's costume designs for *Schéhérazade* (1910) and *L'Après-midi d'un faune* (1912) (Met, Harvard Theatre Collection, Wadsworth Atheneum; Bakst d. 1924, PD) `scheherazade-1910` `apres-midi-faune-1912`; Roerich's costume for two Clowns in *The Rite of Spring* (1913; Harvard Theatre Collection, MS Thr 414.4 (119)) `rite-of-spring-1913`; Picasso's *Parade* curtain (1917; Centre Pompidou; © Succession Picasso, link-only) `parade-1917`.
- **What changed and why.** A private company needed a Paris audience and a press. Painters gave it news value, and the look became the brand [A, Garafola 1989]. The painter replaced the house workshop as the author of the picture, though the workshop did not disappear (see 04 §1.4).

### Era 7. Revolution and the state stage: Soviet design (1917–1990)

- **Visual language.** Two lines. First, an avant-garde decade: Constructivist settings and machine imagery (*The Red Poppy*, 1927, Kurilko; *The Golden Age*, 1930, Khodasevich; *The Bolt*, 1931, Bruni and Korshikov) [register]. Second, from the 1930s, socialist realism's "drambalet": historically detailed, monumental sets for *The Flames of Paris* (1932, Dmitriev), *The Fountain of Bakhchisarai* (1934, Khodasevich) and *Romeo and Juliet* (1940, Pyotr Williams) [register]. From 1957 Simon Virsaladze's partnership with Yuri Grigorovich replaced heavy realism with a single unit set, a few hanging cloths and costumes built on colour fields (*The Stone Flower*, 1957; *Legend of Love*, 1961; *Spartacus*, 1968) [D for the partnership, see 04; P for the description].
- **Technologies.** State workshops (the Mariinsky's Golovin Hall for painting cloths, 12 × 20 m, two at a time) [P, see 03 log]; large stage machinery in the rebuilt Bolshoi; projection in the 1920s avant-garde [P].
- **Materials and colour.** Red as a political colour (*The Red Poppy*, *Spartacus*); synthetics in later decades [P].
- **Designers.** Mikhail Kurilko, Valentina Khodasevich, Tatiana Bruni, Vladimir Dmitriev, Pyotr Williams, Simon Virsaladze, Boris Messerer (*Carmen-Suite*, 1967, the bullring as a wicker-backed arena), Valery Levental [register].
- **Signature images.** Virsaladze's designs for *Spartacus* (1968; Bakhrushin Museum; © heirs, link-only) `spartacus-1968`; Messerer's *Carmen-Suite* ring `carmen-suite-1967`.
- **What changed and why.** The designer worked inside a state theatre and answered to cultural politics. Elena Zaitseva, the Bolshoi's costume restorer, called Virsaladze "a genius of optical deception" in contrast to Ponomarev's literalness [D: Vremya novostei 2007, quoted]. Western teaching barely covers this era (see 04 §6).

### Era 8. The bare stage, the sculptor and the light designer: America and the post-war West (1934–1975)

- **Visual language.** Three options coexisted. (a) The practice-clothes ballet: leotards and tights on an empty stage lit in blue (*The Four Temperaments* from 1951; *Agon*, 1957) [D, see 04 §2.5] `four-temperaments-1946` `agon-1957`. (b) The sculptor's set: Noguchi's *Orpheus* (1948), with its lyre, rocks and veil (not in the register). (c) The designer's narrative ballet, which in Britain and Germany became a picture of history (Messel's *Sleeping Beauty*, 1946; Georgiadis's Renaissance Verona for MacMillan, 1965; Jürgen Rose's *Onegin*, 1965) [D, see below and 04].
- **Technologies.** Lighting as a credited art (Jean Rosenthal at Ballet Society and NYCB from 1946–48) [D, see 04]; the cyclorama (a curved backcloth evenly lit to look like infinite sky), descended from Fortuny's dome of c.1902 [D: Britannica]; nylon and stretch fabrics; Karinska's hoopless "powder puff" tutu (1950) [D, see 03].
- **Materials and colour.** Black and white leotards; nylon net; the colour carried by light on a pale cyc [A].
- **Designers.** Noguchi, Karinska, Rosenthal, Eugene Berman, Pavel Tchelitchew, Oliver Messel, Sophie Fedorovitch, Osbert Lancaster, Nicholas Georgiadis, Yolanda Sonnabend, Jürgen Rose; in modern dance, Rauschenberg, Johns, Warhol for Cunningham [D, Part 2].
- **Signature images.** Noguchi's *Orpheus* lyre (1948; NYCB emblem; © INFGM/ARS, link-only); *Agon*'s black and white practice clothes (photographs by Martha Swope, NYPL; © link-only) `agon-1957`.
- **What changed and why.** Balanchine's company had little money and a theory: the music was the scenery. The bare stage was a cost decision and an aesthetic one at once (see 04 concept 8; Part 4 below). In Britain, a national company needed a look the state and the audience could recognise as "the Royal Ballet", and designers supplied it [A].

### Era 9. Dance theatre, the found object and the fashion house (1975–2005)

- **Visual language.** The stage as a real place covered in a real material: Rolf Borzik's peat for Bausch's *Rite of Spring* (1975), ankle-deep water for *Arien* (1979), fallen leaves for *Bluebeard* (1977) [D: Pina Bausch Foundation, Borzik biography]; Peter Pabst's 8,000 pink carnations for *Nelken* (1982), trampled through the performance [D: Pabst, "Peter on Pina", 2020]. In ballet proper: Forsythe designing his own spaces and light at Frankfurt (*In the Middle, Somewhat Elevated*, 1987, with golden cherries hanging in the flies) [P; register `in-the-middle-1987`]; re-readings of the classics in a modern room (Mats Ek with Marie-Louise Ekman, *Giselle* 1982, *Swan Lake* 1987) [register].
- **Technologies.** Computer lighting consoles (the Palais Garnier installed a 500-circuit console in 1971) and moving lights and colour changers from the 1980s [D: Opéra national du Rhin, *Les lumières à l'opéra* (teaching dossier)]; the sprung, portable floor (Harlequin, 1977) [D, see 03].
- **Materials and colour.** Organic and found matter (earth, water, flowers); street clothes and evening dress; designer fashion (Gaultier for Régine Chopinot from 1983, *Le Défilé* 1985; Miyake for Forsythe, 1991) [D: FIT *Dance & Fashion* 2014; see 04].
- **Designers.** Rolf Borzik, Peter Pabst, Marion Cito, Marie-Louise Ekman, William Forsythe, Jean Paul Gaultier, Miyake Issey, Lez Brotherston (Bourne's *Swan Lake*, 1995) `swan-lake-bourne-1995`.
- **Signature images.** The peat-covered stage of Bausch's *Rite* `sacre-bausch-1975`; the carnation field of *Nelken* (photographs © Pina Bausch Foundation / photographers, link-only).
- **What changed and why.** The floor became the set. Because the dancers' bodies marked the material (peat on sweat, crushed carnations), the design recorded the performance as it went [D: Borzik biography says "the dancers often carry traces of the sets' materials"; A for the reading].

### Era 10. Screen, LED and the couture commission (2005–now)

- **Visual language.** Clean architectural voids lit to change their depth (John Pawson's set for McGregor's *Chroma*, 2006, in which "the back plane can be made to appear nearer or further" through light alone) [D: John Pawson works page]; projected and filmed doubles (Puissant and the Ballet Boyz for Wheeldon's *Electric Counterpoint*, 2008) [D: Puissant project page]; theatrical illusion returned for new narrative ballets (Crowley's *Alice*, 2011, with projections by Jon Driscoll and Gemma Carrington; *The Winter's Tale*, 2014, with silk effects by the puppeteer Basil Twist and projections by Daniel Brodie) [D: ROH press release 2024; programme 2014]; couture commissions at galas (NYCB Fall Fashion Gala from 2012) [D, see P].
- **Technologies.** LED fixtures (used for small effects in opera houses from the 2000s, then whole rigs) [D: Opéra national du Rhin dossier]; video projection and projection mapping; 3D-printed and laser-cut costume parts (van Herpen) [P]; streaming and cinema relays, which made the close-up a design condition [A, see P].
- **Materials and colour.** Polyester, Lycra, PVC and rubber-like composites (van Herpen's *Neverwhere*, 2013, which crackled audibly on stage) [D: Fashion Week Daily interview with Marc Happel 2019; Dance Magazine 2013]; recycled and archive-derived palettes [P].
- **Designers.** John Pawson, Gareth Pugh, Iris van Herpen, Christian Lacroix, Karl Lagerfeld, Bob Crowley, Jean-Marc Puissant, Tim Yip, Es Devlin (in dance mainly for Rambert, Northern Ballet and Cullberg) [D: Design Week 2007], Lucy Carter (light) [Part 2].
- **Signature images.** *Chroma*'s white box with its rectangular opening (photographs © ROH / Johan Persson, link-only) ; Tim Yip's concrete wall in Akram Khan's *Giselle* (2016), which the dancers push against as the curtain rises [D: ENB page; New Yorker 2019] `giselle-khan-2016`.
- **What changed and why.** Two pulls at once. Money and audience: the gala and the couture name bring sponsors and press, and the cinema screen shows a costume's surface in close-up [A]. Technology: projection and LED make scenery a light event that can be changed every night and toured in a truck [A]. Against both, the white box and the leotard survive as the default look of new choreography [A].

### Across the eras [argued]

- **Light leads, paint follows.** Every major change in look came after a change in light: gas (the white act), electricity (the coloured painter's stage and the cyclorama), the console and the moving light (dance theatre's pools and darkness), projection and LED (scenery as light). Paint was strongest when light was weakest (the baroque and the Ballets Russes cloth), and weakest when light was strongest (the leotard ballet).
- **The designer's identity moved outward.** Court office, house workshop, state department, easel painter, sculptor, fashion house, architect, video artist. Each move brought in a new public and a new market (see 04 §1.5; P B9).
- **The body was freed and then re-armoured.** From tonnelet and pannier to muslin, tunic and leotard; then back to sculptural constraint with Schlemmer (1922), the Cunningham and Forsythe objects, and van Herpen's carapaces. Both directions are design choices about what dancing should look like.

### Court, Opéra and Imperial workshops

**1. Jean Bérain the Elder** (1640–1711). Costumes for Lully's operas and ballets (*Le Triomphe de l'Amour*, 1681; *Le Temple de la paix*, 1685); Opéra decorator and machinist from 1680, succeeding Vigarani, until 1707 [D: Artcena; Wikipedia]. **Brought:** a unified house style of ornament (arabesques, grotesques, acanthus) laid over one silhouette; the costume print as a means of spreading a look across Europe. **Wider art:** ornament, furniture designs for Boulle, tapestry patterns for Beauvais, decoration of the king's ships; the "Berainesque" grotesque led into the Régence and rococo [P: Wikipedia]. **Fact:** for Lully's *Amadis* (1684) he researched medieval and Renaissance dress, an early historicist costume [P: Wikipedia]. **Images:** Met 62.676.20, *Du Moulin in Peasant Garb Dancing at the Opera* (Public Domain, download) [D] https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/386244 ; Musée Condé, Chantilly, 34 hand-coloured etchings (PD works; photo terms not checked) [D].

**2. Louis-René Boquet** (1717–1814). Opéra costumes from 1758–59 (earliest known Opéra work *Pygmalion*, 1748, per Grove); Noverre's *Les Fêtes chinoises* (1754), *Médée et Jason* (1763) `medee-et-jason-1763` [D: CESAR, quoting Jordan 2005 and Grove]. **Brought:** the rococo stage costume at its fullest; a pen-and-watercolour sheet that travelled as a working document to Stuttgart, Vienna, Warsaw, Stockholm and Lisbon. **Wider art:** a pupil of Boucher [P: Lacy, via CESAR]; inspector-general of the Menus-Plaisirs from c.1770. **Fact:** in 1791 Noverre sent Gustav III of Sweden two volumes with 147 Boquet sketches for 19 ballets as a job application; the king thanked him nine months later and did not hire him [D: LoC]. **Images:** LoC/World Digital Library, item 2021668154 (National Library of Sweden S 254:1-2; PD work; LoC rights statement not checked) https://loc.gov/item/2021668154 ; Bibliothèque-musée de l'Opéra via Gallica (non-commercial reuse) [D, see 03].

**3. Pierre-Luc-Charles Ciceri** (1782–1868). Opéra sets: *Aladin* (1822, with Daguerre), the nuns' cloister in *Robert le diable* (1831), *La Sylphide* (1832), *Giselle* (1841) [D, see 04]. **Brought:** the painted cloth designed for gaslight; the moonlit ruin and the forest glade as the two halves of the Romantic stage. **Wider art:** landscape painting; lithography. **Fact:** from 1822 to 1831 every Opéra décor went out under his signature, which hides the painters in his workshop [D, see 04]. **Images:** Gallica watercolours for *Robert le diable* (PD; Gallica non-commercial terms).

**4. Eugène Lami** (1800–1890) and **Paul Lormier** (1813–1895). Lami is credited with the costume of *La Sylphide* (1832); Lormier designed *Giselle*'s costumes (1841) [register]. **Brought:** the white bell skirt of gauze over a fitted bodice, the garment later called the Romantic tutu. **Wider art:** Lami was a watercolourist of fashionable Paris and of Louis-Philippe's court [P]. **Fact:** the Lami attribution is a classic contested credit: secondary sources say Ciceri designed the Sylphide "in collaboration with" Lami, and no signed Lami design for the dress has been cited in this project [C, see 04 log]. **Images:** Gallica (Lormier designs for *Giselle*, PD; non-commercial terms) [P].

**5. "C. Wilhelm" (William J. C. Pitcher)** (1858–1925; dates probable). Costume and scenery designer for London's music-hall ballets at the Empire Theatre, Leicester Square, in the 1880s–1900s [P]. **Brought:** a lavish, saleable spectacle ballet outside the opera houses, with a women-led corps and English subjects. **Wider art:** pantomime and theatre design. **Fact:** he used a German-sounding pseudonym; Harvard's Houghton Library bought 21 of his designs in 2018 [D: HOLLIS finding aid MS Thr 1770]. **Images:** Houghton MS Thr 1770 (not digitised, per finding aid; PD works).

**6. Ivan Vsevolozhsky** (1835–1909). Director of the Imperial Theatres (1881–99) and costume designer for *The Sleeping Beauty* (1890) and *The Nutcracker* (1892) [register; P]. **Brought:** the director as designer; a Louis XIV court used as a mirror of the Russian one. **Fact:** when the Mariinsky reconstructed his *Sleeping Beauty* costumes in 1999, the press attacked their bright colour, as his successor Telyakovsky had a century before [D: Vremya novostei 2007, quoting restorer Yelena Zaitseva] `sleeping-beauty-1890`. **Images:** St Petersburg State Theatre Library (PD works; reproduction terms not found).

**7. Evgeny Ponomarev** (1852–1906). Head of costume for the St Petersburg Imperial Theatres from 1887 to his death; *La Bayadère* (the Mariinsky still credits its costumes to him), *Le Corsaire* [D: SPb Theatre Library calendar; Mariinsky]. **Brought:** costume history as method: he studied Russian dress for an Academy prize; three colours in many shades for *Corsaire* [D: Vremya 2007]. **Fact:** his costumes dressed the characters of 38 ballets, 67 operas and 35 plays, and he designed Russian court dress for the Winter Palace costume ball of 1903, where Nicholas II's costume was made by the theatres' costumier I. I. Caffi [D: SPb Theatre Library calendar]. The Bolshoi rebuilt his *Corsaire* costumes from his sketches in 2007 (Zaitseva) [D]. **Images:** St Petersburg State Theatre Library (SPbGTB) sketches (PD works; terms not found).

**8. Konstantin Korovin** (1861–1939). Bolshoi from 1899; chief designer of the Imperial Theatres from 1910; Gorsky's *Don Quixote* (1900, with Golovin), *La Bayadère* (1904), *Raymonda* (1908), *Salammbô* (1910), *Le Corsaire* (1912) [D: Churakova, Tretyakov Gallery Magazine 2012] `don-quixote-1869`. **Brought:** the painter as chief artist of a production; impressionist colour; looser tunics in place of corsets [D: Churakova]. **Wider art:** Russian Impressionist landscape painting; Mamontov's private opera. **Fact:** he let his workshop painters (Golov, Clodt) execute freely from his sketches, where Golovin in St Petersburg policed every stroke [D: Churakova, PDF]. **Images:** Tretyakov and Bakhrushin (PD works; Bakhrushin "all rights reserved" on its online images, see 03).

**9. Aleksandr Golovin** (1863–1930). *The Firebird* sets (1910) `firebird-1910`; Mariinsky studio above the auditorium [D, see 04]. **Brought:** the "laminated" backdrop, built from layers of texture and paint. **Fact:** the cloth Paris saw in 1910 was painted by Nikolai Sapunov, who ignored that method [D, see 04]. **Images:** Tretyakov; Harvard Theatre Collection (PD works).

### The painter's stage, 1909–1933

**10. Léon Bakst** (1866–1924). *Cléopâtre* (1909), *Schéhérazade* (1910), *Le Spectre de la rose* (1911), *L'Après-midi d'un faune* and *Daphnis et Chloé* (1912), *Jeux* (1913), *The Sleeping Princess* (1921) `scheherazade-1910` `spectre-de-la-rose-1911` `apres-midi-faune-1912` `jeux-1913`. **Brought:** colour as sensation (clashing greens, oranges, pinks), the costume drawn as a moving body, and a studio that produced copies of each design [D: Met, Speelberg 2017]. **Wider art:** portraits, illustration, *Mir iskusstva*; fashion sketches for Paquin (1912) [D, see 04]. **Fact:** the Met catalogues one of its sheets as "Léon Bakst ... and/or workshop", describing it as "somewhat coarse" and possibly an assistant's copy [D: Met 2021.16.26]. **Images:** Met (see the finding above: link-only); Harvard Theatre Collection, Rothschild gift (e.g. MS Thr 414.4 (8), (20)) [D: NGA 2013 checklist; Harvard licence for images not verified]; Wadsworth Atheneum, *Faune* costume design [D: NGA checklist]; V&A, Blue God costume, *Le Dieu bleu* (1912), S.547-1978 (V&A non-commercial terms) [D].

**11. Alexandre Benois** (1870–1960). *Le Pavillon d'Armide* (1907/1909), *Les Sylphides* (1909), *Petrushka* (1911) `pavillon-darmide-1907` `les-sylphides-1909` `petrushka-1911`. **Brought:** the historical reverie (Versailles, 1830s Petersburg fairground) and the scenario-writer-designer. **Wider art:** art historian and critic; co-founder of *Mir iskusstva*. **Fact:** he wrote much of the scenario of *Petrushka* and fought for years over its credit [P]. **Images:** © to 2030 (link-only); Wadsworth Atheneum holds his 1925 *Petrushka* front-cloth design [D: NGA checklist].

**12. Nicholas Roerich** (1874–1947). *Polovtsian Dances* (1909), *The Rite of Spring* (1913) `rite-of-spring-1913`. **Brought:** archaeology as design source (Slavic and pre-Christian ornament, felt and fur, sacred hills). **Wider art:** painter, archaeologist, later mystic and author of a 1935 treaty for protecting cultural monuments (the Roerich Pact) [P]. **Fact:** his costume for two Clowns in *Rite* is in the Harvard Theatre Collection (MS Thr 414.4 (119)) [D: NGA 2013 checklist]; the 1987 Joffrey reconstruction rebuilt his costumes (see 04). **Images:** PD work; Harvard image licence to verify.

**13. Natalia Goncharova** (1881–1962). *Le Coq d'or* (1914), *Les Noces* (1923), *The Firebird* (1926 décor) `les-noces-1923`. **Brought:** Russian folk and icon colour (1914), then its opposite: brown and white work clothes for *Les Noces* [P]. **Wider art:** Rayonism with Larionov; Neo-Primitivism. **Fact:** a Sea Horse costume for *Sadko* (c.1916), in silk-cotton satin, lamé, paint and wire, belongs to the Dansmuseet, Stockholm [D: NGA checklist]. **Images:** © ADAGP (link-only); LoC holds designs (br0041, br0052) [D, see 04].

**14. Mikhail Larionov** (1881–1964). *Soleil de nuit* (1915), *Contes russes*, *Chout* (1921), *Renard* (1922) [P]. **Brought:** Futurist-folk costume, built and padded to distort the body. **Wider art:** Rayonism. **Fact:** he partnered Goncharova in work and life for six decades [P]. **Images:** © (link-only); Harvard Theatre Collection [D: Harvard Library page].

**15. Pablo Picasso** (1881–1973). *Parade* (1917), *Le Tricorne* (1919), *Pulcinella* (1920), *Cuadro flamenco* (1921), *Mercure* (1924), the *Train Bleu* curtain (1924) `parade-1917` `tricorne-1919` `train-bleu-1924`. **Brought:** Cubist construction as costume (the towering cardboard Managers in *Parade*) [P]; the curtain as a separate painting. **Fact:** the Miller's Wife dress from *Tricorne*, pink with black lace, is one of two known survivors and is in the London Museum [D, see 03]; FIT suggests it influenced a Paquin dress of c.1946 [D: FIT *Dance & Fashion*]. **Images:** © Succession Picasso (link-only).

**16. Henri Matisse** (1869–1954). *Le Chant du rossignol* (1920); *Rouge et Noir* (Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo, 1939) [P]. **Brought:** decoration as structure: flat colour, white felt and blue for the Chinese court [P]. **Fact:** Marie Muelle made the *Rossignol* costumes, "closely supervised by Matisse" [D: NGA Teacher's Notes, see 03]. **Images:** PD in the EU from 2025; museum photo terms vary.

**17. André Derain** (1880–1954). *La Boutique fantasque* (1919) `boutique-fantasque-1919`; later *Jack in the Box* (1926) and work for the Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo [P]. **Brought:** a Fauve painter's pastel, naive toyshop. **Fact:** Karinska realised Derain's later sketches, choosing fabrics and construction herself [P: see 03 on Karinska]. **Images:** PD in EU from 2025.

**18. Georges Braque** (1882–1963). *Les Fâcheux* (1924), *Zéphire et Flore* (1925) [P]. **Brought:** Cubist still-life colour (browns, greens, greys) on stage. **Images:** ©.

**19. Marie Laurencin** (1883–1956). *Les Biches* (1924) `les-biches-1924`. **Brought:** a pastel palette of pink, grey-blue and white for a ballet about flirtation at a house party; the 1920s "modern girl" in a short tunic. **Wider art:** painter of women; close to Apollinaire. **Fact:** her designs enter the EU public domain on 1 January 2027 [A, life+70]. **Images:** LoC (br0055) [D, see 04]; © until end 2026.

**20. Juan Gris** (1887–1927). *Les Tentations de la bergère* (1924); *La Colombe* (1924) [P]. **Brought:** Cubist order applied to an 18th-century pastoral. **Images:** PD; LoC, shepherdess design (br0051) [D, see 04].

**21. Joan Miró** (1893–1983). *Roméo et Juliette* (1926, with Max Ernst); *Jeux d'enfants* (Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo, 1932) [P]. **Brought:** Surrealist biomorphic shapes as moving scenery. **Fact:** the Surrealist group protested at the 1926 premiere because two of their members had worked for Diaghilev [P]. **Images:** ©; Harvard Theatre Collection holds designs [D: Harvard Library page].

**22. Giorgio de Chirico** (1888–1978). *Le Bal* (1929) [P]. **Brought:** costumes painted with columns and masonry, so that dancers became "animated stones" [D: Bellow 2013 chapter title, see 04]. **Images:** ©.

**23. Georges Rouault** (1871–1958). *Le Fils prodigue* (1929) `prodigal-son-1929`. **Brought:** a painter of sacred subjects in heavy black outlines and stained-glass colour. **Fact:** a well-told story says Diaghilev had him locked into his Monte Carlo hotel until the designs were finished [P, anecdote; not checked at source]. **Images:** ©.

**24. Fernand Léger** (1881–1955). *Skating Rink* (1922), *La Création du monde* (1923), Ballets Suédois `skating-rink-1922` `creation-du-monde-1923`. **Brought:** the machine aesthetic and painted cut-out figures; for *Création*, African sculpture as a source (the exotic question, Part 4). **Wider art:** the film *Ballet mécanique* (1924). **Fact:** the Ballets Suédois was Rolf de Maré's rival company in Paris, 1920–25, and its archive founded the Dansmuseet, Stockholm [P]. **Images:** PD in the EU from 2026; Dansmuseet holdings.

**25. Gabrielle "Coco" Chanel** (1883–1971). *Le Train Bleu* (1924) costumes; *Apollon musagète* (1929 costumes) `train-bleu-1924` `apollon-musagete-1928`. **Brought:** real sportswear (jersey bathing suits, tennis whites, golf knits) as costume, an early, and probably the earliest well-known, couturier's commission for a whole ballet's costumes [D for the costumes: Davis 2010, see 04; P for the priority]. **Fact:** Chanel now patronises costume conservation as the Australian Ballet's "Living Heritage Partner" [D, see 03]. **Images:** ©.

**26. Naum Gabo** (1890–1977) and **Antoine Pevsner** (1884–1962). *La Chatte* (1927): "architecture and sculpture" in plastic, oilcloth, wood and steel [D, see 04]. **Brought:** transparent Constructivist sculpture as set; light passing through it. **Images:** Pevsner PD in EU from 2033; Gabo ©.

**27. Alexandra Exter** (1882–1949). Designs for Bronislava Nijinska's Théâtre Chorégraphique (1925: *Holy Etudes*, *Jazz*, *Le Guignol*, *The Sports and Touring Ballet Revue*) [D: LoC Nijinska Collection]; Tairov's Chamber Theatre in Moscow [P]. **Brought:** Cubo-Futurist colour planes and costumes built of geometric volumes; a teaching studio in Kyiv. **Fact:** her Aviator and Porter costumes for a 1925 "sports and touring" revue show ballet answering the cult of the athlete and the tourist [D for the designs; A for the reading]. **Images:** PD work; LoC items https://loc.gov/item/2023861859 and https://www.loc.gov/item/ihas.200154561 (LoC rights statement not checked; "probably no known restrictions") [D/P].

**28. Lyubov Popova** (1889–1924). **Boundary entry.** No ballet design was found in this pass. Her "constructivist apparatus" for Meyerhold's *The Magnanimous Cuckold* (1922), a working frame of ramps, wheels and ladders with performers in identical work-suits (*prozodezhda*), is the theatrical model that Yakulov's *Pas d'acier* (1927) and Gabo and Pevsner's *La Chatte* carried into ballet [P; A for the line of influence]. **Images:** PD work; set reconstruction photographs © holders.

**29. Oskar Schlemmer** (1888–1943). *Das Triadische Ballett* (Stuttgart, 1922; Hindemith score 1926) `triadic-ballet-1922`. **Brought:** the costume as "ambulant architecture" that sets the movement: "it is the costume and not the mobility of the dancer that determines the dance movement" [D: Schlemmer estate website]; three acts on lemon-yellow, pink and black stages [D: ibid.]. **Wider art:** Bauhaus master of the stage workshop; painter of figures in space. **Fact:** in 1923 an ownership agreement handed some of the costumes to his co-creators, the dancers Albert Burger and Elsa Hötzel, so the work "was judged not to be Schlemmer's individual achievement" [D: Aoki Kanae, *Design Theory* 52, 2008, citing Scheper 1988]. A 2014 Brazilian research project rebuilt all 18 costumes with more than 50 students [D: Ramos & Boccara, *Estudos em Design* 2015]. **Images:** PD work (d. 1943); Harvard Art Museums, 1926 costume chart BR50.428 (photo © President and Fellows of Harvard College; IIIF reuse offered, licence not verified) https://harvardartmuseums.org/art/304377 .

**30. Pavel Tchelitchew** (1898–1957). *Ode* (1928), *L'Errante* (1933), *Orpheus* (Met Opera, 1936), *Nobilissima Visione* (1938) [D: Windham, *Dance Index* 1944]. **Brought:** light as the set: projections on gauze, drapery, a "world of light" [D: Windham]. **Fact:** *L'Errante* ended with "a great, wet cloud of white Chinese silk" falling from above to bury the heroine; Balanchine called the ballet "impossible to describe; it's all visual" [D: V&A O1222655, quoting Windham 1944 and *Sunday Times*, 2 July 1933]. **Images:** © to 2027; V&A record (link-only) https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O1222655/lerrante-set-design-tchelitchev-pavel ; *Dance Index* 1944 text on archive.org [D].

**31. Christian Bérard** (1902–1949). *Cotillon* (1932), *Mozartiana* (1933), *Symphonie fantastique* (1936) [P]. **Brought:** Neo-Romantic dream colour and loose, painterly costume; worked with Karinska as executant [P]. **Wider art:** fashion illustration for *Vogue*; Cocteau's *La Belle et la Bête* (film, 1946). **Images:** PD in EU since 2020.

### Soviet and Eastern stages

**32. Valentina Khodasevich** (1894–1970; dates probable). *The Golden Age* (1930), *The Fountain of Bakhchisarai* (1934) `golden-age-1930` `fountain-bakhchisarai-1934`. **Brought:** a painter of the avant-garde who moved from satirical modern costume to the historical spectacle of drambalet; one designer across the turn in Soviet policy [A]. **Images:** © (link-only).

**33. Simon (Soliko) Virsaladze** (1908/09–1989). *The Stone Flower* (1957), *Legend of Love* (1961), *Spartacus* (1968), *The Nutcracker* (1966) with Grigorovich `stone-flower-1957` `legend-of-love-1961` `spartacus-1968` [D, see 04]. **Brought:** the unit set and costume as colour field; he dyed and painted fabric so that colour, not ornament, carried character [P]. **Fact:** the Bolshoi's costume restorer called him a "genius of optical deception" [D: Vremya 2007]. **Images:** © (link-only).

**34. Boris Messerer** (b. 1933). *Carmen-Suite* (Alberto Alonso for Plisetskaya, 1967) `carmen-suite-1967`. **Brought:** one image as the whole set: a bullring with high-backed chairs and a bull's-head emblem. **Images:** ©.

### America and the post-war West

**35. Isamu Noguchi** (1904–1988). *Orpheus* (1948); with Martha Graham, more than twenty works from *Frontier* (1935) [P]. **Brought:** sculpture as scenery (stones, a lyre, a veil), see 04. **Fact:** NYCB adopted his lyre as its emblem [D, see 04]. **Images:** © INFGM/ARS.

**36. Barbara Karinska** (1886–1983). Executant then designer: Bérard, Derain, Miró, Berman, Dalí; NYCB from 1949; *Symphony in C* (1950 costumes), *Jewels* (1967) `symphony-in-c-1947` `jewels-1967` [D, see 03]. **Brought:** the hoopless "powder puff" tutu and the bias-cut bodice. **Fact:** NYCB credits Berman's *Danses concertantes* costumes as "Executed by Karinska (1944); Executed by Barbara Matera (1972)", a rare credit for makers [D: NYCB repertory page]. **Images:** ©.

**37. Eugene Berman** (1899–1972). *Danses concertantes* (Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo, 1944), *Ballet Imperial* (1941 design, probable), *Roméo et Juliette* [P]. **Brought:** Neo-Romantic ruins and Baroque melancholy. **Fact:** Stravinsky "particularly admired" his *Danses concertantes* designs, and NYCB still uses them [D: NYCB]. **Images:** ©; Harvard Theatre Collection [D: Harvard Library page].

**38. Jean Rosenthal** (1912–1969). Lighting for Ballet Society and NYCB from 1946–48; *Orpheus*; designed set and light for Robbins's *The Cage* (1951) `the-cage-1951` [register; D, see 04]. **Brought:** light as an authored element with its own credit. Successors in ballet: Jennifer Tipton (*Push Comes to Shove*, 1976; *Electric Counterpoint*, 2008), Lucy Carter (McGregor's *Chroma*, *Carbon Life*) [D: register; McGregor and Puissant pages]. **Images:** none needed; plots and cue sheets in NYPL [P].

### Britain and Europe: the designer's ballet

**39. Oliver Messel** (1904–1978). *The Sleeping Beauty* (Covent Garden, 1946) [D, see 04]. **Brought:** a baroque-rococo fairy court that defined the post-war Royal Ballet. **Images:** © (V&A, link-only).

**40. Cecil Beaton** (1904–1980). *Apparitions* (1936, probable); *Marguerite and Armand* (Royal Ballet, 12 March 1963: sets and costumes Beaton, lighting William Bundy) [D: ROH Collections production 1489; Ashton Foundation]. **Brought:** the society photographer's and fashion illustrator's eye: a Second Empire world for Fonteyn and Nureyev. **Wider art:** photography; *My Fair Lady* (film, 1964). **Fact:** he gave the V&A Karinska's *Bugaku* tutu [D, see 03]. **Images:** ©.

**41. Sophie Fedorovitch** (1893–1953). *Symphonic Variations* (1946) `symphonic-variations-1946`. **Brought:** the near-bare stage in Britain: a pale green-gold cloth with curving black lines and white tunics, so Ashton's six dancers read against light [P]. **Fact:** Ashton relied on her as a "conscience" for cutting the unnecessary [P]. **Images:** ©.

**42. Nicholas Georgiadis** (1923–2001). MacMillan's *Romeo and Juliet* (1965), *Manon* (1974), *Mayerling* (1978) `romeo-juliet-macmillan-1965` `manon-1974` `mayerling-1978`. **Brought:** monumental, multi-level architecture in which the lovers are small; Quattrocento painting "through the filter of mid-20th century theatre and fashion" [D: V&A S.1903-1986]. **Fact:** he could give the three-act ballet only 20 days of design time; the Covent Garden board had refused MacMillan's first choice, Kenneth Rowell [D: V&A]. **Images:** © Georgiadis estate; V&A record (link-only) https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O1221267/romeo-and-juliet-set-design-georgiadis-nicholas .

**43. Yolanda Sonnabend** (1935–2015). MacMillan's *Symphony* (1963), *Rituals* (1975), *Requiem* (Stuttgart, 1976), *My Brother, My Sisters* (1978); Dowell's *Swan Lake* (1987) `swan-lake-1877-1895` [D: Bristol Theatre Collection]. **Brought:** the anatomical costume: for *Requiem*, Lycra body tights with torsos painted with veins and muscles after Vesalius and Blake, against translucent fibreglass panels [D: MacMillan estate site]. **Fact:** five days before the premiere of *Different Drummer* (1984), MacMillan dropped her set and kept her costumes [D: Bristol]. **Images:** ©; archive at University of Bristol Theatre Collection (uncatalogued).

**44. Jürgen Rose** (b. 1937). Cranko's *Onegin* (1965; new version 1967), Neumeier's *Lady of the Camellias* (1978) `onegin-1965` `lady-of-camellias-1978` [D: Stuttgart Ballet]. **Brought:** the narrative ballet's period world built with a painter's detail and a theatre director's realism; designs that tour for sixty years. **Images:** ©.

### Art-world collaborators of modern dance (the contrast case)

**45. Robert Rauschenberg** (1925–2008). Resident designer for Merce Cunningham, 1954–64, more than twenty works (*Minutiae*, 1954; *Summerspace*, 1958; *Story*, 1963) [D, see 04]. **Brought:** the Combine (found objects joined with paint) as set; costume and light made independently of the dance. **Images:** © Rauschenberg Foundation; Walker Art Center.

**46. Jasper Johns** (b. 1930) **and the artists he invited.** As Cunningham's artistic adviser from 1967 Johns brought in Frank Stella (*Scramble*, 1967), Andy Warhol (*RainForest*, 1968), Robert Morris (*Canfield*, 1969), Bruce Nauman (*Tread*, 1970) and Neil Jenney (*Objects*, 1970), and made *Walkaround Time* (1968) after Duchamp's *Large Glass* [D: Rivera 1997, Routledge abstract]. **Fact:** in *RainForest* (premiere Buffalo, 9 March 1968) Warhol's *Silver Clouds*, helium-filled Mylar pillows, floated free, and Johns, uncredited, cut the dancers' flesh-coloured leotards with a razor blade to roughen them [D: Merce Cunningham Trust]. **Images:** © (link-only).

**47. Andy Warhol** (1928–1987). *RainForest* (1968). **Brought:** a gallery installation lent to a dance: the set already existed as an artwork. **Images:** ©.

**48. Donald Judd** (1928–1994), with a note on **Ellsworth Kelly** (1923–2015). Judd designed for Trisha Brown's company: coloured drops for *Son of Gone Fishin'* (1981, date probable) and the "visual presentation, sets and costumes" and sound concept for *Newark (Niweweorce)* (1987, date probable), with drops in cadmium red light, burnt sienna, cadmium yellow and deep blue that rose and fell to change the stage's depth [D: Judd Foundation]. **Fact:** his 1990 "Stage Set", made for an exhibition in Vienna, has stood in the city's Stadtpark since 1996 [D: Judd Foundation; its photo by Gugerell is CC0 on Wikimedia Commons per that page]. **Kelly:** no ballet design was found in this pass; the MoMA 1973 catalogue records that as a student he designed sets and costumes for the whole of *Petrushka* on a folding screen [D: Goossen, MoMA 1973]. Both are modern-dance or non-dance cases and should be taught as such.

### Dance theatre

**49. Rolf Borzik** (1944–1980) and **Peter Pabst** (b. 1944). Borzik designed for Pina Bausch from 1973 to 1980: peat for *Le Sacre du printemps* (1975), leaves for *Bluebeard* (1977), chairs for *Café Müller* (1978), water for *Arien* (1979) `sacre-bausch-1975` [D: Pina Bausch Foundation]. Pabst designed 26 sets for Bausch from *1980* to 2009, including *Nelken* (30 Dec 1982) [D: Pabst 2020; pinabausch.org]. **Brought:** the floor as material and as record. **Fact:** after each *Nelken*, technicians and volunteers "comforted" the 8,000 trampled carnations the next morning [D: Pabst 2020]. **Images:** © Pina Bausch Foundation and photographers.

### Fashion houses on the ballet stage

**50. Jean Paul Gaultier** (b. 1952). Costumes for Régine Chopinot from the 1980s (*Le Défilé*, 1985; *Façade*, 1993) and for Preljocaj's *Blanche Neige* (Lyon Biennale, 2008) [D: FIT *Dance & Fashion*; CNCS dossier]. **Brought:** the runway's own icons (underwear as outerwear, tattooed unitards, sailor stripes). **Fact:** for *Blanche Neige* he sketched while Preljocaj explained the dramaturgy, and costumes were refitted and sometimes remade on the dancers as the choreography changed [D: CNCS/Opéra de Rennes dossier 2015]. **Images:** ©; CNCS Moulins.

**51. Miyake Issey** (1938–2022). Forsythe's *The Loss of Small Detail* (Frankfurt, 1991) [D/P, see 04]. **Brought:** pleated polyester that moves and packs flat; the stage as a test bed for a fashion line (Pleats Please, probable). **Images:** ©.

## Part 3. Design ideas that recur

Eighteen ideas a maker can borrow. Each has a definition, works from at least three periods, what the idea does, and the trap in it. Readings are [A] unless marked.

### 3.1 The white tutu as a uniform
**What it is.** A corps of women in identical white skirts, so that thirty bodies read as one surface and one idea (spirit, swan, shade, dream). **Works.** *La Sylphide* (1832) and *Giselle* Act II (1841), bell skirts of tarlatan `sylphide-1832` `giselle-1841`; the Kingdom of the Shades in *La Bayadère* (1877) `bayadere-1877`; the swans of *Swan Lake* (1895) `swan-lake-1877-1895`; *Les Sylphides* (1909), which made the long white tutu a "chopinienne" [D: Vremya 2007 notes the Russian name *shopenovka*] `les-sylphides-1909`; *Serenade* (1934) and *Symphony in C* (1947/1950, Karinska's powder-puff tutus) `serenade-1934` `symphony-in-c-1947`; then its reversal in Tim Yip's tattered, dust-coloured Wilis (2016) `giselle-khan-2016` [D: New Yorker 2019]. **What it does.** Removes individuality so that pattern can be seen; makes the women a choir. **Trap.** It is also a designed default body (white, slim, identical), the issue that 03 raises for pink tights and shoes.

### 3.2 The costume family
**What it is.** A set of costumes built on one pattern with controlled variation (colour, trim, length) to show rank and group: corps, demi-soloists, soloists, principals. **Works.** Bérain's one silhouette with endlessly varied trimming (1680s) [D: Britannica]; the six fairies and their cavaliers in Vsevolozhsky's *Sleeping Beauty* (1890), one cut in six colours `sleeping-beauty-1890`; Bakst's odalisques in *Schéhérazade* (1910); *Jewels* (1967), three acts as three gem colours, emerald, ruby and diamond `jewels-1967` (see P on Van Cleef & Arpels); Gareth Pugh's *Carbon Life* (2012), where each dancer's costume grows by added layers [D: McGregor]. **What it does.** Gives the audience a legend for reading the stage, exactly as a product line does for a shop (see P §C). **Trap.** Variation that is too small vanishes at 30 m; designers test at distance.

### 3.3 The transformation scene
**What it is.** The set changes in view of the audience, so the change itself is the event. **Works.** Torelli's simultaneous wing change (Venice 1641; Paris, *Les Noces de Thétis*, 1654) [D, see 04]; the moving panorama of the Lilac Fairy's journey in *Sleeping Beauty* (1890) and the growing Christmas tree in *The Nutcracker* (1892) `nutcracker-1892` [P]; the Firebird's enchanted garden dissolving (1910) [P]; Basil Twist's silk sea and storm in *The Winter's Tale* (2014) [D: ROH 2024]; Crowley and Driscoll's projected rabbit-hole fall in *Alice* (2011) [D, see 04]. **What it does.** Marks the passage between two worlds, which is the structural spine of most narrative ballets. **Trap.** Machine time can upstage dance time; the best ones are timed to the music, not to the stage crew.

### 3.4 The gauze and the vision
**What it is.** A scrim (gauze) painted on its front looks solid when lit from the front and vanishes when lit from behind, so a second world appears inside the first. **Works.** The nuns rising in *Robert le diable* (1831) `robert-le-diable-nuns-1831`; the Wilis' forest (1841); the Vision scene in *Sleeping Beauty* (1890), where Aurora appears to the Prince; Tchelitchew's projections on gauze and drapery in *L'Errante* (1933) [D: V&A]; Puissant's projected dancer-doubles in *Electric Counterpoint* (2008) [D]. **What it does.** Gives ballet its characteristic doubled world: the real and the dreamed on one stage. **Trap.** Every layer of gauze dims the dancers behind it; lighting designers fight the scenic designer for each one.

### 3.5 Colour as character
**What it is.** A colour assigned to a person, group or idea and kept through the work. **Works.** Ponomarev's three colours for *Le Corsaire* (red, blue, white) [D: Vremya 2007]; Bakst's colour clash in *Schéhérazade* (1910); Laurencin's pinks and greys for *Les Biches* (1924) `les-biches-1924`; Schlemmer's yellow, pink and black acts (1922) [D]; Virsaladze's colour fields for *Spartacus* (1968); Lagerfeld's black and white (2016); McGregor and Pawson's *Chroma* (2006), whose title McGregor glossed as the body "as a frequency of color—in freedom from white" [D: Ailey page]. **What it does.** Lets an audience follow a story without words. **Trap.** Stage light changes colour: a red under blue light is brown. Designers sample fabric under the actual rig.

### 3.6 The painted cloth against the built set
**What it is.** The oldest choice in stage design: an illusion painted on a flat surface, or a solid, three-dimensional structure the dancers can use. **Works.** Torelli's machine stage (built) against Ciceri's painted cloths (1820s–40s); Bakst and Benois's painted cloths (1909–14) against Gabo and Pevsner's constructions (1927) and Yakulov's machines for *Pas d'acier* (1927) `pas-dacier-1927`; Georgiadis's multi-level architecture for *Romeo and Juliet* (1965) against Sonnabend's soft "floating backcloths" for *Symphony* (1963) [D: V&A; Bristol]; Pawson's built white box (2006) against projection. **What it does.** Painted cloths travel and change quickly; built sets give levels for choreography. The choice is often driven by touring and money as much as by taste. **Trap.** A built set made for one stage does not fit others: Georgiadis's 1965 *Romeo* sets had to be adapted for other houses until a touring version was made in 2001 [D: MacMillan estate].

### 3.7 The bare stage and the leotard
**What it is.** No scenery; dancers in practice clothes or body tights; the music and light as the only setting. **Works.** Fedorovitch's near-empty *Symphonic Variations* (1946) [P] `symphonic-variations-1946`; *The Four Temperaments* in practice clothes from 1951 and *Agon* (1957) [D, see 04]; Cunningham's *RainForest* in flesh leotards (1968) [D]; Sonnabend's painted body tights in *Requiem* (1976) [D]; McGregor's *Chroma* (2006) and *Carbon Life*'s opening (2012) [D]. **What it does.** Puts the line of the body and the structure of the music at the centre. **Trap.** It is still designed: the cut of the leotard, the colour of the tights and the cyclorama's light are decisions, and "flesh colour" has meant one skin tone (see 03 §2.3).

### 3.8 Light as scenery
**What it is.** Light is the set, not just the means of seeing it. **Works.** Gas and the dimmed auditorium (1822) [D]; Loïe Fuller's patented stage devices and coloured projections on her silk (patents filed in France 1893, US 1894–95) [D: Bilska 2024; US patent 533,167] (a dancer outside ballet whose devices ballet borrowed); Tchelitchew's *Ode* (1928) and *L'Errante* (1933) [D]; Rosenthal at NYCB (from 1948) [D]; Lucy Carter's light changing the depth of Pawson's *Chroma* box (2006) [D: Pawson]. **What it does.** Changes place and time in seconds; costs little to tour. **Trap.** Light leaves no object: it is the part of the design the archive keeps worst (see 04 concept 7).

### 3.9 The floor as material
**What it is.** The floor becomes a designed surface: raked, sprung, painted, covered or flooded. **Works.** The Palais Garnier's raked stage (5 per cent, 1875) [D, see 03]; the Harlequin portable dance floor (1977) [D, see 03]; Borzik's peat for Bausch's *Rite* (1975) and the flooded stage of *Arien* (1979), the painted sea of *Keuschheitslegende* (1979) [D: Pina Bausch Foundation]; Pabst's carnations for *Nelken* (1982) [D]. **What it does.** Changes how dancers move (peat drags; water splashes; carnations crush) and keeps a record of the performance on the floor and on the bodies [D: Borzik biography]. **Trap.** Safety and cost: real material must be cleaned, replaced, quarantined at borders and made non-slip.

### 3.10 Fashion houses on stage, stage in fashion
**What it is.** Traffic between the couture house and the ballet stage in both directions. **Works.** Bakst sketching for Paquin (1912) [D, see 04]; Chanel's *Le Train Bleu* (1924) [D]; Yves Saint Laurent's "Ballets Russes" collection (1976) [D, see P]; Gaultier for Chopinot (1985) and Preljocaj (2008) [D]; Miyake for Forsythe (1991); Valentino (2012), van Herpen (2013, 2025), Lagerfeld (2016), Lacroix (2011, 2017) [D]. **What it does.** Brings money, press and new audiences; tests fashion against a moving body. **Trap.** "The dancer is not walking down a runway—she is leaping into the air" (Marc Happel) [D: FIT 2014]. See P §B9 for what is kept and dropped each way.

### 3.11 The poster and the programme as identity
**What it is.** The printed image that sells the season becomes the company's face. **Works.** Cocteau's matched pair of posters for 1911 (Nijinsky and Karsavina in *Le Spectre de la rose*) [D, see P]; the *Comœdia illustré* souvenir programmes with Bakst covers (1911–12) [D, see P]; Noguchi's *Orpheus* lyre as NYCB's emblem (1948) [D, see 04]; the Royal Ballet and Opera's 2025 identity built from an archive wordmark [D, see P]. **What it does.** Carries a stage world into the street. **Trap.** The poster image is usually a designer's drawing or a posed photograph, not the dance as performed.

### 3.12 The curtain as artwork
**What it is.** A front cloth painted as an independent picture, seen before the dance begins. **Works.** Benois's *Petrushka* front cloth (1911; a 1925 design is at the Wadsworth Atheneum) [D: NGA checklist]; Picasso's *Parade* curtain (1917) and *Train Bleu* cloth (1924) [D, see 04]; Chagall's four backdrops for *Aleko* (Mexico City, 1942) and his *Firebird* curtain (1945) [D: LACMA Unframed 2017]. **What it does.** Sets the mood and announces the artist; it is also the most collectible piece of a production (the *Aleko* backdrops were sold at auction in 1977 when the company needed money [D: LACMA]). **Trap.** A curtain is seen for a minute; its fame can outrun the ballet's.

### 3.13 The mask and the made-up face
**What it is.** A designed face: mask, wig and make-up as part of the costume. **Works.** Court masks (1581–1670s) [P]; Jean Hugo's masks for *Les Mariés de la tour Eiffel* (1921) `maries-tour-eiffel-1921`; Schlemmer's masks and helmets (1922) [D]; Germán Cueto's masks for *La Coronela* (1940) [register]; the "Chinese" make-up of *Nutcracker* "Tea", questioned by the Final Bow for Yellowface pledge (2017) [D, see 03]. **What it does.** Abstracts the dancer into a type. **Trap.** Make-up has been a technology of racial caricature; see Part 4.4.

### 3.14 The archive as a source (historicism)
**What it is.** Design that studies the past (paintings, dress, earlier productions) and quotes it. **Works.** Bérain's research for *Amadis* (1684) [P]; Ponomarev's costume history (1887–1906) [D]; Bakst's *Sleeping Princess* sets after Galli Bibiena, Bérain and Boquet (1921) [D: NGA Australia]; Georgiadis and MacMillan studying Quattrocento paintings (1965) [D: V&A]; Lacroix wanting *La Source*'s costumes to look as if they "had come out of a long sleep" (2011) [D: CNCS]; RBO's palette and wordmark from its archive (2025) [D, see P]. **What it does.** Gives authority and depth; ties a new work to a lineage. **Trap.** Historical accuracy fights the moving body: MacMillan argued for freedom while his designers argued for accuracy [D: MacMillan estate, "MacMillan and his Designers"].

### 3.15 The machine and the mechanical body
**What it is.** The dancer as automaton, robot or part of a machine. **Works.** The doll in *Coppélia* (1870) `coppelia-1870`; Picasso's Managers in *Parade* (1917); Schlemmer's *Triadic Ballet* (1922); Yakulov's *Pas d'acier* factory (1927); van Herpen's carapace costumes (2013). **What it does.** Asks what is human in movement; mirrors each era's technology (clockwork, the factory, the computer). **Trap.** Constraint can make dancing impossible; Schlemmer accepted that the costume determines the movement [D].

### 3.16 Ornament as geography (the "exotic" look)
**What it is.** A place signalled by costume ornament and colour rather than by research or by people from that place. **Works.** Jean-Baptiste Martin's "Inca, African, Chinese, and Mexican motifs" (Opéra, from 1748) [D: Britannica]; *La Fille du Pharaon* (1862) and *La Bayadère* (1877); *Schéhérazade* (1910) and *Le Dieu bleu* (1912); Léger's *Création du monde* (1923) after African sculpture; Béjart's *The Kabuki* (Tokyo, 1986) with a European designer `the-kabuki-1986`. **What it does.** Delivers spectacle and a market (see P B9). **Trap.** See Part 4.4.

### 3.17 The found object and the readymade
**What it is.** A set or costume taken from everyday life or from an existing artwork. **Works.** Chanel's sportswear (1924); Goncharova's work clothes for *Les Noces* (1923); Warhol's *Silver Clouds* lent to *RainForest* (1968) [D]; Judd's colour drops for Trisha Brown (1981, 1987) [D]; Borzik's café chairs in *Café Müller* (1978) [D]. **What it does.** Brings the street or the gallery onto the stage and questions what counts as "design". **Trap.** A readymade carries meanings the choreographer did not choose.

### 3.18 One artist, one world
**What it is.** A single designer (or a designer-choreographer) who makes the whole look, against the committee of a house workshop. **Works.** Bérain's office (1680–1707); Ciceri's signed workshop (1822–31) [D]; Korovin as "chief artist" (1900s) [D]; the Ballets Russes painter (1909–29); Forsythe designing his own sets and light (from the 1980s) [register `in-the-middle-1987` lists Forsythe as designer]; Bausch with Borzik and then Pabst for 36 years combined [D]. **What it does.** Coherence: the world reads as one mind. **Trap.** The single name hides the workshop (see 04 §1.4).

## Part 4. Comparisons and disputes in design

Each dispute is given as two readings in equal grammar, then the evidence, then what a maker can do with it.

### 4.1 Designer against choreographer: who leads?

**Reading A: the painter's ballet.** Diaghilev's company made the painter an equal or leading author, and some works were built around the design. The Musée Picasso says that in *Parade* "it is the dance that illustrates and underlines the sets and costumes" [D, see 04]. Juliet Bellow argues that the stage let painters test painting against "duration and dimension, figuration and embodiment" [D quote via JDH review, see 04]. Tchelitchew's *L'Errante* (1933) was, in Balanchine's words, "all visual" [D: V&A]. Schlemmer said outright that his costume, not the dancer, set the movement [D].

**Reading B: see the music.** Balanchine's line "See the music, hear the dance" [D: quoted by the Chicago Symphony Orchestra, 2024; the *New Yorker* (2009) says it is "arguably rightfully his", so attribution probable] stands for the opposite view: design should clear the stage so music and movement can be seen. *The Four Temperaments* dropped its 1946 Seligmann designs for practice clothes in 1951 [D, see 04]. Hanna Järvinen's critique of design-led histories (2014) says they show "how easily dance becomes secondary to music and set design" [D, see 04].

**The record.** Both views lived in the same people: Balanchine worked with Tchelitchew, Berman, Noguchi and Karinska while also making the leotard ballet. MacMillan argued with his designers for freedom of the body while choosing monumental sets [D: MacMillan estate]. McGregor invited the architect Pawson precisely to "disrupt the spaces in which the body performs" [D: McGregor on the RBO page]. **For a maker [A]:** decide early which element leads in each scene; the dispute is resolved scene by scene, not once.

### 4.2 Reconstruction: can a design be brought back?

**Reading A: yes, from the documents.** The Joffrey's *Rite* (1987) rebuilt Roerich's costumes and cloths after about fifteen years of research by Hodson and Archer [D, see 04]. The Mariinsky rebuilt Vsevolozhsky's *Sleeping Beauty* costumes (1999) and the Bolshoi rebuilt Ponomarev's *Corsaire* costumes (2007) from sketches; the restorer Yelena Zaitseva says Ponomarev's sketches are literal: "what is drawn on the sketch is what you will see on stage" [D: Vremya 2007]. Ratmansky's *Sleeping Beauty* (ABT, 2015) is designed by Richard Hudson "after Léon Bakst" `sleeping-beauty-ratmansky-2015` [register]. A Brazilian university project rebuilt Schlemmer's 18 *Triadic* costumes in 2014 [D].

**Reading B: every reconstruction is a new design.** The V&A says Messel's own 1976 remake for ABT was "surprisingly unlike" his 1946 original [D, see 04]. Aoki Kanae calls Gerhart Bohner's Berlin version of the *Triadic Ballet* (Akademie der Künste; 1977, date probable) closer to a "reinterpretation" than a reconstruction [D: Aoki 2008]. Fabrics, dyes, lights and bodies have all changed; Zaitseva herself describes 1999 critics attacking the "brightness" of the rebuilt colour [D].

**For a maker [A]:** a reconstruction is a translation; state what was kept (the drawing, the palette, the cut) and what was changed (the fabric, the light, the body).

### 4.3 Costume and the body: what costumes allowed and forbade

- **Forbade.** Panniers, tonnelets, heeled shoes and heavy headdresses kept 18th-century Opéra dancing close to the ground; Noverre complained of dancers "trapped" in them [D: Collins & Jarvis 2016]. Corsets limited the Imperial ballerina's torso until Korovin pushed for tunics [D: Churakova]. Schlemmer's sculptural costumes "constrain[ed] the spatial movements" by design [D: schlemmer.org]. Van Herpen's boots changed the line of the leg and made noise [D].
- **Allowed.** Sallé's muslin (1734) and the shorter skirts credited to Camargo let the feet be seen [P]. The Romantic bell skirt hid the leg's working and showed the pointe [P]. Karinska's bias-cut bodice, in six to fifteen panels, moved with the ribcage [D, see 03]. Sonnabend split a period skirt "to the waist to give the legs maximum freedom" in a MacMillan ballet [D: MacMillan estate].
- **Two readings.** (A) Design history as liberation: each generation freed the body further, up to the leotard. (B) A history of trade-offs: each freedom brought a new rule (pointe shoes, "flesh" tights, the slim uniform body), and recent designers have chosen constraint again (Forsythe, van Herpen) because resistance makes new movement [A for both; see 03 on the "technology of gender"].

### 4.4 Design and empire: the exotic costume

**Reading A: Orientalism.** The exotic look is an imperial gaze in fabric: ornament stands in for real places, non-European people are cast as sensual or cruel, and make-up turns caricature into convention (Said 1978; Garafola 1989; Chan 2020, all P in 03). Bakst's *Schéhérazade* (1910) set the pattern for Paris fashion (Poiret's vogue), and the costume was then sold back as luxury goods [see P B9].

**Reading B: exchange and self-fashioning.** Russian designers came from an empire that was itself an "Orient" to Paris; Jane Sharp's "Beyond Orientalism" (2007) reads Russian modernism from the empire's edge [P, see 04]. Companies outside Europe have used the same tools for national stories: Mexico (*La Coronela*, 1940, Posada-style masks), Azerbaijan (*Maiden Tower*, 1940), China (*Raise the Red Lantern*, 2001), Egypt (*Cleopatra*, 2019) [register]. Akram Khan and Tim Yip moved *Giselle*'s villagers to migrant garment workers in 2016 [D].

**Evidence to show both.** *Le Dieu bleu* (1912; V&A costume S.547-1978) and the Final Bow for Yellowface pledge (2017) on one page; *La Fille du Pharaon* beside the Cairo *Cleopatra*. **For a maker [A]:** research the place, credit the sources, and put people from that place on the team (the *Kabuki* credits list a Japanese dance teacher, Hanayagi Yoshijirō, beside a European designer [register]).

### 4.5 The designer's credit and the scene painter's anonymity

Scope 04 holds the core case (Sapunov, Shervashidze, Polunin, Muelle). New evidence from this pass:

- **The workshop behind the drawing.** The Met catalogues a Bakst sheet as by "Léon Bakst ... and/or workshop" [D]; LoC credits a design to the "Studio of Léon Bakst" [D, see 04].
- **The costumier named.** NGA Australia lists the *Sleeping Princess* (1921) costumiers as Pierre Pitoeff, Lovat Fraser, "Miss Norman" and Maison Muelle-Rossignol [D: NGA Australia, *Ballets Russes: The Art of Costume*]; NYCB credits *Danses concertantes* "Executed by Karinska (1944); Executed by Barbara Matera (1972)" [D]; Puissant's credits name the dyers ("Parveen Banga and ROH Dye Shop") [D: Puissant, *Electric Counterpoint*].
- **The co-creator removed.** Schlemmer's dancers Albert Burger and Elsa Hötzel received some of the *Triadic* costumes in 1923 because the work was judged a joint achievement, yet the ballet is known as Schlemmer's [D: Aoki 2008].
- **The uncredited artist.** Jasper Johns cut the *RainForest* leotards and is not in the original credit [D: Cunningham Trust]; Rambert's 2010 programme carried no costume credit at all [D: Rambert archive].
- **The union rule.** Chagall moved *Aleko* to Mexico City in 1942 partly because New York union rules would not let him paint his own backdrops; he and Bella then worked with "hundreds of local Mexican artisans" [D: LACMA Unframed 2017]. The painter-designer's wish to paint met the scene painters' trade protection.
- **The film credit.** Rodarte's *Black Swan* tutus and the costume designer's credit (2010–11) [D, see P].

**Two readings.** (A) The named designer is the author; executants follow instructions. (B) Design is a chain, and the credit line is a convention that serves the market and the museum [A, see 04 thesis]. **For a maker:** write the full credit for your own world, including dyers, painters and fabricators.

### 4.6 Famous failures, and what they teach

| Work | What went wrong | Source | Lesson [A] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gorsky's *Don Quixote*, Moscow 1900 (Korovin, Golovin) | Cool reception; designers not called out for applause; the director Telyakovsky "appalled" | [D: Koppers 2025] | A new look needs an audience prepared for it. |
| *The Sleeping Princess*, London 1921 (Bakst) | Bakst's six sets and nearly 300 costumes overran the budget; the run ended early; Stoll seized the sets and costumes; the company was barred from England until late 1924 | [D: LoC; London Museum; NGA Australia] | Couture-level making for every minor role can sink a company. **Contested detail:** the run is 105 consecutive performances (London Museum), 114 (NGA Australia) or 115 (LoC). |
| *Les Ballets 1933* (Tchelitchew, Derain, Bérard) | A single season; Caroline Hamilton calls it "a successful failure" | [D: Hamilton thesis 2022, see 04] | A season can fail commercially and still shape design for decades. |
| *Different Drummer*, Royal Ballet 1984 (Sonnabend) | Set dropped five days before the premiere | [D: Bristol] | When a concept changes late, the set is what goes. |
| NYCB Fall Fashion Gala 2012 (Valentino; Rodarte) | Critics said gowns and tutus hid the dancing; Rodarte's striped tops "looked blocky and didn't move well" | [D: Dance Magazine 2013; CriticalDance 2013] | Couture is designed to be seen still; ballet costume is designed to be seen moving. |
| *Neverwhere*, NYCB 2013 (van Herpen) | Costumes crackled audibly, discovered at the dress rehearsal | [D: Happel 2019] | Test sound and movement, not only look. Note it was also praised [D: Dance Magazine]. |

### 4.7 Two smaller disputes worth posing

- **Who is the designer of a Bausch piece?** Borzik and Pabst built the worlds in close partnership with Bausch; Pabst calls it "almost symbiotic" [D]. Is the peat floor of *Rite* a design or part of the choreography? [A, seminar question]
- **Is couture on stage design or sponsorship?** NYCB's gala was conceived to bring fashion designers to choreographers, with sponsors named [D, see P]; critics judged the results as design. Both readings hold [A].

## Gaps, surprises and checks for the human gate

**Not found in this pass.** An Ellsworth Kelly ballet design; a Japanese designer of a classical full-length ballet; African and Indian ballet designers; biographies of Egyptian designers (El Gharabawy); dates for Puissant, Hijikata Umeko, Fernández Ledesma, Bernal and Tim Yip at a primary source; Harvard Library's and the LoC's licence statements for specific digitised designs; the Mariinsky and Bakhrushin reproduction terms.

**Three surprises.**
1. The Met's Bakst designs are not downloadable on their record pages, despite Bakst's death in 1924; the scope files' "Met CC0 Bakst" plan needs changing (Part 2 finding).
2. Schlemmer's *Triadic Ballet*, the icon of single-author modernist design, was legally treated in 1923 as a joint work, and some costumes went to his dancer co-creators (Aoki 2008).
3. Chagall's *Aleko* premiered in Mexico City partly because a New York union would not let a painter paint his own scenery: the scene painters' trade defended itself against the painter-designer (LACMA 2017).

**Contested facts to resolve.** Lami's *Sylphide* credit; Ciceri's appointment year (1815/1818); the Opéra's earliest arc effect (1846/1849); the year of electric light at the Opéra (1880/1881); Boquet's title (1764/1765); *Sleeping Princess* run (105/114/115); Gerhart Bohner's *Triadic* date.
