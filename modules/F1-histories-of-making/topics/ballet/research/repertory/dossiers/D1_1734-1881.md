# D1: Repertory dossiers, 1734 to 1881

Pass D1 of the repertory brief (`../BRIEF_REPERTORY.md`), 21 November 2026. Eleven works from Marie Sallé's London *Pygmalion* to Manzotti's Milan *Excelsior*. Works already taken apart in `../../works/W1_court_to_imperial.md` (*La Sylphide*, *Giselle* and others) are referred to, not repeated.

## Pigmalion / Pygmalion (1734)
`id: pygmalion-salle-1734` · threads: T19, T12, T04 · confidence of the header: probable (date documented in Dacier 1909; music contested)

**Header.** Premiere 14 February 1734 (English Old Style calendar; 25 February in the French New Style), Covent Garden Theatre, London, in John Rich's company, as a dance "entertainment" inside a programme of plays [documented: Dacier 1909, appendix chronology, archive.org]. Choreography and scenario: Marie Sallé (1707 to 1756), French dancer [documented]. Music: often credited to Jean-Joseph Mouret, a French composer of stage music [contested: the credit appears in secondary sources only; no score was found in this pass]. Sets and costumes: no designer named; Sallé chose her own costume [documented: Mercure de France letter]. One act, a short ballet-pantomime of perhaps 15 to 20 minutes [probable]. Version seen today: none. The steps are lost; what survives is a printed description.

### In one line
A sculptor's statue comes to life and learns to dance from its maker, and the dancer who played the statue dressed as a statue, not as a lady of the court.

### How it came to be
Marie Sallé was the daughter of a fairground acrobat and grew up dancing at the Paris fairs and in London [documented: Universalis]. By 1733 she was a star of the Paris Opéra, but she quarrelled with its management and with the rivalry of Marie Camargo, a dancer famous for fast footwork [probable: Britannica]. In October 1733 she left Paris for London, where the manager John Rich ran a commercial playhouse with no state rules about what a dancer could wear or do [documented: Dacier; *Costume* journal article, 2016]. She opened at Covent Garden on 8 November 1733 [documented: Dacier]. On 14 February 1734 she gave her own short ballet on the Pygmalion story, with herself as the statue [documented: Dacier]. It ran for weeks. On 26 March she added a second ballet, *Bacchus and Ariadne*, in which she played a tragic heroine [documented: Dacier]. Her benefit night, when the takings went to her, was taken over by the royal family and the court [documented: Mercure letter]. A London correspondent sent a long, admiring account to the Paris monthly *Mercure de France*, and it is that letter, not any score or notation, that makes the work known [documented]. Paris never let her stage it there [probable: Potter 2013].

### Roots and reference points
- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* book X.** The sculptor Pygmalion carves an ivory woman, falls in love with her, and Venus brings her to life [documented]. The name "Galatea" for the statue is a later, eighteenth-century addition, not Ovid's [probable].
- **French opera-ballet.** Sallé trained in the *belle danse*, the noble style of the Paris Opéra, under Françoise Prévost, and had danced without masks as early as 1729 [documented: Britannica].
- **English pantomime.** Rich's theatre lived on wordless pantomime, so a London audience already knew how to read a story told by gesture [documented: *Costume* 2016, citing the commercial theatre]. John Weaver's *The Loves of Mars and Venus* (Drury Lane, 1717) is the earlier London attempt at a dance drama without words [probable].
- **Classical sculpture.** Her costume copied "the model of a Greek statue" [documented: Mercure]. The idea of the statue that wakes was a favourite of the age; it answered a philosophical question about how the senses make a mind (Condillac's statue, 1754, comes later) [argued].

### The world it built
The *Mercure* letter gives the plot in order [documented: translated in Sutherland Edwards, *History of the Opera*, Project Gutenberg]. Pygmalion enters his studio with his pupils, who dance "chisel and mallet in hand". He has a curtain drawn back to show more statues; one, in the centre, holds every eye. He sighs, touches its feet and waist, puts bracelets on its arms and diamonds at its neck, kisses its hands. He prays to Venus. The statue comes alive slowly, "astonished" at itself and at the world. Then Pygmalion dances before her "as if to instruct her", and she repeats his steps, "from the easiest to the most difficult" [documented].

That last passage is the core idea: the dance is the statue's education. The maker teaches movement, the made thing learns it and then feels the love that drives it. Susan Au says the choreography gave "the impression of a danced conversation" [documented as quoted: Wikipedia, citing Au, *Ballet and Modern Dance*]. Movement was expressive gesture joined to the noble steps of the Opéra [probable]. The music is lost or unidentified [not found in this pass].

### Design and art
The costume is the famous thing. The *Mercure* says she dared to appear "without basket, without skirt, without a dress, in her natural hair, and with no ornament on her head" [documented]. Over her bodice and under-petticoat she wore only "a simple robe of muslin", draped like a Greek statue [documented]. The "basket" is the pannier, the hooped frame that held a court skirt out sideways. Muslin is fine, light, white cotton, then imported from India [probable: Potter 2013]. So the look meant three things at once: it was antique (the statue), it was natural (her own hair), and it let the body be seen moving. No picture of the costume survives [documented: Potter 2013]. A 1734 portrait print shows Sallé in a conventional blue and white Opéra costume, which makes the contrast vivid [probable: Potter 2013].

The set was a sculptor's studio with a curtain at the back and a row of statues [documented: Mercure]. No designer is named.

### Premiere and reception
Sallé danced Galatea; the playbill names Mr Malter (probably Pygmalion) and M. Dupré among the dancers [probable: playbill quoted by Walton, from an older source]. The *Mercure* correspondent called it "this ingenious ballet" and spoke of "prodigious success" [documented]. Her benefit was sold out "for a month past" [documented]. One later account says guineas wrapped in banknotes rained on the stage and the takings passed £8,000; that sum is not credible for one night and should be treated as legend [contested: Walton blog, from a nineteenth-century source]. On 24 May 1734 the last performance was cancelled because, says Rich's own register, "Madem Sallé wou'd not come to the House" [documented: QUB Dance Biographies blog, quoting the register].

### Versions and lineage
No notation survives, and Sallé never revived it in Paris [probable]. The subject did live on: Barbara Campanini, "la Barbarina", danced a *Pygmalion* at the Prussian court, painted by Antoine Pesne at Sanssouci [documented: Dacier's footnote]. Rameau's one-act opera-ballet *Pigmalion* (Paris, 1748) and Rousseau's spoken "scène lyrique" *Pygmalion* (written 1762, staged 1770) carry the story on [probable]. Modern dancers have made new versions to period music; none claims to reconstruct hers [probable]. Sallé's Covent Garden season is sometimes called the first recorded ballet in London, which is wrong: there was dance in London theatres for decades before [argued].

### Readings and disputes
- **A reform before Noverre.** Ivor Guest and Britannica present Sallé as the forerunner of the *ballet d'action*, the story ballet that Jean-Georges Noverre argued for in his *Lettres sur la danse* (1760) (see W1 §3) [probable]. Noverre himself praised her [documented: Britannica].
- **A professional woman managing her image.** Sarah McCleave, "Marie Sallé, a Wise Professional Woman of Influence" (in Lynn Matluck Brooks, ed., *Women's Work: Making Dance in Europe before 1800*, 2007) and *Dance in Handel's London Operas* (2013), reads her choices as a careful public persona: the chaste, antique heroine against the old link between dancers and prostitution [documented as cited: Curzon 2015].
- **Was the costume new?** One scholar notes that the English dancer Hester Santlow had appeared "her hair loose" as Diana in 1723 [documented: *Costume* 2016]. So "the first uncorseted dancer" is a myth of priority; Sallé made the gesture famous because a French journal printed it [argued].
- **Was it a scandal?** Some sources say the costume "roused shock"; the *Mercure* letter is all praise [contested].
- **The date puzzle.** The *Mercure* letter is dated 15 March and says the ballet had run "nearly two months". Dacier's chronology gives a premiere of 14 February [documented]. England used the Julian calendar until 1752, eleven days behind France, so the benefit "on the first of April" in the letter is Dacier's 21 March [argued: the arithmetic fits]. Even so, "nearly two months" does not fit a February opening [contested].

### Facts worth telling
1. The only full account of the ballet is a letter in a French magazine [documented: Mercure de France, 1734].
2. Sallé's costume was a muslin shift over her underwear, with her own hair down [documented].
3. In the ballet the statue learns to dance by copying her maker, step by step [documented].
4. The register says Rich had to send an audience home when Sallé refused to come [documented: QUB blog].
5. England and France were on different calendars in 1734, so the ballet has two dates [argued].
6. Sallé is often called the earliest known woman to choreograph a ballet she danced in [probable: Britannica; "earliest known" per the rules].
7. Her student Noverre said she practised every day after retiring [probable: Wikipedia].

### Set it beside
- **coppelia-1870** (W1 §6): the woman pretends to be the doll; in Sallé the woman is the statue who becomes a woman. Both are about the maker and the made, but Coppélia mocks the maker.
- **apollon-musagete-1928**: Balanchine's god teaches muses their arts, as Pygmalion teaches the statue; and the Greek tunic returns as a modernist costume.
- **pas-de-quatre-1845** (this file): the star dancer as author of her own image, a century on.
- **medee-et-jason-1763** (W1 §3): Noverre's tragic ballet d'action, the reform Sallé is said to anticipate.

### What a maker can take from it
- A costume can carry the whole concept. One change, from pannier to muslin, told the audience what the world was (antique, natural, alive).
- Make the learning visible. The best passage shows the created thing acquiring its powers in front of us.
- Go where the rules are looser. Sallé made her boldest work in a commercial house abroad, not at the state opera at home.

### Beyond the stage
The Pygmalion story runs on through Rousseau, W. S. Gilbert's *Pygmalion and Galatea* (1871), Shaw's *Pygmalion* (1913) and *My Fair Lady* (1956, film 1964) [probable]. Sallé's own afterlife is mostly in writing on dance dress and in Handel studies, since Handel wrote the *Terpsichore* prologue (1734) and dances in *Ariodante* and *Alcina* for her [documented: Handel Friends 2022].

## Le Ballet de la paille, ou Il n'est qu'un pas du mal au bien; La Fille mal gardée / The Wayward Daughter (1789; 1828; 1885; 1960)
`id: fille-mal-gardee-1789` and `id: fille-mal-gardee-ashton-1960` · threads: T07, T11, T14, T13 · confidence of the header: documented (1789, 1828, 1885 and 1960 premieres); 1789 music contested

**Header.** One work, four main forms.
- *1789.* 1 July 1789, Grand Théâtre de Bordeaux; choreography and scenario Jean Dauberval (1742 to 1806), pupil of Noverre; music a pastiche of popular French airs, the arranger unknown or Franz Ignaz Beck [contested]; Lise (then "Lison") danced by Dauberval's wife, Marie-Madeleine Crespé, known as Mlle Théodore [documented: Petipa Society]. Designer not recorded. Two acts.
- *1828.* 17 November 1828, Paris Opéra, Salle Le Peletier; staging Jean-Pierre Aumer, another Dauberval pupil; new score by Ferdinand Hérold, partly built from older tunes and Rossini; Lise Pauline Montessu [documented: Petipa Society; Theater Basel].
- *1864 and 1885.* Paul Taglioni, Berlin Court Opera, 7 November 1864, new score by Peter Ludwig Hertel [documented: Staatsballett Berlin]. Marius Petipa and Lev Ivanov, Mariinsky Theatre, St Petersburg, 16/28 December 1885 (Old Style / New Style), Hertel's score, for Virginia Zucchi; Russian title *Tshchetnaya predostorozhnost* ("The Vain Precaution") [documented: Petipa Society].
- *1960.* 28 January 1960, Royal Opera House, London, Royal Ballet; choreography Frederick Ashton; Hérold's 1828 music "freely adapted and arranged" by John Lanchbery; sets, front cloth and costumes Osbert Lancaster; lighting now credited to John B. Read; two acts, three scenes, about 1 hour 45 minutes with interval [documented: Ashton Foundation; ROH Collections; RBO]. Ashton's is the version most seen today.

### In one line
A farmer's daughter and the young farmer she loves outwit her mother, who wants her married to a rich man's foolish son: ballet's earliest known surviving comedy of ordinary people.

### How it came to be
Jean Dauberval was ballet master at Bordeaux, a rich wine port with a new theatre (the Grand Théâtre, 1780) [probable]. The story goes that he saw, in a print-seller's window, an engraving after Pierre-Antoine Baudouin's picture *La Réprimande* (also called *Une jeune fille querellée par sa mère*), in which a mother scolds a tousled girl while her lover slips away up a ladder to the hayloft [probable: RBO "apparently"; the shop story is tradition]. He built a two-act comic ballet around it and called it *The Ballet of the Straw, or There Is Only One Step from Bad to Good*. It opened on 1 July 1789, two weeks before the fall of the Bastille [documented: date]. In 1791 Dauberval staged it at the Pantheon in London, where it first took the title *La Fille mal gardée* [documented: Theater Basel; medici.tv]. His pupils carried it on. Charles Didelot took it to St Petersburg (1818) [documented]; Aumer re-made it for the Paris Opéra in 1828 with a fresh Hérold score, because the Opéra had a larger company and wanted a full ballet [probable: Jacq-Mioche lecture, 2012]. The Russian branch grew from Taglioni's Berlin version. In 1959 Tamara Karsavina, who had danced Lise in St Petersburg, urged Ashton to make his own, and the historian Ivor Guest pointed him to Hérold's forgotten score [documented: RBO; Barnes].

### Roots and reference points
- **The genre print.** Baudouin's gouache (dated 1764 or 1765 in sources; V&A names an engraving by Pierre-Philippe Choffard after it) was a saucy bedroom-and-barn picture sold widely as a print [contested on date; documented that Guest identified the engraving: V&A]. The ballet cleaned up its erotic joke into courtship comedy [argued].
- **Opéra-comique and the vaudeville air.** Dauberval's music borrowed familiar tunes, so that each melody carried its words in the audience's head, the practice called the *air parlant* [probable; see G1 for borrowed-tune scores].
- **Noverre's ballet d'action** (W1 §3): a whole story told by gesture. Dauberval took the method and pointed it at farmers instead of gods [argued].
- **The 1828 score** quotes Rossini, including the storm from *La Cenerentola*, and later Paris revivals added a pas de deux to Donizetti's *L'elisir d'amore* for Fanny Elssler (1837) [documented: Mark Aspen review; RBO].

### The world it built
The farm is the world. Ashton's version opens in a farmyard at dawn with a cockerel and four hens; Lise and Colas meet secretly, and the Widow Simone, played by a man in skirts, chases him off [documented: Ashton Foundation cast list]. A harvest scene follows, with a maypole, a flute-playing Alain with his red umbrella, a picnic and a storm that blows everyone home. Act II is the farmhouse: Lise alone mimes her dream of marriage and babies, Colas pops out of the haystack, the notary arrives, Alain finds the lovers in the bedroom, and Simone gives in [probable: standard synopsis].

Signature passages, mostly Ashton's:
- **The ribbon.** A pink ribbon is passed between the lovers in Act I and wound into a cat's cradle; in the "Elssler" pas de deux eight friends hold ribbons like spokes while Lise turns on pointe [documented: Critical Dance 2016; Mark Aspen 2025]. The ribbon motif goes back at least to the 1803 Paris libretto, where Lise "takes a ribbon from her bodice and hangs it on a tree" [documented as quoted: waballetomaniac].
- **The clog dance** for Widow Simone, to Hertel's music, after Ashton took Lanchbery to watch Lancashire clog dancers [documented: G1 §S4; Mark Aspen].
- **"When I'm married"**, Lise's mime of her future, taught to Ashton by Karsavina from her St Petersburg memory [documented: Barnes; Petipa Society].
- Folk dances from the Royal Ballet School's English folk syllabus: maypole and stick dance [probable: waballetomaniac].

Music: Lanchbery's score is a patchwork of Hérold, a few 1789 airs, Hertel's clog dance, Donizetti and his own linking passages, tied together by recurring motifs "in the manner used by Adolphe Adam in *Giselle*" [documented: Barnes].

### Design and art
Osbert Lancaster, cartoonist of the *Daily Express* and an architectural writer, designed Ashton's production [documented: V&A]. He aimed at "the freshness and naïveté of Images d'Epinal", the cheap coloured popular prints of nineteenth-century France, and at the French children's illustrator Louis-Maurice Boutet de Monvel [documented: Lancaster quoted in Guest 1960, via V&A]. So the look is a picture book: flat, bright cut-out farm buildings, a front cloth, pastel petticoats and striped stockings. A real Shetland or Welsh pony pulls Lise and Alain's cart [documented: DanceTabs; Mark Aspen]. The hen and cockerel costumes were made by Alick Shanks [documented: V&A]. Ashton's own image was of Suffolk, "the suspended stillness of a Constable landscape" [documented: RBO]. Note the double source: an English pastoral dream drawn in French popular style [argued].

The 2015 Ekaterinburg version took the opposite route: a Russian 1885 ballet shown as if the Imperial Directorate had hired Van Gogh, with sets after his Arles paintings and a final scene before his *Irises* [documented: Ural Opera Ballet].

### Premiere and reception
1789: Mlle Théodore as Lison, Eugène Hus as Colin, François Le Riche as the mother, called Ragotte [documented: Petipa Society]. No review found in this pass. 1885: Zucchi's acting made the "When I'm married" scene famous; Italian stars had arrived in St Petersburg at the request, it is said, of Alexander III [probable]. 1960: Nadia Nerina (Lise), David Blair (Colas), Stanley Holden (Simone), Alexander Grant (Alain), Leslie Edwards (Thomas) [documented: Ashton Foundation]. It was made in "a whirlwind month of rehearsals" and won instant success [documented as quoted: Ashton Foundation]. Luke Jennings later called it "probably the best-loved of English ballets" [documented as quoted]. The BBC filmed it in 1962 [documented: NYPL record].

### Versions and lineage
1789 Bordeaux → 1791 London → 1800 Moscow (Solomoni) → 1803 Paris libretto → 1818 Berlin (Michel-François Hoguet; the Staatsballett Berlin page says "to Hérold's music", which cannot be right, since Hérold's score dates from 1828) [contested] → 1818 St Petersburg (Didelot, music Cavos) → 1828 Paris (Aumer, Hérold) → 1864 Berlin (P. Taglioni, Hertel) → 1885 St Petersburg (Petipa and Ivanov, Hertel; notated in Stepanov notation 1903 to 1906, Sergeyev Collection, Harvard) → 1903 Moscow (Gorsky, adding Drigo, Delibes, Pugni, Minkus, Rubinstein) → 1912 London (Pavlova's one-act) → 1940 New York (Nijinska for Ballet Theatre) → 1960 London (Ashton) → 1989 Kirov (Vinogradov) → 2015 Ekaterinburg (Sergei Vikharev, from the Harvard notation, with gaps filled in period style) [documented: Petipa Society; RBO; Ural Opera; Barnes]. Two families of score now coexist: Hérold-Lanchbery in the West, Hertel in the Russian line [documented: Theater Basel]. Ashton's version has replaced older ones at the Paris Opéra, the Bolshoi and ABT [documented: DanceTabs]. It is the version licensed by the Ashton Foundation.

### Readings and disputes
- **A ballet of the Revolution.** Russian and French writers stress that it put the Third Estate on stage two weeks before the Bastille: "real people" in place of knights and goddesses [documented: Belyaeva, Belcanto.ru 2015; Muzobozrenie 2015]. Sylvie Jacq-Mioche (Paris Opéra school lecture, 2012) adds that it showed "a fairly realistic peasant universe" for the first time [documented as reported].
- **A ballet of property.** Against that, it is a comedy about a marriage contract between farmers who own land and vines; the "peasants" are prosperous, and the poor are not on stage [argued]. Both readings hold: it is bourgeois realism, not revolution.
- **English or French?** Ashton's pastoral is Suffolk; Lancaster's drawing is French popular print; the steps are partly Russian through Karsavina. Clive Barnes, "La Fille mal Gardée in Perspective" (*Dance and Dancers*, reprinted ballet.co.uk), traces how close the Ivanov links are [documented].
- **Contradictions.** The 1789 music: "popular airs" (most sources) or arranged by Franz Ignaz Beck (Theater Basel programme, 2021) [contested]. Baudouin's picture is dated 1764 (V&A, RBO), 1765 (Basel) or wrongly 1789 (Petipa Society) [contested]. RBO's "Aumer's version in 1823" conflicts with the 1828 date given everywhere else [contested; 1828 is right]. Medici.tv says Hérold's score reached Moscow in 1845 [probable]. "Oldest ballet" is true only of the story and title; no step of 1789 is known to survive [argued].
- **Myth.** The "chicken incident" of 1905, in which Kschessinska is said to have sabotaged her rival Preobrazhenskaya's début, is theatre legend [documented as legend: Petipa Society].

### Facts worth telling
1. It premiered 1 July 1789, fourteen days before the Bastille fell [documented].
2. Its first title was *The Ballet of the Straw* [documented].
3. Widow Simone is always a man in a dress, a pantomime-dame tradition kept in Russia too [documented: Ural Opera].
4. A real pony pulls a cart across the stage in Ashton's version [documented].
5. A Donizetti pas de deux was found as a violin part in a box at the Paris Opéra [documented: G1 §S4].
6. Ashton went to watch Lancashire clog dancers before making the clog dance [documented].
7. The 1885 Russian version survives in notation at Harvard and was revived in 2015 [documented].

### Set it beside
- **giselle-1841** (W1 §5): the same village and harvest festival turned to tragedy and ghosts.
- **coppelia-1870** (W1 §6): the next great comic village ballet, with a machine at its centre.
- **bright-stream-1935**: a Soviet collective-farm comedy that also hides lovers in disguise; the state replaces the mother.
- **estancia-1952**: another rural comedy built from national folk dance, Argentine instead of English.

### What a maker can take from it
- A whole world can grow from one picture. A scolding mother and a hidden lover gave the plot, the props (straw, ladder, loft) and the tone.
- Props can carry the love story. The ribbon is passed, tied, stretched into a cat's cradle and spun into a maypole, so one object charts a relationship.
- A work can be renewed by changing its parts while keeping its frame: four scores and dozens of choreographers, one story.

### Beyond the stage
The BBC film (1962) and the Royal Ballet's later recordings sell widely [documented: NYPL; RBO]. The clog dance is a fixture of light-music radio and brass-band arrangements [probable]. The RBO shop sells a "Fille" merchandise line [documented: RBO page]. Vikharev's Van Gogh version won six Golden Mask nominations in 2016 [documented: Kommersant via Ural Opera].

## Napoli, eller Fiskeren og hans Brud / Napoli, or The Fisherman and His Bride (1842)
`id: napoli-1842` · threads: T01, T05, T14, T21 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 29 March 1842, Royal Theatre (Det Kongelige Teater), Copenhagen, Royal Danish Ballet [documented: Royal Theatre repertoire database, danskforfatterleksikon.dk]. Choreography and scenario August Bournonville (1805 to 1879), the company's ballet master. Music by four Danish composers: Edvard Helsted (Act I and part of III), Holger Simon Paulli (Act III, with the tarantella), Niels W. Gade (Act II, the Blue Grotto), Hans Christian Lumbye (the closing galop) [documented: Wikipedia; Heinz-Bosl-Stiftung]. Scenery C. F. Christensen, Royal Theatre painter [documented: "The Grand Tour of Europe", *Nordic Theatre Studies*, 2021]. Costumes: designer not found in this pass. Three acts ("tableaux": Santa Lucia; the Blue Grotto; the pilgrimage to Madonna dell'Arco), about 1 hour 45 minutes [documented: repertoire database; Opus Arte DVD timing]. Version most seen today: the Royal Danish Ballet's 2009 staging by Nikolaj Hübbe and Sorella Englund, set in the 1950s, with a new Act II [documented]; and Act III alone as a divertissement, worldwide.

### In one line
A Neapolitan fisherman brings his drowned bride back from a sea-demon's grotto by faith, and the town dances their wedding.

### How it came to be
In March 1841, during a performance in Copenhagen, part of the audience hissed Bournonville. He turned to King Christian VIII in the royal box and asked, from the stage, whether he should go on [probable: standard account; Alison's Studio 2017; Ballet Arizona calls it "insulting" the king]. Speaking to the monarch from the stage broke court etiquette, and he was sent away on leave for some months [probable]. He used the time to travel to Paris, Naples and Milan [documented: Jürgensen (ed.), *Dance Chronicle* 2002, letters of 1841]. In Paris he saw *Giselle* (premiered June 1841, W1 §5), which he did not much like [probable]. In Naples he watched the fishermen's quarter of Santa Lucia, the street sellers, the processions and the shrines [documented: GBOpera 2023; Ballet Arizona]. On a long, dull coach journey he hummed the tune that became the first three sections of the tarantella [documented: Bournonville's memoirs, *My Theatre Life*, as cited by Wikipedia]. Back in Copenhagen, forgiven, he made *Napoli* in a few months. It played nineteen times before the end of May 1842 [documented: repertoire database], and it has never left the Danish repertory.

### Roots and reference points
- **Travel and the Grand Tour.** Danish painters working in Italy had already given Bournonville *The Festival in Albano* (1839), made before he had been there, with Christensen's sets drawn from his own Italian journey [documented: *Nordic Theatre Studies* 2021].
- **Giselle.** *Napoli* follows the Giselle pattern of a real village act, a supernatural act and a return, but makes the spirit realm blue, not white, and ends in a wedding, not a grave [argued; see the Orpheus comparison in ConcertoNet 2012].
- **Naples's own ballet.** The grotto scene borrowed from a similar one in Salvatore Taglioni's *Il Duca di Ravenna* (San Carlo, 1830) [documented as claimed: GBOpera 2023, Maria Venuso].
- **Rossini.** Peppo's slander scene parodies the "calumny" aria in *The Barber of Seville*; the folk song *Te voglio bene assai* marks the lovers [documented: Ballet Arizona].
- **Catholic devotion.** The Madonna's image, the monk Fra Ambrosio and the hymn *O sanctissima* carry the rescue; for Lutheran Copenhagen this was local colour [documented: Ballet Arizona; argued].
- **The tarantella**, the fast Neapolitan couple dance in 6/8 time, with tambourines [probable].

### The world it built
Three acts, three worlds [documented: repertoire database].
- **Act I, Santa Lucia.** A crowded quay: Teresina's widowed mother Veronica pushes two old suitors, Peppo the lemonade seller and Giacomo the macaroni seller; Gennaro the fisherman returns; street singers and a puppeteer perform; a storm sinks the lovers' boat, and only Gennaro is found [documented: Wikipedia synopsis]. Mostly mime.
- **Act II, the Blue Grotto.** Golfo, a sea-spirit, has turned Teresina into a naiad who has forgotten her past. Gennaro arrives with a picture of the Madonna; she remembers, turns human, and Golfo, defeated, gives them treasure [documented].
- **Act III, the pilgrimage.** The town suspects Gennaro of witchcraft; the monk clears him; then comes the long dance suite: the pas de six, solos, and the tarantella with the whole company, ending in Lumbye's galop [documented].

The movement language is the Bournonville style: quick, buoyant jumps, beaten steps, soft arms held low, and an even, smiling calm in the upper body however hard the feet are working [probable]. The men dance as much as the women, unlike in Paris at the time [probable]. The Danish children's story is part of it: as one former dancer puts it, "we all stood on the bridge waving in the third Act" and then moved up through the roles [documented as quoted: MusicWeb review of Opus Arte DVD].

### Design and art
C. F. Christensen painted the sets after his own Grand Tour [documented]. For Act II the 1842 stage instructions describe wings, a new border and a "cut out cave drop" that together make "the dark part of the so called Blue Grotto" [documented: *Nordic Theatre Studies*]. A painting of the grotto by Christensen in the Music Museum, Stockholm, matches the set [documented]. The same study argues that Christensen borrowed from Paris Opéra painters (Philastre and Cambon) and from the Bolognese Antonio Basoli, then made it his own [documented]. Vesuvius smokes on the painted backcloth of Act I [probable]. The 2009 production by Maja Ravn moved the costumes to the 1950s: circle skirts, sailors, fishermen in cut-off jeans, a nod to Italian post-war cinema and Fellini [documented: Dance Enthusiast 2019; ConcertoNet; Kulturinformation]. Famous images: lithographs of the tarantella with sashes by Gaetano Dura (Biblioteca Nazionale, Naples) [documented as named: GBOpera]; photograph of Richard Jensen and Grethe Ditlevsen in the pas de six, 1909 [documented: Wikipedia].

### Premiere and reception
Bournonville danced Gennaro himself; Caroline Fjeldsted was Teresina; Andreas Füssel was Golfo [documented: repertoire database cast list]. Twenty performances in the first spring [documented]. Act II has been called dull "ever since its premiere" [documented as claimed: Dance Enthusiast 2019]. No 1842 critic's words found in this pass; the database lists reviews from 1849 in *Berlingske Tidende* and *Thalia* [documented].

### Versions and lineage
The Danes have kept it in a line of teaching from Bournonville to Hans Beck (ballet master 1894 to 1915), who added solos to the pas de six danced from 1944 to 1956; Hans Brenaa added an Act II pas de deux from 1963 [documented: repertoire database]. Dinna Bjørn rechoreographed Act II in 1992 [documented: Lex]. Hübbe and Englund (2009) replaced Gade's Act II music with a new score by Louise Alenius and made new choreography for it, while keeping about 35 minutes of Act I and 38 of Act III in Bournonville's steps [documented: MusicWeb]. The Royal Ballet has danced the Act III divertissement since 3 May 1962 [documented: ROH Collections]. Ballet Arizona gave the earliest known full US staging in 2015 with Danish sets [documented as the company's claim]. Emil Hansen wrote down several Bournonville ballets in his own notation system [documented: *Dance Chronicle* 2005].

### Readings and disputes
- **The national ballet.** Erik Aschengreen (Lex, *Den Store Danske*) reads *Napoli* as the "optimism and the bright side of Danish Romanticism", the company's "calling card" [documented]. The ballet is "said to be Denmark's national ballet" [documented: Ballet Arizona], though it is set in Italy.
- **A reply to Giselle.** Several writers read it as Bournonville's answer to Paris: realism over the ideal, marriage over death, a balanced company over the star ballerina [probable: Alison's Studio; Heinz-Bosl-Stiftung]. The Bosl essay argues that by splitting the two worlds into three acts and weighting the real one, it points ahead to Petipa [documented as argued].
- **Faithful heritage or living work?** Critics divided over 2009: Kulturinformation found the new Act II gave "an extra fresh dimension"; the Dance Enthusiast thought it "calls into question the entire enterprise of presenting the 'complete' ballet" [documented].
- **Contradictions.** Performance totals disagree: 555 to 1975 (repertoire database: 219 to 1889 and 336 to 1975), "more than 700" (Lex), "more than 950" (*Nordic Theatre Studies*, 2021) [contested; the figures may count Act III alone differently]. Whether the king "banned" Bournonville or granted leave is told both ways [contested]. GBOpera notes that his own writings build "an image of himself that the archive documents sometimes contradict" [documented as argued].

### Facts worth telling
1. Bournonville spoke to the king from the stage, was sent away, and came back with this ballet [probable].
2. Four composers wrote one score, act by act [documented].
3. He hummed the tarantella on a coach journey [documented: his memoirs].
4. The supernatural act is blue, not white: the Blue Grotto of Capri [documented].
5. A holy picture of the Madonna, not a sword, defeats the sea-demon [documented].
6. Danish ballet children start on the bridge in Act III and can end as Teresina or Gennaro [documented: dancer's account].
7. Act II was replaced with new music and steps in 2009 [documented].

### Set it beside
- **giselle-1841** (W1 §5): one year earlier; white ghosts and death in Paris against a blue grotto and marriage in Copenhagen.
- **sylphide-bournonville-1836** (W1 §4): Bournonville's other survivor, where the hero who follows the spirit is destroyed.
- **don-quixote-1869** (this file): another southern city square full of vendors and a wedding, seen from Moscow.
- **petrushka-1911**: a crowd scene of street performers and vendors that turns the fair into the subject itself.

### What a maker can take from it
- Build from field notes. The sellers, monk and puppeteer came from what Bournonville saw on the quay; specific types make a crowd believable.
- Recolour the borrowed form. He took the Giselle structure and changed the colour (blue), the agent (faith) and the end (a wedding), and it became his own.
- Give the place a ritual. The final pilgrimage and tarantella let the whole community dance, so the world ends as a celebration of itself.

### Beyond the stage
The Opus Arte DVD (recorded 2014) circulates widely [documented]. The tarantella and Lumbye's galop are concert pieces in Denmark [probable]. Hans Christian Andersen, Bournonville's friend, is said to have urged the Italian trip [documented as claimed: Giornale della Danza 2024].

## La Esmeralda / Esmeralda (1844)
`id: esmeralda-1844` · threads: T18, T05, T21 · confidence of the header: documented (premiere, cast, design credits); act structure contested

**Header.** Premiere 9 March 1844, Her Majesty's Theatre, London, under the manager Benjamin Lumley [documented: Petipa Society; Wikipedia]. Choreography and scenario Jules Perrot (1810 to 1892), the theatre's ballet master and himself a dancer; music Cesare Pugni; sets William Grieve; costumes Mme Copère; machinery D. Sloman [documented: Pugni critical edition, Cambridge Scholars, introduction]. After Victor Hugo, *Notre-Dame de Paris* (1831). Three acts and five scenes, or one act in five tableaux in London [contested: Wikipedia against the IMSLP preface]. Version most seen today: in Russia, Vladimir Burmeister's 1950 Moscow production (Stanislavsky and Nemirovich-Danchenko Music Theatre), about 2 hours 50 minutes [documented: MAMT], and the Bolshoi's 2009 reconstruction by Yuri Burlaka and Vasily Medvedev; in the West, only excerpts: the Esmeralda pas de deux, the pas de six, and the "Diana and Actaeon" pas de deux, which was not in the original [documented: Wikipedia].

### In one line
A Romani street dancer in medieval Paris is loved by a poet, a soldier, a priest and a bell-ringer, framed for murder and saved at the gallows: Hugo's tragedy with a happy ending.

### How it came to be
By 1844 Jules Perrot, once the most admired male dancer in Paris, had been pushed out of the Opéra, where male dancing was falling from favour and where his choreography for Carlotta Grisi in *Giselle* (1841) went largely uncredited (see W1 §5) [probable]. In London, Benjamin Lumley ran Her Majesty's Theatre, the opera house of the aristocratic season, and hired Perrot to make new ballets [documented]. Hugo's novel was the bestseller of the age, and Hugo himself had already turned it into a libretto, *La Esmeralda*, for the composer Louise Bertin (Paris Opéra, 1836) [probable]. Perrot took Hugo's title and cast his former partner Grisi as the heroine, with himself as the poet Gringoire and the young Arthur Saint-Léon as Captain Phoebus [documented]. The ballet was a hit; Grisi danced it until she had to return to Paris for *Giselle* in May, and Fanny Elssler took it over at the end of the season [documented: Petipa Society]. Perrot staged it at La Scala for Elssler months later, and in St Petersburg in December 1848 / January 1849, again with Elssler, after he became ballet master there [documented]. In Russia it never left the repertory.

### Roots and reference points
- **Hugo's novel** (1831): medieval Paris seen around Notre-Dame cathedral; Esmeralda, her goat, the Court of Miracles where beggars and thieves rule, the archdeacon Claude Frollo, the deaf bell-ringer Quasimodo [documented].
- **Hugo and Bertin's opera** (1836), which already cut and simplified the novel for the stage [probable].
- **The Romantic "Spanish" and "gypsy" dancer**: Elssler's *cachucha* (in *Le Diable boiteux*, 1836) made the dark, fiery southern woman a ballet type [probable]. Esmeralda inherits it.
- **Melodrama** of the London stage: the gallows, the rescue in the nick of time [argued].
- The Gothic revival in architecture and painting, which Hugo's novel had helped launch [probable].

### The world it built
The ballet keeps the crowd and the city and drops the end. Gringoire falls among the beggars of the Court of Miracles and is about to be hanged; Esmeralda saves him by "marrying" him by gypsy law, a marriage she tells him is only on paper [documented: Petipa Society; Spanish Wikipedia]. Frollo orders Quasimodo to seize her; Phoebus rescues her and she falls for him. At Fleur-de-Lys's engagement party, Phoebus's fiancée, Esmeralda dances and is shamed. Frollo stabs Phoebus and Esmeralda is accused. At the gallows Phoebus, who is not dead, reappears and the crowd turns on Frollo [documented: Pugni edition synopsis]. In Hugo she is hanged. Perrot's ending is the kind of rescue the audience wanted [argued].

The dances serve the story, which is Perrot's signature: a *pas d'action*, a dance in which the plot moves forward while people dance. The famous example is the "pas de deux" for Esmeralda and Gringoire, where she teaches him to dance and keeps him at arm's length [probable]. The Esmeralda solo with a tambourine, still a competition favourite, belongs to this line [probable; Wikipedia notes variations at competitions]. Pugni's music is quick, tuneful and theatrical; when the ballet moved to Russia he expanded it to fill a whole evening, and Riccardo Drigo later added numbers, including the pas de six for Virginia Zucchi in 1886 [documented: IMSLP preface; Wikipedia].

### Design and art
William Grieve, of a family of London scene painters, made the sets: the cathedral square, the beggars' den, the Gothic interior [documented: credit; probable: subject]. Costume credits go to Mme Copère, who also danced Aloisa [documented]. The Russian revivals were huge: for 1899 six painters each took a scene, including Orest Allegri, Vasily Shiryaev and Leonid Andreev [documented: Petipa Society]. Burmeister's 1950 production was designed by Alexander Lushin, revived in 2009 by Valery Leventhal [documented: MAMT]. Famous images: lithographs of Grisi and of Fanny Cerrito "in the title role" (c. 1844) [documented: Wikipedia caption]; photographs of Zucchi (1886) and of the young Agrippina Vaganova as Esmeralda (1910) [documented: Spanish Wikipedia captions].

### Premiere and reception
Cast 1844: Grisi (Esmeralda), Perrot (Gringoire), Saint-Léon (Phoebus), Louis-François Gosselin (Frollo), Antoine-Louis Coulon (Quasimodo), Adelaide Frassi (Fleur-de-Lys) [documented]. The Pugni edition calls it "reasonably successful" and reports that Grisi was pronounced the perfect blend of Cerrito's gaiety, Elssler's coquetry and Taglioni's "ineffable poetry" [documented as reported]. Grisi took two of its variations into Mazilier's *Le Diable à quatre* the next year [documented]. In Russia Elssler made it a personal triumph and chose it for her farewell in Moscow in March 1851 [documented]. Zucchi in 1886 was said to shed real tears in the execution scene [documented as "allegedly": IMSLP preface]. Money figures not found in this pass.

### Versions and lineage
1844 London (Perrot) → 1844 Milan (Perrot, Elssler) → 1846 Brussels (parts on IMSLP) → 1848/49 St Petersburg (Perrot, Elssler; Pugni enlarged the score) → Petipa's insertions 1866 (Pas Cucchi), 1871, 1872 → 1886 Petipa for Zucchi, four acts, Drigo additions → 1899 Petipa for Kschessinska → Gorsky in Moscow (named by MAMT; date not found in this pass) → 1935 Vaganova, Kirov, for Tatiana Vecheslova, adding the Diana and Actaeon pas de deux for Galina Ulanova and Vakhtang Chabukiani → 1949 Gusev, Kirov → 1950 Burmeister, Moscow, with Pugni's music re-orchestrated by Reinhold Glière and new music by Sergei Vasilenko → 2004 New Jersey Ballet (full staging in the US) → 2009 Burlaka and Medvedev, Bolshoi, after 1899 [documented: Wikipedia; MAMT; Petipa Society]. Petipa's versions were notated in the Stepanov system and are among the Sergeyev papers [probable]. Burmeister's is the only version that "exists without loss of choreographic text", says the theatre that owns it [documented as the company's claim].

### Readings and disputes
- **Literature cut to the bone.** Marian Smith, *Ballet and Opera in the Age of Giselle* (2000), shows how Romantic ballets told stories through mime and music that "spoke" [probable]. *Esmeralda* is the test case of the literary ballet (T18): which of Hugo's themes survive without words? The priest's guilty desire does; the cathedral as a living thing does not [argued].
- **The gypsy as spectacle.** Postcolonial and Romani-studies readings treat Esmeralda as an exotic, sexualised outsider whom the plot ennobles by love for a gentleman soldier; Hugo himself made her secretly French by birth [argued; Hugo plot documented]. Burmeister kept that ending of birth revealed, in the prologue with her mother Gudule [documented: MAMT synopsis].
- **Contradiction on Diana and Actaeon.** One account says Drigo composed it for Petipa's 1888 production [documented: Pugni edition introduction]; Wikipedia says Vaganova arranged it in 1935 from the *Pas de Diane* in Petipa's *Le Roi Candaule* (1868), and that crediting it to Petipa's *Esmeralda* is a mistake [documented]. The weight of evidence favours 1935 [argued].
- **Contradiction on form.** One act or three in 1844 [contested above].
- **Contradiction on revival year.** Petipa's first full revival is dated 1886 (most sources) and 1888 (Pugni edition) [contested; 1886 is better supported].

### Facts worth telling
1. Hugo turned his novel into an opera libretto in 1836, eight years before the ballet [probable].
2. The choreographer danced the poet Gringoire himself [documented].
3. The ballet saves Esmeralda from the gallows, unlike the novel [documented].
4. Its most famous duet today, Diana and Actaeon, comes from a different ballet [documented].
5. Six painters designed the 1899 St Petersburg sets, one or two to a scene [documented].
6. Zucchi was said to weep real tears at the gallows [documented as "allegedly"].
7. Fanny Elssler chose it for her farewell to Moscow in 1851 [documented].

### Set it beside
- **giselle-1841** (W1 §5): the same choreographer and ballerina; the village girl betrayed by a disguised aristocrat, ended by death and forgiveness rather than rescue.
- **paquita-1846** (this file): two years later, the Romani girl again, revealed to be of noble birth.
- **romeo-juliet-lavrovsky-1940**: the Soviet drambalet that Burmeister's *Esmeralda* belongs to: a novel told through acting.
- **red-detachment-1964**: W3 notes a Chinese troupe danced *Esmeralda* (as *Notre-Dame de Paris*) before making its revolutionary ballets [documented in register note].

### What a maker can take from it
- Adapting means choosing what to drop. Perrot kept the crowd, the triangle of desire and the gallows, and cut the ending; the work lived because the audience got the rescue it wanted.
- Let the dance carry the plot. A dance in which someone teaches, flirts or refuses is a scene, not a decoration.
- Excerpts can outlive the whole. Design the parts that will travel alone (a solo with a prop, a duet) as carefully as the frame.

### Beyond the stage
Roland Petit's *Notre-Dame de Paris* (Paris Opéra, 1965, music Maurice Jarre, costumes Yves Saint Laurent) is a separate ballet on the same novel [probable]. Films: Disney's *The Hunchback of Notre Dame* (1996) [probable]; the musical *Notre-Dame de Paris* (1998) [probable]. Pugni's scores are now in print: *Cesare Pugni: Esmeralda and Le Violon du diable* (Cambridge Scholars) [documented].

## Pas de Quatre / Pas de Quatre (1845)
`id: pas-de-quatre-1845` · threads: T10, T19 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 12 July 1845, Her Majesty's Theatre, London, at the suggestion of its manager Benjamin Lumley [documented: V&A print title; Wikipedia]. Choreography Jules Perrot; music Cesare Pugni (original orchestral score at the BnF) [documented: Wikipedia via LODance]. No scenario; no designer named; the dancers wore the white Romantic dress with floral wreaths [documented: V&A description of the Chalon print]. One short divertissement: an entrance, four solos and a finale, perhaps 15 to 20 minutes [probable]. Version most seen today: Anton Dolin's (Ballet Theatre, New York, 1941), itself built on Keith Lester's 1936 London reconstruction; Dolin's rights are held by his heirs [documented: Petipa Society; Growcott 2025; LODance].

### In one line
Four rival star ballerinas share one stage, each given a solo that shows her best, so that the star system itself becomes the subject of the ballet.

### How it came to be
By the 1840s the ballerina was the reason people bought tickets, and five women ruled the Romantic stage: Marie Taglioni, Fanny Elssler, Carlotta Grisi, Fanny Cerrito and Lucile Grahn [documented: Petipa Society]. Benjamin Lumley, running the opera house of the London season, had the idea of putting them together on one night [documented: Wikipedia; Tacoma lecture]. Taglioni, Grisi and Cerrito accepted; Elssler declined; the young Danish ballerina Lucile Grahn took her place [documented]. Perrot, who had partnered three of them and choreographed for Grisi, had to make dances that flattered each woman without letting any outshine the others [probable]. Rehearsals were a war of precedence over who danced last, who wore what, and who was favoured [probable: Tacoma lecture, after Lumley's memoir]. Lumley's own account says the quarrel was ended by ordering the solos by age, youngest first, the place of honour last going to the eldest; at once no one wanted to be the eldest [probable: Lumley, *Reminiscences of the Opera*, 1864, as retold]. The order became Grahn, Grisi, Cerrito, Taglioni [documented: Wikipedia]. The four danced it together only four times, on 12, 15, 17 and 19 July, before Grisi left London [documented: Petipa Society].

### Roots and reference points
- **The benefit and the gala**: London's opera season loved one-off nights built around stars [probable].
- **The pas de trois and pas de cinq** of opera divertissements, where ballerinas danced in turn [probable].
- **The souvenir print.** A. E. Chalon had already made lithograph portraits of Taglioni as the Sylphide; the group picture was planned as part of the event's fame [probable: Chalon's 1845 *Souvenir d'adieu* print of Taglioni is listed in the Taglioni image archive].
- **The Three Graces** of classical sculpture, which the tableau groupings echo; an American print of 1845 by Nathaniel Currier is titled *The Three Graces* [documented: marietaglioni.orchesis-portal.org listing; the link to the Pas de Quatre is argued].

### The world it built
There is no story. The world is the four women and their differences. They enter hand in hand, bow, and form "sculpturesque" groups [documented: Growcott, quoting a period description]. Each then dances a variation made for her: Grahn's quick, clean footwork; Grisi's charm and speed; Cerrito's brilliance and dash; Taglioni's lightness and calm [probable: standard accounts]. A short coda brings them back together for the final pose, the one Chalon drew: Taglioni on pointe in the centre, arms raised, Grisi and Cerrito kneeling at either side, Grahn bending forward behind [documented: V&A physical description]. The steps call on every area of the technique of the time: slow adagio, small fast jumps, big jumps, quick footwork, and soft, rounded arms [documented: Wikipedia]. Pugni's music is a string of short numbers, each matched to its dancer [probable]. No man appears [documented: V&A].

### Design and art
The costume was the uniform of Romantic ballet: a low-cut pointed bodice and a bell-shaped, knee-length skirt of tiers of tarlatan, a stiff open-weave cotton muslin, with a transparent top layer [documented: V&A]. Three of the four wore "bold floral wreaths", the fourth a flower at the back of her head [documented: V&A]. Hand-coloured prints show the skirts in pale pink with roses [documented: Growcott's description]. The famous image is the lithograph by Thomas Herbert Maguire after Alfred Edward Chalon, 1845 (V&A, given by Marie Rambert; NYPL, Cia Fornaroli Collection; Bibliothèque de l'Opéra) [documented]. The *Illustrated London News* printed a wood engraving of the scene [documented: Petipa Society]. These prints are the main evidence for every later reconstruction [documented].

### Premiere and reception
The house was "crowded to suffocation", the stage covered in bouquets [documented as quoted: Tacoma City Ballet lecture]. *The Era* wrote that it "shook one's soul to the very centre"; the *Morning Herald* that it marked "an era in the records of the ballet-master's art" [documented as quoted: Petipa Society]. *The Times* is quoted as calling it "the greatest Terpsichorean exhibition that ever was known in Europe" [documented as quoted, secondary: Tacoma lecture]. Lumley wrote that it "probably formed the culminating point in the History of Ballet in England" [documented as quoted]. Queen Victoria and Prince Albert attended on 17 July [documented]. The four never danced together again [documented]. Money figures not found in this pass.

### Versions and lineage
1845 London (four performances) → 1846 Milan, La Scala, staged by Filippo Taglioni → 1847 London revival, two nights, Carolina Rosati for Grahn → 1847 Warsaw (two stagings by F. Taglioni) [documented: Growcott; Petipa Society]. Then the dance was lost. In the 1930s the historian Cyril Beaumont found Pugni's piano score in the British Museum; Keith Lester reconstructed the dances from the prints and eyewitness accounts for the Markova-Dolin company, Lyric Hammersmith, 1936, with music arranged by Leighton Lucas [documented: Petipa Society]. In 1940 Lucia Chase of Ballet Theatre asked for it; Dolin said Lester's notes were lost at sea and staged his own, premiered 16 July 1941 at the Majestic Theatre, New York [documented: Growcott 2025]. Dolin restaged it for the Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo (1948 or 1949) [probable]. Dolin's version is the common one; its performance rights were left to Belinda Wright and Jelko Yuresha [documented as reported: LODance]. Pugni's orchestral parts turned up in Paris in the 1970s [documented: Petipa Society].

### Readings and disputes
- **A ballet about the market.** Read as the star system in its purest form: the "work" is a showcase that sells personalities, like an all-star bill today (T10) [argued]. Lynn Garafola, ed., *Rethinking the Sylph* (1997), treats the Romantic ballerina as both idol and commodity [probable].
- **A ballet about difference.** Read as an early plotless ballet whose content is style: four ways of moving, compared side by side. This makes it an ancestor of Balanchine's plotless works [argued; compare T10].
- **Credit.** Who made the version on stage today? Amy Growcott, "Keith Lester: the resurrector of the Pas de Quatre" (2025), argues that Lester did the reconstruction and Dolin took the name [documented as argued]. Dolin's side was that he worked independently [documented as his claim, via Growcott].
- **Contradictions.** Wikipedia gives Dolin's 1941 cast as Krassovska, Slavenska, Danilova and Markova; Growcott gives Nana Gollner, Alicia Alonso, Krassovska and Slavenska for the 16 July 1941 premiere [contested; the Danilova and Markova cast fits the later Ballet Russe staging]. The "order by age" story comes from Lumley, the one person whose interest it served to tell it well [argued].

### Facts worth telling
1. Four performances made it legendary [documented].
2. The solos were ordered by age, youngest first, to stop the stars quarrelling [probable: Lumley's story].
3. Fanny Elssler was invited and refused [documented].
4. No man dances in it [documented].
5. Every modern version was rebuilt from a lithograph [documented].
6. The piano score was rediscovered in the British Museum in the 1930s [documented].
7. Who should be credited with the modern version, Lester or Dolin, is still argued [documented].

### Set it beside
- **pygmalion-salle-1734** (this file): a century earlier, a single star controlling her own image.
- **les-sylphides-1909**: Fokine's plotless Romantic "reverie", with the white skirts and no story, made sixty years later as homage.
- **jewels-1967**: Balanchine's three plotless acts, each a different style, a direct descendant of style-as-subject.
- **push-comes-to-shove-1976**: Tharp's comic showcase for a star (Baryshnikov), another ballet about celebrity.

### What a maker can take from it
- The line-up can be the concept. Bringing rivals together, with rules for how they share the stage, is itself a design.
- Design for each user. Perrot made each solo fit its dancer's strengths; a collection can be built of pieces cut for different people within one frame.
- Plan the image. The tableau was designed to be drawn, and the print made the event last 180 years.

### Beyond the stage
The Chalon print is among the most reproduced dance images; Rambert's copy is at the V&A [documented]. A 1938 set of 28 hand-coloured prints reissued it [documented: V&A O599138]. The work became a gala staple, and is often danced as comedy or parody, for example by Les Ballets Trockadero de Monte Carlo [probable].

## Paquita / Paquita (1846; Grand pas 1881)
`id: paquita-1846` · threads: T05, T07, T10, T13 · confidence of the header: documented (1846 and 1881 premieres); décor credits and some dates contested

**Header.** Premiere 1 April 1846, Paris Opéra, Salle Le Peletier [documented: Wikipedia; Petipa Society]. Choreography Joseph Mazilier, the Opéra's ballet master; music Édouard Deldevez; scenario Paul Foucher (Victor Hugo's brother-in-law) and Mazilier [documented: French Wikipedia; Bolshoi]. Décor: "Jourdeuil (scenes 1 and 2) and Heinrich Wagner (scene 3)" according to the Petipa Society, which may mix the Paris and Petersburg productions [contested]. Two acts, three scenes. Petipa's revival: St Petersburg, Bolshoi Kamenny Theatre, 27 December 1881 Old Style (8 or 9 January 1882 New Style), with new numbers by Ludwig Minkus: the pas de trois, the *Grand pas classique* and the children's mazurka [documented: Petipa Society; French Wikipedia; ROH Collections]. Version most seen today: the *Grand pas classique* alone, about 25 to 30 minutes, in many editions; full-length versions by Pierre Lacotte (Paris Opéra, 2001), Alexei Ratmansky and Doug Fullington (Bavarian State Ballet, 2014), Sergei Vikharev (Ekaterinburg, 2017) and Yuri Smekalov (Mariinsky, 2017) [documented].

### In one line
A Romani girl in Napoleonic Spain saves a French officer from assassination and turns out to be a lost aristocrat; its last-act ballroom became the most portable showpiece of classical ballet.

### How it came to be
In the 1840s the Paris Opéra wanted vehicles for Carlotta Grisi, the star of *Giselle*. Mazilier, who would later make *Le Corsaire*, built *Paquita* around her with Lucien Petipa, the house's leading man, as the officer [documented]. Spain was the fashion: Fanny Elssler's Spanish dances had been a sensation since 1836, and Paris had seen real Spanish troupes [probable]. The ballet was a "tremendous success" and stayed in the Paris repertory until 1851 [documented: Petipa Society; Wikipedia]. Théophile Gautier is said to have been dazzled [documented as "aurait ébloui": French Wikipedia]. Lucien's younger brother Marius, a dancer of no great fame, had just been hired by the Imperial Theatres in St Petersburg. His first production there, staged with Pierre-Frédéric Malavergne, was *Paquita*, with himself as Lucien and Elena Andreyanova as Paquita, on 26 September / 8 October 1847 [documented: Petipa Society; Wikipedia]. Thirty-four years later, now chief ballet master, Petipa revived it for the benefit night of his ballerina Ekaterina Vazem and asked Minkus for new dances [documented]. Those dances outlived the ballet: the full work left the Mariinsky in 1926, but the *Grand pas* never left the stage [documented].

### Roots and reference points
- **Cervantes, *La Gitanilla*** (one of the *Exemplary Novels*, 1613), the tale of a gypsy girl who is really a noblewoman stolen as a child, is the plot's ancestor; the Mariinsky's 2017 version returned to it openly [documented: Mariinsky; the 1846 debt is probable].
- **The foundling plot** of melodrama: a medallion or portrait proves birth (here, a miniature of her father) [documented: Petipa Society synopsis].
- **Spain under Napoleon** (1808 to 1814), a recent war for the audience's parents [documented setting].
- **Elssler's cachucha** and the Spanish dancers in Paris: castanets, the *bolero*, and the "Spanish" ballerina [probable].
- **Esmeralda** (1844, this file), the Romani heroine two years earlier [argued].

### The world it built
Act I: a valley near Saragossa; the Spanish governor Don Lopez plans to kill the French officer Lucien d'Hervilly, who has refused his sister Serafina; the Romani chief Iñigo wants Paquita for himself [documented: synopsis]. Act II: in Iñigo's house Paquita foils the poisoning plot and leads Lucien to safety; then a ball at the French general's house, where she sees a portrait, recognises the man in her own miniature, and learns that she is Lucien's cousin, whose parents were murdered; the wedding follows [documented: Petipa Society].

The *Grand pas classique* is pure display, almost unconnected to the story: an entrée for the corps and soloists, a grand adagio for the ballerina and her partner (in 1881 with a violin cadenza written for Leopold Auer), a chain of solo variations, and a fast coda with the ballerina's turns [documented: Wikipedia]. In 1881 there was only one variation, a polonaise for Vazem. The "tradition" of many variations, each lifted from another ballet, began at a Peterhof gala in 1896 or 1897 when Mathilde Kschessinska invited soloists to dance their favourites; a 1902 farewell gala for Enrico Cecchetti fixed it, with "some twenty-one variations" [documented as reported: Alison's Studio; Bolshoi]. So each company's *Paquita* is a different anthology. The children's mazurka, for pupils of the Imperial school, is the other survivor [documented].

### Design and art
The look is Romantic Spain: mantillas, short tiered skirts, boleros, castanets, and the officers' Napoleonic uniforms [probable]. In the *Grand pas* the tutu changes the period: the ballerinas wear classical tutus with Spanish touches, a comb or a carnation, red and black or white and gold, so a Spanish ball becomes an Imperial parade [probable]. Lacotte's 2001 production, designed by Luisa Spinatelli, aimed at Mazilier's "look and atmosphere" [documented: DanceTabs; designer probable]. Vikharev's Ekaterinburg version, rebuilt from the Harvard notation, received ten Golden Mask nominations [documented: Ural Opera, Sklyarevskaya 2019]; its designer was not found in this pass. The famous images are photographs: Vazem in the title role (1881), children in the mazurka (1881), Michel Fokine as Lucien (1898) [documented: Alison's Studio captions].

### Premiere and reception
1846: Grisi and Lucien Petipa; Zélie Pierson as Doña Serafina [documented: French Wikipedia]. 1847 Petersburg: Andreyanova and Marius Petipa [documented]. When Andreyanova danced it in Moscow, a hostile faction insulted her on stage (a dead cat was thrown, in the usual telling) [probable; the Petipa Society says "the incident infuriated the public"]. 1881: Vazem and Pavel Gerdt [documented]. No critic's words from 1846 or 1881 found in this pass.

### Versions and lineage
1846 Paris (Mazilier) → 1847 St Petersburg (Petipa and Malavergne) → 1848 Moscow (Petipa) → 1881 St Petersburg (Petipa, Minkus additions); notated in Stepanov notation while Petipa coached Anna Pavlova, now in the Sergeyev Collection at Harvard → full ballet dropped 1926 → *Grand pas* staged by Pavlova's company, by Oleg Vinogradov for the Kirov (1978; danced at the Paris Opéra 1980 to 2001), by Rudolf Nureyev, by Natalia Makarova (ABT, 1984), by Sadler's Wells Royal Ballet (1980, music arranged by Gordon Jacob) → 2001 Lacotte, Paris (Mazilier's mime restored, Petipa's 1881 dances kept) → 2014 Ratmansky and Fullington, Munich, from the notation → 2017 Vikharev, Ekaterinburg → 2017 Smekalov, Mariinsky, a new three-act ballet after *La Gitanilla* with the *Grand pas* reconstructed by Yuri Burlaka [documented: Wikipedia; French Wikipedia; ROH; Mariinsky; Makarova date probable].

### Readings and disputes
- **The return to the father.** The plot heals class by birth: the poor girl who loves the officer is revealed as his equal, so the romance never truly crosses class (T07) [argued]. Compare *Giselle*, which refuses that comfort.
- **The French saviour in Spain.** A French officer is the hero of a war in which France occupied Spain, and the Spanish governor is the villain [argued; plot documented]. Read beside Goya's *Disasters of War*, the ballet is the occupier's romance.
- **Heritage as anthology.** The *Grand pas* shows how a classical "text" is made of insertions; there is no single authentic version, because the variations were always chosen by the dancers [documented as history: Alison's Studio; Bolshoi]. Doug Fullington's notation work (with Ratmansky) argues for recovering what Petipa set; Lacotte's method was to rebuild an atmosphere [documented: DanceTabs; NYCB]. Both sides claim to serve the past.
- **Contradictions.** Grand pas premiere: 8 January 1882 NS (French Wikipedia; ROH), 9 January (Petipa Society), "27 January 1881" (Mariinsky, apparently mixing styles) [contested]. 1847: St Petersburg (most sources) or Moscow (ROH Collections) [contested]. Kschessinska's gala: 1896 (Bolshoi, for the centenary of Catherine the Great's death) or 1897 (Alison's Studio) [contested; 1896 fits the centenary]. Whether any Mazilier steps survive in Petipa's version is "still to be confirmed" [documented: Petipa Society].

### Facts worth telling
1. *Paquita* was Marius Petipa's first production in Russia, in 1847 [documented].
2. His brother Lucien created the hero in Paris [documented].
3. The *Grand pas* was added 35 years after the ballet was made [documented].
4. Its variations are borrowed from other ballets, chosen by each company [documented].
5. One 1902 gala used about 21 variations [documented as reported].
6. The 1881 adagio had a violin cadenza for Leopold Auer, later Heifetz's teacher [documented: Wikipedia; Auer's pupil probable].
7. Four full-length *Paquitas* appeared in Europe and Russia between 2001 and 2017 [documented].

### Set it beside
- **esmeralda-1844** (this file): the Romani heroine in Paris, saved by a soldier; in *Paquita* saved by birth.
- **don-quixote-1869** (this file): Petipa's other Spanish ballet, which ends in the same kind of grand pas.
- **raymonda-1898**: Petipa's last great full-length, whose last-act wedding is another national-dance parade.
- **theme-and-variations**-type plotless works (e.g. **symphony-in-c-1947**): Balanchine took the Imperial grand pas and removed the ballet around it.

### What a maker can take from it
- A frame can host contributions. The *Grand pas* became an open format into which each star brought her own variation; a collection can be designed to accept guest pieces.
- The finale can become the product. The part made for display travelled; the plot did not.
- Know which source you are restoring: the original look (Lacotte), the notated text (Ratmansky) or the living habit of performance. Each gives a different *Paquita*.

### Beyond the stage
The *Grand pas* is a fixture of galas, competitions and school graduations worldwide [probable]. Lacotte's Paris production was filmed (2001) and released on DVD [documented: French Wikipedia "enregistrée"].

## Le Corsaire / The Corsair (1856)
`id: corsaire-1856` · threads: T05, T18, T13 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 23 January 1856, Théâtre Impérial de l'Opéra (Salle Le Peletier), Paris [documented: Lecomte 2016; Petipa Society]. Choreography Joseph Mazilier; music Adolphe Adam, his last ballet score; scenario Jules-Henri Vernoy de Saint-Georges and Mazilier, loosely after Lord Byron's poem *The Corsair* (1814) [documented]. Sets: Hugues Martin (Act I), Édouard Despléchin (Acts II and III scene 2), Joseph Thierry and Charles Cambon (Act III scene 1); stage machinery Sacré; costumes Alfred Albert [documented: Lecomte 2016, table 1]. Three acts in Paris; later four acts and five scenes in St Petersburg [documented]. Version most seen today: productions descended from Petipa's 1899 revival, chiefly the Bolshoi's 2007 reconstruction by Alexei Ratmansky and Yuri Burlaka (3 hours 35 minutes with two intervals), Anna-Marie Holmes's version (ABT, English National Ballet), the Mariinsky's, and the *Corsaire* pas de deux on its own [documented: Bolshoi; Wikipedia].

### In one line
A pirate steals a Greek girl back from a slave market and a pasha's harem, and the whole romance ends in a shipwreck.

### How it came to be
In 1855 the Paris Opéra hired two Italian virtuosos, Carolina Rosati and Amalia Ferraris, to fill the gap left by Fanny Cerrito [documented: Petipa Society]. Mazilier, near the end of his career, made ballets for both; *Le Corsaire* was Rosati's [documented]. Saint-Georges, co-author of *Giselle*, wrote the scenario, and Adam, *Giselle*'s composer, wrote the score; he died a few months after the premiere [documented]. The Second Empire Opéra was a showcase of money and machinery, and the climax was a ship sinking in a storm, with the stage machinist Sacré in charge [documented: Lecomte; Opéra's machinery]. It ran to October 1858, and Mazilier revived it for the 1867 Universal Exhibition with Adèle Grantzow, for whom Léo Delibes, Adam's pupil, wrote a new *Pas des fleurs* [documented: Wikipedia]. After 1868 Paris dropped it for good [documented: Lecomte]. Meanwhile Jules Perrot had taken it to St Petersburg in January 1858, with the young Marius Petipa dancing Conrad and adding dances [documented]. Petipa then revised it four more times, for a new ballerina each time: his wife Maria Surovshchikova-Petipa (1863), Grantzow (1868), Eugenia Sokolova (1880) and Pierina Legnani (1899) [documented: Petipa Society; Lecomte].

### Roots and reference points
- **Byron, *The Corsair*** (1814), a verse tale that sold 10,000 copies on its first day: the brooding pirate Conrad, his gentle Medora, and the harem slave Gulnare who kills the Pasha Seyd to free him [probable: sales figure is the common claim]. The ballet keeps the names and the exotic setting and drops the gloom [documented: PBT audience guide].
- **Earlier *Corsaire* ballets**: three versions between 1826 and 1844 by other choreographers [documented: PBT guide; names not found in this pass].
- **The Greek War of Independence** (1821 to 1829), in which Byron died; Greek captives and Ottoman pashas were a living image for Paris [argued].
- **Orientalist painting**: Ingres's odalisques and Delacroix's harems and slave markets [argued].
- **The machine play**: Paris's taste for spectacular stage effects, from Ciceri's gaslight (W1 §4) to shipwrecks [probable].

### The world it built
In the Petipa line: a slave market in Adrianople, where the slave dealer Lankedem sells women and Conrad's pirates seize Medora; a pirates' grotto, where Medora and Conrad dance and his lieutenant Birbanto betrays him with a drugged flower; the Pasha's palace, where Medora is sold to Seyd, and the Pasha dreams the *Jardin animé*, his wives become a living flower garden; the escape; and the shipwreck, with Conrad and Medora washed ashore alone [documented: synopsis, PBT guide; Bolshoi].

It is a ballet of numbers more than story. Many were added later, each by a different composer:
- the **Pas d'esclave** (1858 or 1863), to music by Prince Peter of Oldenburg, a Russian royal amateur [documented: Bolshoi; Wikipedia];
- the **Pas de trois des odalisques** [documented];
- the **Jardin animé** (St Petersburg 1868, from Delibes's 1867 *Pas des fleurs*), in which dozens of women with garlands fill the stage in symmetrical patterns [documented: Petipa Society; PBT];
- the **Corsaire pas de deux**, built on a 1899 Drigo number, made a pas de trois for Medora, Conrad and the slave Ali by Samuil Andrianov around 1915, and turned into a show-stopper by Vakhtang Chabukiani in the 1930s [documented as stated: PBT guide; Wikipedia]. The slave Ali, the role that makes male virtuosos famous, has no ancestor in Byron [argued].

Adam's score has been mixed with Pugni, Delibes, Drigo, Oldenburg, Minkus, Albert Zabel and Julius Gerber [documented: Bolshoi credits]. Gusev's 1955 Leningrad version even replaced most of Adam's 1856 music with other Adam music [documented: Wikipedia].

### Design and art
The Paris 1856 sets were by the leading Opéra studios (Martin, Despléchin, Thierry, Cambon) [documented]. St Petersburg used Andrei Roller and Heinrich Wagner [documented: Petipa Society; Lecomte]. Roller, chief machinist and painter of the Imperial Theatres, is said to have tried electric effects for the lightning in the shipwreck [documented as "according to some sources": Hungarian State Opera]. The 1899 costumes by Evgeny Ponomaryov survive as sketches; the Bolshoi's 2007 production by Boris Kaminsky (sets) and Elena Zaitseva (costumes) reworked them [documented: Bolshoi]. The look is Ottoman fantasy: turbans, baggy trousers and bare-chested pirates against pastel tutus; a painted harbour; a palace of arches; and the garden of white and pink flowers [probable]. The ship that breaks and sinks is the ballet's famous image [probable].

### Premiere and reception
1856: Rosati (Medora), the Italian mime Domenico Segarelli (Conrad), Claudina Cucchi (Gulnare) [documented]. Segarelli's heavy mime "did not much excite" Paris, which "had eyes only for the ballerinas" [documented: Lecomte, translated]. Lecomte stresses that mime ruled the original, climaxing in the escape and the shipwreck [documented]. The 1867 revival had 38 performances with Grantzow [documented: Wikipedia]. Whether Napoleon III and the Empress Eugénie attended the 1856 premiere was not checked in this pass. The Bolshoi's 2007 production is reported to have cost about US$1.5 million, called "the most expensive production of a ballet ever mounted" [documented as claimed: Wikipedia; contested, unsourced comparison].

### Versions and lineage
1856 Paris (Mazilier) → 1858 St Petersburg (Perrot with Petipa) → 1863, 1868, 1880 (or 1885), 1899 Petipa revivals → 1915 last Imperial production; in repertory to 1928 → Soviet versions (Gusev for the Maly, Leningrad, 1955, with a libretto by Gusev and the historian Yuri Slonimsky) → Konstantin Sergeyev, Kirov, 1973 [probable] → the pas de deux popularised in the West by Rudolf Nureyev and Margot Fonteyn from 1962 [documented: Lecomte names Nureyev; Fonteyn and date probable] → Anna-Marie Holmes, Boston and ABT (1997 or 1998) [probable] → 2007 Ratmansky and Burlaka, Bolshoi, from the Harvard notation, BnF score and 1899 sketches [documented]. The Stepanov notation is in the Sergeyev Collection [documented: Bolshoi credits].

### Readings and disputes
- **An Orientalist fantasy.** Under Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978), the ballet is a European dream of the Muslim East as a slave market and a harem, with Greek Christian women as captives and Turks as lustful buffoons [argued]. Recent stagings have had to decide what to do with the slave market; some companies have rewritten or cut it [probable].
- **A pure dance machine.** Nathalie Lecomte, "Dans le sillage du *Corsaire*: de Paris (1856) à Saint-Pétersbourg (1899)", *Slavica Occitania* 43 (2016), shows how Petipa turned a mime-heavy Paris ballet into a frame for classical dance, the method he used on all the French ballets he inherited [documented]. On this reading the plot barely matters; the work is its numbers.
- **The male virtuoso.** The slave Ali and the pas de deux are where the twentieth century put its male stars (Chabukiani, Nureyev, Baryshnikov) [argued], which reverses the Romantic cult of the ballerina.
- **Contradictions.** Petipa's third revival: 1880 (Petipa Society, Lecomte) or 1885 (Hungarian State Opera) [contested; 1880 better supported]. The Pas d'esclave: added in 1858 (Bolshoi, Hungarian Opera) or 1863 (Wikipedia) [contested]. Wikipedia says the 1863 Conrad was Christian Johansson; the Petipa Society says Petipa [contested].

### Facts worth telling
1. It was Adam's last ballet; he died months after the premiere [documented].
2. Paris dropped it in 1868; Russia kept it [documented].
3. Its most famous duet was once a trio and was shaped over 30 years by three men [documented as stated].
4. A Russian prince composed one of its best-known dances [documented].
5. The "living garden" was added twelve years after the premiere [documented].
6. The 2007 Bolshoi production used 1899 costume sketches and Harvard notation [documented].
7. The ballet ends not with a wedding but with a shipwreck [documented].

### Set it beside
- **bayadere-1877** (W1 §7): Petipa's other Orientalist spectacle, with a temple instead of a harem and a destroyed palace instead of a ship.
- **scheherazade-1910**: the harem ballet remade by Fokine and Bakst as erotic violence.
- **pharaohs-daughter-1862** (this file): Petipa's Egypt, another East built for St Petersburg.
- **swan-lake-bourne-1995**: a later work where the star male role, not the ballerina, carries the evening.

### What a maker can take from it
- A world can be a scaffold for set pieces. *Le Corsaire* survives because its frame (pirates, a market, a palace, a storm) can hold any number of showpieces.
- Name your borrowings. Every production credits six or seven composers; honest credit makes the patchwork legible.
- A setting carries politics whether you mean it or not. A slave market staged as fun reads differently to each age.

### Beyond the stage
The pas de deux is a staple of galas and television dance competitions [probable]. Nureyev and Fonteyn's filmed performance (1960s) made it famous in the West [probable]. Verdi's opera *Il corsaro* (1848) and Berlioz's overture *Le Corsaire* (1844) come from the same Byron poem [probable].

## Дочь фараона (La Fille du Pharaon) / The Pharaoh's Daughter (1862)
`id: pharaohs-daughter-1862` · threads: T05, T20, T01, T18, T13 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 18/30 January 1862 (Old Style / New Style), Bolshoi Kamenny Theatre, St Petersburg, Imperial Ballet, for the benefit of Carolina Rosati [documented: Petipa Society; Mariinsky exhibition]. Choreography Marius Petipa, his first full-length ballet; music Cesare Pugni; scenario Jules-Henri Vernoy de Saint-Georges and Petipa, after Théophile Gautier's *Le Roman de la momie* (1857 to 1858) [documented]. Sets Heinrich Wagner and Andreas Roller, scene by scene; costumes Philippe Calver (Kelwer) and Alexei Stoliarov [documented: Petipa Society; Bolshoi]. A prologue, three acts and an epilogue, nine scenes [documented]. Versions seen today: Pierre Lacotte's at the Bolshoi (premiere 5 May 2000; 2 hours 55 minutes with two intervals; revived 19 July 2018) and the Mariinsky's 2023 reconstruction by Toni Candeloro, designed by Roberto Perdziola [documented: Bolshoi; Muzobozrenie 2023].

### In one line
An English traveller smokes opium in a pyramid and dreams himself into ancient Egypt as the lover of a princess-mummy who has come back to life.

### How it came to be
In 1861 Carolina Rosati, the Italian star of *Le Corsaire*, was ending her St Petersburg contract and was owed a new ballet for her benefit night [documented: Mariinsky exhibition]. The Director of the Imperial Theatres, Andrei Saburov, said there was no time. In Petipa's memoirs Rosati takes Petipa to the Director's house; the argument grows hot until Saburov, in a dressing-gown, realises with embarrassment that his gown has fallen open, and to end the scene asks Petipa, "can you stage this ballet in six weeks?" [documented as quoted: Petipa Society, from *Russian Ballet Master: The Memoirs of Marius Petipa*, pp. 50–52]. Petipa, still a dancer by contract, had made only one-act works [documented]. He went to Paris to work on the scenario with Saint-Georges, the fashionable librettist, and took the subject from Gautier's new novel [documented: Mariinsky exhibition]. In six weeks the dances were made and rehearsed and the workshops produced a vast number of costumes, eight changes of scenery, two life-size camels, a lion and a swarm of bees [documented: Mariinsky exhibition]. Pugni wrote the music while Petipa set the steps [documented: classicalmusicnews.ru]. The success won Petipa the post of ballet master [documented].

### Roots and reference points
- **Gautier, *Le Roman de la momie*** (serialised 1857, book 1858): an English lord and a German Egyptologist open an untouched tomb and find the mummy of Tahoser; the lord falls in love with the dead woman, and the novel tells her ancient story [probable]. The ballet keeps the English lord and makes the dream his own.
- **Egyptomania**: Napoleon's Egyptian campaign (1798) and the *Description de l'Égypte*; Champollion's decipherment of hieroglyphs (1822); Auguste Mariette's excavations at Saqqara (from 1850) [probable].
- **The opium dream** of Romantic literature (De Quincey's *Confessions of an English Opium-Eater*, 1821) [argued]; it reappears in *La Bayadère* (W1 §7).
- **Gautier as ballet author**: he wrote *Giselle* (1841) and had imagined dance as the living statue [probable].
- **The féerie**, the Paris fairy spectacle with transformations and apotheoses [probable].

### The world it built
Prologue: a desert storm drives Lord Wilson, his servant John Bull and Arab merchants into a pyramid; they smoke opium; mummies rise; a sign through the clouds reads "A Dream from the Past" [documented: Bolshoi]. Wilson becomes Ta-Hor, an Egyptian, and Princess Aspicia wakes. Act I: a lion hunt in a tropical forest, where Ta-Hor saves her life. Act II: Aspicia is promised to the King of Nubia; the lovers flee to a fisherman's hut by the Nile; to escape the King she throws herself into the river. Underwater, the god of the Nile shows her a festival of the world's great rivers (the Guadalquivir, the Thames, the Congo, the Neva and others) before sending her back [documented: Bolshoi "a fantastic celebration of all the world's rivers"; river list probable]. Act III: Ta-Hor is condemned to die by snakebite; Aspicia threatens to let the snake bite her first; the Pharaoh relents; an apotheosis of Osiris and Isis; and Wilson wakes in the pyramid [documented: Petipa Society; Belcanto].

The ballet is a long chain of dances: hunts by men with spears and women with bows, a monkey danced by a student, a grand pas, character dances, the river variations, each for a different soloist [documented: Vaganova Today 2023]. Petipa wrote new variations for almost every ballerina who took the role, so the notation records several versions [documented: Belcanto]. Pugni's music is quick, tuneful and sectional, written to order [probable; G1 on Pugni].

### Design and art
The 1862 sets showed "a huge Egyptian jungle, the Pharaoh's palace and an enchanted underwater kingdom" [documented: Petipa Society]. Roller was made an academician for the perspective of the hall in the Pharaoh's palace [documented: classicalmusicnews.ru]. Later sets for the 1885 revival were by Orest Allegri and Pyotr Lambin; costume sketches by Evgeny Ponomaryov and Pavel Grigoriev survive at the St Petersburg State Theatre Library [documented: Mariinsky exhibition; Muzobozrenie]. The Egypt is a pastiche: lotus capitals, striped headcloths, gilded collars and short tutus with Egyptian motifs, with no real attempt at archaeological accuracy; critics in 1862 regretted "the absence of national Egyptian colour" [documented as reported: classicalmusicnews.ru]. Lacotte designed his own sets for 2000 (costumes Elena Zaitseva) [documented: Bolshoi]. Perdziola's 2023 designs were made "after the sketches" of 1862 and 1885 [documented].

### Premiere and reception
Cast: Rosati (Aspicia), Petipa (Lord Wilson / Ta-Hor), Lyubov Radina (Ramzea), Timofei Stukolkin (John Bull), Lev Ivanov (the Fisherman), Nikolai Goltz (the Pharaoh), Felix Kschessinsky (King of Nubia) [documented: Petipa Society]. Rosati wrote to Saint-Georges: a full house "at triple prices", and the Emperor stayed to the end, "which happens very rarely" [documented as quoted, Russian translation: classicalmusicnews.ru]. The press mocked the "senseless libretto", praised the décor and judged it "an effective spectacle" [documented as reported]. It played seven times in a row [documented]. Money: the Directorate's 1861 budget for a new ballet was 12,000 roubles; the 1864 Moscow staging cost 16,480 roubles [documented: Makarova 2021, via scope/05]. Petipa was offered a one-off 800 roubles and instead got a ballet master's contract, raised by 1,540 a year [documented: scope/05]. By 1924 the press called it "an example of archaism"; audiences still filled the house; it was last given on 19 April 1928 [documented: Mariinsky exhibition].

### Versions and lineage
1862 St Petersburg → 1864 Moscow (Petipa) → 1885 and 1898 Petipa revivals (the last for Kschessinska) → notated in Stepanov notation 1903 to 1906 (Sergeyev Collection, Harvard) → 1905 Gorsky, Moscow → dropped 1928 → 2000 Lacotte, Bolshoi, new choreography in period style, Pugni score edited by Alexander Sotnikov → 2023 Candeloro, Mariinsky, after more than five years of work on the notation [documented: Bolshoi; Petipa Society; Vaganova Today]. The Lacotte version was filmed by Bel Air Media [documented].

### Readings and disputes
- **Restoration or pastiche?** The Bolshoi calls Lacotte's work "the second birth" and a "reconstruction" [documented as the company's claim]. Belcanto.ru says plainly that Lacotte "turned to the historical libretto and Pugni's music and composed new dances" [documented]. So 2000 is a new ballet in old dress, and 2023 is the first serious attempt from the notation [argued].
- **Empire's dream of the East.** Read with Said's *Orientalism* (1978): the hero is an Englishman, the East is a dream he can enter and leave, and the ancient woman exists for his pleasure [argued]. Set against that, the 1862 critics wanted *more* Egyptian authenticity, not less [documented as reported].
- **The golden ballet.** O. N. Makarova, "'The Pharaoh's Daughter': the golden ballet of the St Petersburg repertoire", *Vestnik Akademii Russkogo baleta* 6 (77), 2021 (OA), reads it through money: costly for the Directorate, a "gold mine" for the box office, and literally golden for dancers who received jewels at benefit nights [documented].
- **Myth and memoir.** The dressing-gown scene and the six weeks come from Petipa's own memoirs, written late in life [documented]; the six weeks is repeated by the Mariinsky [documented]. Whether it was truly six weeks has not been checked against Directorate records in this pass [contested].

### Facts worth telling
1. Petipa made his first full-length ballet in six weeks, by his own account [documented].
2. The props included two life-size camels and a swarm of bees [documented].
3. Under the Nile the world's rivers dance, the Neva among them [probable].
4. The 1862 hero is a sleeping Englishman with a comic servant called John Bull [documented].
5. Lev Ivanov, later co-choreographer of *Swan Lake*, danced the Fisherman [documented].
6. The scene painter Roller was made an academician for one palace perspective [documented].
7. It left the Soviet stage in 1928 and returned in 2000 [documented].

### Set it beside
- **bayadere-1877** (W1 §7): Petipa again, an opium vision of an eastern woman; the dream is now tragic.
- **cleopatra-cairo-2019**: Egypt staged from Cairo, the register's suggested pair on the T05 wall.
- **sleeping-beauty-1890** (W1 §8): another sleeping woman woken, now inside a fairy tale rather than a tomb.
- **excelsior-1881** (this file): the other great "all-the-world" spectacle of the century, where rivers and nations are replaced by inventions.

### What a maker can take from it
- A frame story lets you enter any world. The sleeping traveller can visit Egypt, then wake: it licenses fantasy and keeps a familiar point of view.
- Catalogue scenes are generous. The festival of rivers is a list turned into choreography, a way to fit many dancers and many colours into one logic.
- Speed and scale can be the story of the making. "Six weeks" became part of the ballet's legend and Petipa's career.

### Beyond the stage
Gautier's novel had a film descendant in the mummy genre (*The Mummy*, 1932) [argued]. Lacotte's version is on DVD (Bel Air) [documented].

## Дон Кихот / Don Quixote (1869; Gorsky 1900)
`id: don-quixote-1869` · threads: T18, T05, T14, T21, T20, T13 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 14/26 December 1869, Bolshoi Theatre, Moscow, Imperial Ballet (Moscow troupe) [documented: Petipa Society; Mariinsky]. Choreography and scenario Marius Petipa, after an episode of Cervantes's *Don Quixote* (Part II, 1615); music Ludwig Minkus, their first collaboration [documented]. Sets Pavel Isakov, Ivan Shangin and Fyodor Shenian [documented: Petipa Society; Philadelphia Ballet]. Four acts, eight scenes in Moscow; five acts and eleven scenes in St Petersburg, 9/21 November 1871 [documented]. Alexander Gorsky's revision: Bolshoi, Moscow, 6/19 December 1900, three acts and six scenes, extra music by Anton Simon; brought to the Mariinsky 20 January 1902; sets Alexander Golovin and Konstantin Korovin, costumes Korovin [documented: Mariinsky; Petipa Society]. Every version seen today descends from Gorsky's; the Mariinsky's runs 2 hours 55 minutes with two intervals [documented]. Bachtrack listed it as the seventh most performed ballet worldwide in 2023 [documented: CENSUS §1.1].

### In one line
In a sunlit Barcelona square, an innkeeper's daughter and a barber outwit her father's plan to marry her to a rich fop, while the old knight wanders through, dreams of his Dulcinea and blesses the wedding.

### How it came to be
In the late 1860s Petipa was second ballet master in St Petersburg, under Arthur Saint-Léon, and was sent to stage new work for the Moscow troupe [probable]. He wanted Cesare Pugni as composer, but Pugni was failing from drink and depression, so he turned to Ludwig Minkus, a Viennese violinist and composer then working in Moscow [documented: Petipa Society]. Petipa had danced in Spain as a young man (Madrid, 1844 to 1847) and loved its dances; late in life he boasted he played the castanets "no worse than the leading dancers of Andalusia" [documented as quoted: Bolshoi]. He made the ballet a comedy built almost entirely from Spanish character dances [documented: Bolshoi]. Moscow liked it. Two years later he moved it to St Petersburg, where taste demanded classical dancing for the prima ballerina, so Minkus wrote a fifth, classical act; Kitri and Dulcinea became one dual role, and the ballet ended with the knight's death [documented: Bolshoi; Mariinsky Primorsky]. Then in 1900 the young Alexander Gorsky, sent to Moscow to keep Petipa's ballets alive, remade it his own way [documented]. Petipa was hurt: "Monsieur Gorsky shamelessly borrows my ideas" [documented as quoted: Mariinsky, Olga Makarova].

### Roots and reference points
- **Cervantes, *Don Quixote*, Part II, chapters 19 to 21**: the wedding of rich Camacho (Gamache) and fair Quiteria (Kitri), which the poor Basilio wins by a fake suicide [probable: chapter numbers working knowledge].
- **Earlier *Don Quixote* ballets**: Franz Hilverding (Vienna, 1740), Noverre (1768), Didelot (1828), Paul Taglioni (1850) [documented: San Francisco Ballet].
- **Spanish dance in Paris and Petersburg**: the jota, the seguidilla, the fandango, the bolero, the castanets; Elssler's cachucha [probable]. The Bolshoi lists the original as built from "the jota, morena, zingara, lola", picadors and toreadors [documented].
- **Stanislavski**: Gorsky was "heavily influenced" by the Moscow Art Theatre, which opened in 1898, and its call for lifelike crowds [documented: Mariinsky].
- **The vision scene** of Romantic ballet (*Giselle*, W1 §5), reborn as the Don's dream of dryads and Cupid [argued].

### The world it built
Gorsky's three acts (with a prologue) [documented: Mariinsky synopsis]:
- **Prologue**: the Don's study; he reads chivalric romances and sets out with Sancho Panza.
- **Act I**: the square before Lorenzo's inn in Barcelona; Kitri and Basilio flirt and quarrel; her father wants the fop Gamache; street dancers, toreadors with capes, a dancer among knives; the Don mistakes Kitri for Dulcinea; the lovers run away.
- **Act II**: a gypsy camp and puppet show; the windmills, which the Don attacks; he falls stunned and dreams of Dulcinea among dryads, with the Queen of the Dryads and Cupid; then a tavern where Basilio fakes suicide to force the father's consent.
- **Act III**: the wedding, with a fandango and the grand pas de deux.

Signature passages: Kitri's entrance with a fan; her high split jumps and the one-arm lifts Basilio holds overhead; the toreadors' entrance; the dream scene, the one classical white-tutu world in the ballet; and the wedding pas de deux, whose coda with Kitri's 32 fouettés is the gala favourite [probable]. A story from the 1870s: Alexandra Vergina, the St Petersburg Kitri, jumped ahead of the music on a leap into the arms of Lev Ivanov, her short-sighted Basilio, who was waiting for the beat; she fell and the show stopped [probable: Ballet Alert forum, after period accounts]. Later choreographers added to Gorsky: Fyodor Lopukhov's fandango (1923) and Nina Anisimova's gypsy and oriental dances (1946) [documented: Mariinsky]. Minkus's music is *musique dansante*: polkas and waltzes with a Spanish surface, clear beats for jumps and turns [documented: Petipa Society].

### Design and art
Gorsky's revolution was in the crowd. He broke Petipa's symmetrical corps into a mass of individuals, each with a part to play: "with me they dance one thing to the right, another to the left and upstage a third" [documented as quoted: Bolshoi]. An early spectator recalled believing "to the point of delusion in the sun" of the square [documented as quoted: Mariinsky]. Korovin and Golovin, painters from the Abramtsevo and Moscow Art circle, gave it hot ochres, reds and whites, sun on stone walls, and real-looking Spanish dress rather than ballet costume [probable]. Korovin's 1900s designs are still restored and used at the Mariinsky [documented]. The fan, the cape, the tambourine and the windmill sails are its icons [argued]. The 1869 sets (Isakov, Shangin, Shenian) are lost or unstudied in this pass.

### Premiere and reception
1869 Moscow: Anna Sobeshchanskaya (Kitri), Sergei Sokolov (Basilio), Wilhelm Vanner (Don Quixote), Vasily Geltser (Sancho), Pelageya Karpakova (Dulcinea) [documented: Petipa Society; Wikipedia]. 1871 St Petersburg: Alexandra Vergina (Kitri/Dulcinea), Lev Ivanov (Basilio), Timofei Stukolkin (Don Quixote) [documented]. Critics in 1871 "did not predict a long stage life", yet it ran 13 years in St Petersburg [documented: Mariinsky Primorsky]. 1902: Kschessinska (Kitri and Dulcinea), Nikolai Legat, Enrico Cecchetti as Sancho, Anna Pavlova as Juanita, Tamara Karsavina as Cupid [documented: Wikipedia]. Moscow audiences in 1869 "had no complaints" that it was far from Cervantes [documented as stated: Bolshoi].

### Versions and lineage
1869 Moscow (Petipa) → 1871 St Petersburg (Petipa, five acts) → 1900 Moscow (Gorsky) → 1902 St Petersburg (Gorsky) → 1923 Lopukhov fandango, 1946 Anisimova dances, Kirov → later Bolshoi editions (not traced in this pass) → 1962 Witold Borkowski for Ballet Rambert, London, the first complete production in the West [documented: Philadelphia Ballet] → 1965 Balanchine, NYCB, a different ballet to Nicolas Nabokov's music, with Suzanne Farrell as Dulcinea [documented: SF Ballet] → 1966 Rudolf Nureyev, Vienna, then Australian Ballet (filmed 1973) [probable] → 1978 Mikhail Baryshnikov, ABT [probable] → 2003 Tomasson and Possokhov, San Francisco [documented] → 2013 Carlos Acosta, Royal Ballet [probable]. Souritz argues the Kirov kept Gorsky closer than the Bolshoi, because Lopukhov curated it [documented as reported: Ballet Alert forum, citing Elizabeth Souritz].

### Readings and disputes
- **Whose ballet?** The theatres credit "Gorsky after Petipa"; the Mariinsky's own note says the modern ballet "does not have much left of Marius Petipa's original production" [documented]. Petipa accused Gorsky of theft; Gorsky changed the crowd, cut the tragic end and reused "Petipa's memorable findings from his other works" [documented: Mariinsky]. Both men's names on the poster is the compromise.
- **Spain from Russia.** A Russian Imperial fantasy of Spain, sun and passion staged "in snowy Moscow" (the Bolshoi's own phrase) [documented]. Readers in the tradition of Said see it as exotic surface; Spanish companies have staged it as their own: the Compañía Nacional de Danza under José Carlos Martínez made a version in 2015 [probable].
- **Cervantes lost?** The knight barely matters to the plot; the ballet keeps one wedding episode and the windmills. Critics have called the libretto "undistinguished" [documented: Bolshoi]. Others argue the Don is the ballet's conscience: the dreamer who walks through a comedy of real life and blesses it [argued].
- **Contradictions.** Gorsky's premiere: 6 December 1900 OS (Mariinsky) or 19 December NS (Petipa Society); the Petipa Society also says "Gorsky's 1902 revival" was the basis for modern productions, which is the Petersburg transfer of the 1900 version [contested, resolved as dates]. The 1869 act count: four acts and eight scenes (Petipa Society) or "some three acts and eight scenes" (Ballet Alert) [contested]. Cyril Beaumont's *Complete Book of Ballets* pairs Sobeshchanskaya with Ivanov, though her 1869 Basilio was Sokolov [documented as noted on the forum].

### Facts worth telling
1. Petipa had danced in Spain and claimed to play castanets like an Andalusian [documented as quoted].
2. The St Petersburg 1871 version ended with the death of Don Quixote [documented].
3. Gorsky gave each corps dancer a private part, taking his cue from Stanislavski [documented].
4. Petipa called Gorsky a shameless borrower [documented].
5. The dream scene is the only white-tutu world in a ballet of sun and fans [argued].
6. Pavlova, Karsavina and Cecchetti were all in the 1902 cast [documented].
7. It was among the ten most performed ballets in the world in 2023 [documented].

### Set it beside
- **napoli-1842** (this file): another southern city crowd with vendors and a wedding, from Copenhagen.
- **fille-mal-gardee-1789** (this file): the same plot of a parent's rich choice defeated by young love.
- **giselle-1841** (W1 §5): the vision scene of dryads in Don Quixote is the white act made into a happy dream.
- **tricorne-1919**: Massine, Falla and Picasso's Spain, made with Spanish dancers and a Spanish composer, a contrast in authenticity.

### What a maker can take from it
- Pick the episode, not the book. Petipa took one wedding from a thousand pages and made it the whole world, with the famous hero as a guest.
- Give the crowd a life. Gorsky's idea, that every member of the ensemble has a reason to be there, is what keeps the square alive after 125 years.
- One world can hold many styles if each has its place: folk dance for the square, classical dance for the dream and the wedding.

### Beyond the stage
Nureyev's 1973 film with the Australian Ballet [probable]; Baryshnikov's ABT version on television (1983) [probable]. The Kitri variation and the wedding pas de deux are staples of ballet competitions and galas worldwide [probable]. Balanchine's own *Don Quixote* (1965) is read as a portrait of his love for Farrell [documented as "perceived parallel": SF Ballet].
