## Pigmalion / Pygmalion (1734)
`id: pygmalion-salle-1734` · threads: T19, T12, T04 · confidence of the header: probable (date documented in Dacier 1909; music contested)

**Header.** Premiere 14 February 1734 (English Old Style calendar; 25 February in the French New Style), Covent Garden Theatre, London, in John Rich's company, as a dance "entertainment" inside a programme of plays [documented: Dacier 1909, appendix chronology, archive.org]. Choreography and scenario: Marie Sallé (1707 to 1756), French dancer [documented]. Music: often credited to Jean-Joseph Mouret, a French composer of stage music [contested: the credit appears in secondary sources only; no score was found in this pass]. Sets and costumes: no designer named; Sallé chose her own costume [documented: Mercure de France letter]. One act, a short ballet-pantomime of perhaps 15 to 20 minutes [probable]. Version seen today: none. The steps are lost; what survives is a printed description.

### In one line
A sculptor's statue comes to life and learns to dance from its maker, and the dancer who played the statue dressed as a statue, not as a lady of the court.

### How it came to be
Marie Sallé was the daughter of a fairground acrobat and grew up dancing at the Paris fairs and in London [documented: Universalis]. By 1733 she was a star of the Paris Opéra, but she quarrelled with its management and with the rivalry of Marie Camargo, a dancer famous for fast footwork [probable: Britannica]. In October 1733 she left Paris for London, where the manager John Rich ran a commercial playhouse with no state rules about what a dancer could wear or do [documented: Dacier; *Costume* journal article, 2016]. She opened at Covent Garden on 8 November 1733 [documented: Dacier]. On 14 February 1734 she gave her own short ballet on the Pygmalion story, with herself as the statue [documented: Dacier]. It ran for weeks. On 26 March she added a second ballet, *Bacchus and Ariadne*, in which she played a tragic heroine [documented: Dacier]. Her benefit night, when the takings went to her, was taken over by the royal family and the court [documented: Mercure letter]. A London correspondent sent a long, admiring account to the Paris monthly *Mercure de France*, and it is that letter, not any score or notation, that makes the work known [documented]. Paris never let her stage it there [probable: Potter 2013].

### Roots and reference points
- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* book X.** The sculptor Pygmalion carves an ivory woman, falls in love with her, and Venus brings her to life [documented]. The name "Galatea" for the statue is a later, eighteenth-century addition, not Ovid's [probable].
- **French opera-ballet.** Sallé trained in the *belle danse*, the noble style of the Paris Opéra, under Françoise Prévost, and had danced without masks as early as 1729 [documented: Britannica].
- **English pantomime.** Rich's theatre lived on wordless pantomime, so a London audience already knew how to read a story told by gesture [documented: *Costume* 2016, citing the commercial theatre]. John Weaver's *The Loves of Mars and Venus* (Drury Lane, 1717) is the earlier London attempt at a dance drama without words [probable].
- **Classical sculpture.** Her costume copied "the model of a Greek statue" [documented: Mercure]. The idea of the statue that wakes was a favourite of the age; it answered a philosophical question about how the senses make a mind (Condillac's statue, 1754, comes later) [argued].

### The world it built
The *Mercure* letter gives the plot in order [documented: translated in Sutherland Edwards, *History of the Opera*, Project Gutenberg]. Pygmalion enters his studio with his pupils, who dance "chisel and mallet in hand". He has a curtain drawn back to show more statues; one, in the centre, holds every eye. He sighs, touches its feet and waist, puts bracelets on its arms and diamonds at its neck, kisses its hands. He prays to Venus. The statue comes alive slowly, "astonished" at itself and at the world. Then Pygmalion dances before her "as if to instruct her", and she repeats his steps, "from the easiest to the most difficult" [documented].

That last passage is the core idea: the dance is the statue's education. The maker teaches movement, the made thing learns it and then feels the love that drives it. Susan Au says the choreography gave "the impression of a danced conversation" [documented as quoted: Wikipedia, citing Au, *Ballet and Modern Dance*]. Movement was expressive gesture joined to the noble steps of the Opéra [probable]. The music is lost or unidentified [not found in this pass].

### Design and art
The costume is the famous thing. The *Mercure* says she dared to appear "without basket, without skirt, without a dress, in her natural hair, and with no ornament on her head" [documented]. Over her bodice and under-petticoat she wore only "a simple robe of muslin", draped like a Greek statue [documented]. The "basket" is the pannier, the hooped frame that held a court skirt out sideways. Muslin is fine, light, white cotton, then imported from India [probable: Potter 2013]. So the look meant three things at once: it was antique (the statue), it was natural (her own hair), and it let the body be seen moving. No picture of the costume survives [documented: Potter 2013]. A 1734 portrait print shows Sallé in a conventional blue and white Opéra costume, which makes the contrast vivid [probable: Potter 2013].

The set was a sculptor's studio with a curtain at the back and a row of statues [documented: Mercure]. No designer is named.

### Premiere and reception
Sallé danced Galatea; the playbill names Mr Malter (probably Pygmalion) and M. Dupré among the dancers [probable: playbill quoted by Walton, from an older source]. The *Mercure* correspondent called it "this ingenious ballet" and spoke of "prodigious success" [documented]. Her benefit was sold out "for a month past" [documented]. One later account says guineas wrapped in banknotes rained on the stage and the takings passed £8,000; that sum is not credible for one night and should be treated as legend [contested: Walton blog, from a nineteenth-century source]. On 24 May 1734 the last performance was cancelled because, says Rich's own register, "Madem Sallé wou'd not come to the House" [documented: QUB Dance Biographies blog, quoting the register].

### Versions and lineage
No notation survives, and Sallé never revived it in Paris [probable]. The subject did live on: Barbara Campanini, "la Barbarina", danced a *Pygmalion* at the Prussian court, painted by Antoine Pesne at Sanssouci [documented: Dacier's footnote]. Rameau's one-act opera-ballet *Pigmalion* (Paris, 1748) and Rousseau's spoken "scène lyrique" *Pygmalion* (written 1762, staged 1770) carry the story on [probable]. Modern dancers have made new versions to period music; none claims to reconstruct hers [probable]. Sallé's Covent Garden season is sometimes called the first recorded ballet in London, which is wrong: there was dance in London theatres for decades before [argued].

### Readings and disputes
- **A reform before Noverre.** Ivor Guest and Britannica present Sallé as the forerunner of the *ballet d'action*, the story ballet that Jean-Georges Noverre argued for in his *Lettres sur la danse* (1760) (see W1 §3) [probable]. Noverre himself praised her [documented: Britannica].
- **A professional woman managing her image.** Sarah McCleave, "Marie Sallé, a Wise Professional Woman of Influence" (in Lynn Matluck Brooks, ed., *Women's Work: Making Dance in Europe before 1800*, 2007) and *Dance in Handel's London Operas* (2013), reads her choices as a careful public persona: the chaste, antique heroine against the old link between dancers and prostitution [documented as cited: Curzon 2015].
- **Was the costume new?** One scholar notes that the English dancer Hester Santlow had appeared "her hair loose" as Diana in 1723 [documented: *Costume* 2016]. So "the first uncorseted dancer" is a myth of priority; Sallé made the gesture famous because a French journal printed it [argued].
- **Was it a scandal?** Some sources say the costume "roused shock"; the *Mercure* letter is all praise [contested].
- **The date puzzle.** The *Mercure* letter is dated 15 March and says the ballet had run "nearly two months". Dacier's chronology gives a premiere of 14 February [documented]. England used the Julian calendar until 1752, eleven days behind France, so the benefit "on the first of April" in the letter is Dacier's 21 March [argued: the arithmetic fits]. Even so, "nearly two months" does not fit a February opening [contested].

### Facts worth telling
1. The only full account of the ballet is a letter in a French magazine [documented: Mercure de France, 1734].
2. Sallé's costume was a muslin shift over her underwear, with her own hair down [documented].
3. In the ballet the statue learns to dance by copying her maker, step by step [documented].
4. The register says Rich had to send an audience home when Sallé refused to come [documented: QUB blog].
5. England and France were on different calendars in 1734, so the ballet has two dates [argued].
6. Sallé is often called the earliest known woman to choreograph a ballet she danced in [probable: Britannica; "earliest known" per the rules].
7. Her student Noverre said she practised every day after retiring [probable: Wikipedia].

### Set it beside
- **coppelia-1870** (W1 §6): the woman pretends to be the doll; in Sallé the woman is the statue who becomes a woman. Both are about the maker and the made, but Coppélia mocks the maker.
- **apollon-musagete-1928**: Balanchine's god teaches muses their arts, as Pygmalion teaches the statue; and the Greek tunic returns as a modernist costume.
- **pas-de-quatre-1845** (this file): the star dancer as author of her own image, a century on.
- **medee-et-jason-1763** (W1 §3): Noverre's tragic ballet d'action, the reform Sallé is said to anticipate.

### What a maker can take from it
- A costume can carry the whole concept. One change, from pannier to muslin, told the audience what the world was (antique, natural, alive).
- Make the learning visible. The best passage shows the created thing acquiring its powers in front of us.
- Go where the rules are looser. Sallé made her boldest work in a commercial house abroad, not at the state opera at home.

### Beyond the stage
The Pygmalion story runs on through Rousseau, W. S. Gilbert's *Pygmalion and Galatea* (1871), Shaw's *Pygmalion* (1913) and *My Fair Lady* (1956, film 1964) [probable]. Sallé's own afterlife is mostly in writing on dance dress and in Handel studies, since Handel wrote the *Terpsichore* prologue (1734) and dances in *Ariodante* and *Alcina* for her [documented: Handel Friends 2022].
