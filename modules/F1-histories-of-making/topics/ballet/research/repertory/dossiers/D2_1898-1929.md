## Раймонда / Raymonda (1898)
`id: raymonda-1898` · threads: T05, T14, T20, T13 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 7 January 1898 (Old Style) / 19 January 1898 (New Style), Imperial Mariinsky Theatre, St Petersburg, Imperial Ballet, as the benefit performance of the ballerina Pierina Legnani [documented: Marius Petipa Society; Wikipedia]. Choreographer Marius Petipa, then in his eightieth year. Composer Alexander Glazunov (his op. 57, his first ballet). Scenario Countess Lydia Pashkova, revised by Petipa and the director of the Imperial Theatres, Ivan Vsevolozhsky [documented: Fullington in Macaulay 2022]. Sets Orest Allegri, Konstantin Ivanov, Pyotr Lambin; costumes Ivan Vsevolozhsky [documented: Mariinsky Theatre exhibition "Petipa in the Mariinsky", whose page shows Vsevolozhsky's costume sketches held by the St Petersburg State Theatre Library]. Conductor Riccardo Drigo. Three acts, four scenes and an apotheosis; about three hours with intervals. The version most often seen: Konstantin Sergeyev's Kirov revision (30 April 1948, designs Simon Virsaladze), which underlies most others, including Nureyev's Paris Opéra version (1983) [documented: Mariinsky playbill; Wikipedia].

### In one line
A noblewoman waits for her crusader, is courted and nearly abducted by a Saracen knight, and is saved in time for a Hungarian wedding: the last grand Petipa ballet built as a showcase of the Imperial style.

### How it came to be
After Tchaikovsky died in 1893, Vsevolozhsky needed a new composer who could work to Petipa's detailed orders. He chose Glazunov, a pupil of Rimsky-Korsakov who had never written for the stage [probable: Nureyev Foundation; Landgraf 2014]. The story came from Lydia Pashkova (1850 to 1917), a St Petersburg journalist, traveller and *Le Figaro* correspondent who had already written the scenarios for Petipa's *Cinderella* (1893) and *Bluebeard* (1896) [documented: Fullington and Konaev in Macaulay 2022]. The working title was *The White Lady*, after the ghost who protects the heroine's family [documented: Mariinsky exhibition]. From the scenario Petipa drew up a bar-by-bar plan for Glazunov, as he had done for Tchaikovsky [documented: Mariinsky exhibition]. Glazunov did not like it: he resisted Petipa's requests for changes and published his score before the performing version was fixed [documented: Fullington in Macaulay 2022]. The leading man of the house, Pavel Gerdt, also changed the plan. Petipa meant the Saracen to enter first inside Raymonda's dream; Gerdt objected that his first entrance should not be "in someone's nightmare", so a new entrance was added to Act I [probable: Marius Petipa Society, unsourced anecdote]. The ballet was made for Legnani's benefit night, so the title role was written to show every side of her technique [documented: Mariinsky exhibition].

### Roots and reference points
- **Literature.** Konaev argues that Pashkova rearranged her own semi-autobiographical novel *Hannah Pacha* (1880) [documented as his claim: Macaulay 2022]. The names come from the Fifth Crusade: Jean de Brienne and Andrew II of Hungary were real leaders, though not uncle and nephew as the ballet has it [documented: Macaulay 2022].
- **Taste of the house.** Vsevolozhsky loved the France of Louis XIV and pushed French settings into the 1890s repertory; Pashkova's Perrault ballets and this Provençal castle belong to his programme, not Petipa's [documented as Konaev's view: Macaulay 2022].
- **Earlier ballets.** The structure repeats *The Sleeping Beauty* (1890; see `../../works/W1_court_to_imperial.md` §8): a virtuoso entrance, a vision scene, a court celebration and a wedding act [probable: Marius Petipa Society]. The dream that warns a heroine looks back to the vision scenes of *La Bayadère* and *Don Quixote* (T20).
- **Music.** Glazunov's Saracen music borrows the late nineteenth-century "Oriental" idiom; the Act III Hungarian music imitates the cimbalom of Roma bands [documented: Fullington citing Marian Smith, Macaulay 2022].
- **Folk material.** The Act III *Grand pas classique hongrois* puts Hungarian character steps (the clap, the heel click, the czardas) into a classical suite: the national-character divertissement of T14, here performed by Russians as Hungarians in a French castle [argued].

### The world it built
Act I: Raymonda's name-day in the castle of Countess Sybille; a letter from Jean; the Saracen Abderakhman arrives with gifts; at dusk the White Lady leads Raymonda into a vision where she meets first Jean, then Abderakhman. Act II: a court of love; Abderakhman's retinue dances (Saracen and Spanish dances, a *panaderos*), he tries to carry Raymonda off, Jean returns and kills him in a duel, with the White Lady's help. Act III: the wedding feast at the court of King Andrew II, with a mazurka, a czardas and the *Grand pas hongrois* [documented: synopses at Mariinsky and Marius Petipa Society].

The movement language is the late Imperial mix that Petipa had perfected: classical dance for the nobility, character dance for the foreigners, mime for the plot. The heroine has an unusual number of solos; Macaulay calls one passage "a Raymonda concerto" [documented: Macaulay 2022]. Her entrance with flowers laid on the floor, the scarf dance (*pas de châle*), and the Act III variation of slow bourrées and a single hand-clap to the cimbalom-like piano are the signature passages [documented: Fullington in Macaulay 2022]. Glazunov wrote recurring motifs for each character, so the music cues the audience during the mime rather than imitating words, as older scores did [documented: Fullington in Macaulay 2022].

### Design and art
Lambin's sketch for Act I scene 2 was reproduced in the *Yearbook of the Imperial Theatres 1897 to 1898* [documented: Mariinsky exhibition]. Vsevolozhsky designed the costumes himself, as he had done for *The Sleeping Beauty*: a medieval Europe of long sleeves, wimples and tabards for the court, set against loose robes, turbans and veils for Abderakhman's retinue [probable: from the sketches shown by the Mariinsky]. The design opposed "two worlds", in the Mariinsky's phrase: a knightly idyll invaded by a "barbarian world of unbridled passions" [documented: Mariinsky exhibition, translated]. The costume, not the step, told the audience who was civilised [argued]. Virsaladze's 1948 designs, which won a Stalin Prize, set the look most people know: heavy stylised Gothic, gold and white for the wedding [documented: Mariinsky exhibition]. Nicholas Georgiadis's designs for Nureyev in Paris (1983) pushed the Saracen colour scheme toward "oriental seduction" [documented: nureyev.org]. Vikharev's La Scala reconstruction (2011) rebuilt the 1898 designs [documented: Wikipedia].

### Premiere and reception
Legnani danced Raymonda, Sergei Legat Jean de Brienne, Pavel Gerdt Abderakhman, Olga Preobrazhenskaya Henriette, Nikolai Legat Béranger and Giuseppina Cecchetti the Countess [documented: Marius Petipa Society cast list]. The *Petersburg Gazette* found "everything in Raymonda is artistic, intelligent, and beautiful" (8 January 1898) [documented as quoted: Beke, Heinz-Bosl-Stiftung essay]. Most praise went to Legnani, Petipa and Glazunov; the libretto was thought weak then and is thought weak now [probable: Marius Petipa Society; Landgraf 2014]. Money figures for the benefit: not found in this pass.

### Versions and lineage
1. Petipa 1898; notated in Stepanov notation around 1903 to 1905, with Preobrazhenskaya coached by Petipa; the notation is now in the Sergeyev Collection at Harvard [documented: Marius Petipa Society; Wikipedia gives 1905].
2. Alexander Gorsky, Bolshoi, Moscow 1908 (Ekaterina Geltser) [documented: Marius Petipa Society].
3. Fokine used the czardas and *pas hongrois* in the pastiche *Le Festin* for the 1909 Paris season (see W2 §0.1) [documented: same].
4. Vasily Vainonen, Kirov 1938, designer Valentina Khodasevich, dramaturg Yuri Slonimsky: Raymonda rejects a cold crusader and marries a good Abderakhman [documented as Konaev's account: Macaulay 2022].
5. Konstantin Sergeyev, Kirov 1948, with fragments by Fyodor Lopukhov: cut most mime, rewrote corps dances; the base text worldwide [documented: Mariinsky; Wikipedia].
6. Balanchine's *Pas de dix* (1955) and *Raymonda Variations* (1961), plotless extracts [documented: Marius Petipa Society].
7. Nureyev: Royal Ballet Touring Company, Spoleto 10 July 1964; Australian Ballet 1965; Zurich 1972; ABT 1975; Paris Opéra 1983, his "definitive" version, which gave Abderakhman a dancing solo [documented: Nureyev Foundation].
8. Yuri Grigorovich, Bolshoi 1984 (Virsaladze) [documented: Mariinsky exhibition].
9. Sergei Vikharev, La Scala, 11 October 2011, from the notation [documented].
10. Tamara Rojo, English National Ballet with Finnish National Ballet, January 2022: moved to the Crimean War, Raymonda as a nurse modelled on Florence Nightingale, using Fullington's reading of the notation [documented: Macaulay 2022].

### Readings and disputes
- **An Orientalist ballet.** Fullington says the libretto reflects nineteenth-century Crusade stories where Christians are "righteous saviours" and Muslims "treacherous barbarians", as ballets reflected the mores of their day (Fullington in Macaulay, "Raymonda and Ballet Herstory", 2022) [documented]. On this reading the problem is the story, and new productions should change it.
- **A ballet whose seducer is its best music.** Macaulay holds that Abderakhman "has the most intoxicatingly seductive music in the ballet", so the score itself undermines the plot (same essay) [documented]. Fullington answers that nothing in the sources suggests Raymonda was meant to consider him seriously [documented]. Both readings agree on the evidence and differ on what the music does to it.
- **Whose ballet?** Credit is usually "Petipa and Glazunov". Konaev and Fullington place the concept with Vsevolozhsky and Pashkova, and say the revisions make it "probably not possible to know who contributed what" [documented: Macaulay 2022]. A woman wrote the story of the most-performed late Petipa ballet, and she is rarely named on posters [argued].
- **Contradiction in the record.** Jane Pritchard's ENB programme note said Pashkova offered a Crimean War story as an alternative; Konaev, who holds a copy of the letter, says it is dated 2 September 1898, after the premiere, and does not mention the Crimea as a war setting [contested: both in Macaulay 2022].
- **Notation date.** 1903 (Marius Petipa Society) against 1905 (Wikipedia) [contested].

### Facts worth telling
- The ballet's working title was *The White Lady* [documented: Mariinsky exhibition].
- The director of the Imperial Theatres designed the costumes himself [documented: Mariinsky exhibition].
- Raymonda was kept off the Imperial stage in the First World War because its Hungarian setting tied it to an enemy power [documented as Konaev's claim: Macaulay 2022].
- In 1938 a Soviet version let Raymonda marry the Saracen [documented as Konaev's claim].
- English National Ballet's 2022 version played the Act III variation on a real cimbalom [documented: Fullington in Macaulay 2022].
- Fonteyn missed the Spoleto premiere in 1964 because her husband had been shot; Doreen Wells danced it, and Fonteyn and Nureyev later took 32 curtain calls [documented: Nureyev Foundation].
- Raymonda is the last of the big Petipa ballets that survives in repertory [documented: Mariinsky exhibition].

### Set it beside
- `sleeping-beauty-1890` (W1 §8): the same skeleton; Beauty's fairy-tale court becomes Raymonda's crusader castle, and a fairy's curse becomes a cultural threat.
- `legend-of-love-1961` (Grigorovich, Baku-sourced story, Virsaladze design): the same designer and the same Soviet house dressing an Eastern world from inside the USSR's own East.
- `tricorne-1919` (this file): another "national" divertissement, but learned from a Spanish dancer on the team rather than made up in St Petersburg.
- `corsaire-1856`: an earlier T05 abduction plot; set both beside a work made from inside the culture depicted, as the T05 wall asks.

### What a maker can take from it
- A strong form can outlive a weak story: the structure (entrance, vision, court, wedding) and the score carried this ballet for 125 years while every producer rewrote the plot.
- The person who sets the house style (here a theatre director who drew costumes) shapes a world as much as the named author. Credit the brief-writer.
- When the antagonist gets the best material, the audience's sympathy moves. Design the "other" with care, because the costume and music speak louder than the plot.

### Beyond the stage
Glazunov's concert suite (op. 57a) is a standard orchestral item [probable]. The La Scala reconstruction exists on DVD [documented: Macaulay 2022]. The *Grand pas* is a staple of ballet competitions and galas [probable]. Wider popular-culture use: not found in this pass.

## Лебедь, later Умирающий лебедь / The Swan, later The Dying Swan (1905 or 1907)
`id: dying-swan-1905` · threads: T03, T13, T02 · confidence of the header: contested (date and place)

**Header.** First performance at a charity concert by the chorus of the Imperial Opera, St Petersburg, danced by Anna Pavlova. Date and place are contested: Fokine wrote that he made it "in 1905" and that it was first given in the hall of the Assembly of Nobility [documented: Fokine, *The Dying Swan*, J. Fischer, New York, 1925, and his manuscript draft in the St Petersburg State Theatre Library, f. 11]; Soviet and Russian reference works give 22 December 1907 (Old Style; 4 January 1908 New Style), a date fixed by Vera Krasovskaya in her 1962 edition of Fokine's memoirs, and several place it on the Mariinsky stage [documented: *Russkii balet* encyclopedia 1997, via pro-ballet.ru; Mariinsky playbill; gramota.net article 2026]. Choreographer Mikhail (Michel) Fokine. Music Camille Saint-Saëns, "Le Cygne" from *Le Carnaval des animaux* (1886). No scenario. Costume first attributed to Léon Bakst [probable: London Museum; Bibliothèque-musée de l'Opéra]. One solo, about two and a half to four minutes. The version most often seen: not Fokine's text but the many ballerinas' variants built on it, above all Maya Plisetskaya's [argued].

### In one line
A single dancer in white, a swan, struggles against death and loses: a whole ballet compressed into one song.

### How it came to be
Two stories survive, and they do not agree. In Fokine's own account, Pavlova, newly a ballerina at the Mariinsky, was asked to dance at a concert given by the Imperial Opera chorus and asked him for a number. He had been practising Saint-Saëns's "Swan" on the mandolin and suggested it. He made the dance in a few minutes, "almost an improvisation": he danced in front of her and she followed just behind [documented: Fokine's memoir quoted on the Mariinsky playbill, translated]. A pupil of Fokine, Maria Gorshkova, told a different story to the historian Galina Dobrovolskaya: Pavlova was to dance the *Swan Lake* adagio, her partner fell ill hours before, the Odette costume was already out of the wardrobe, and Fokine found the Saint-Saëns among her music and set it in about fifteen minutes [documented as Gorshkova's memory: Dobrovolskaya 1961, quoted in the gramota.net article 2026]. Either way, the solo was made fast, for a charity night, for one body. Pavlova, who later kept swans on the lake at her London house, made it hers; by her death in 1931 she had danced it some four thousand times [probable: Wikipedia, Russian Wikipedia].

### Roots and reference points
- **Music.** Saint-Saëns wrote "Le Cygne" for cello and two pianos as the thirteenth movement of a private musical joke, *The Carnival of the Animals*, which he barred from publication in his lifetime except for this one movement [probable]. The piece is in G major and the swan in it does not die; Saint-Saëns is said to have been surprised by the tragic reading [probable: Russian Wikipedia, unsourced].
- **Poetry and symbol.** Accounts in English say Pavlova loved Tennyson's "The Dying Swan" (1830) [probable: secondary, contested as a source]. Russian writers point instead to Konstantin Balmont's "The Swan" (1895), which was sometimes recited with the dance, and to Mikhail Vrubel's swan paintings [documented as their claim: belcanto.ru; Lyubimov 2025]. The old belief that a swan sings once, beautifully, before it dies lies behind all of them.
- **Ballet.** The swan-maidens of *Swan Lake* (1877/1895; W1 §10) gave the costume and the arm language; Fokine took the bird and dropped the princess. The bourrées, tiny travelling steps on pointe, stand for the gliding swan; the arms carry the drama [argued].
- **Reform.** Fokine said he made it in answer to critics who accused him of loving barefoot dancing (Isadora Duncan's influence) and rejecting pointe; the solo proved pointe could serve expression [documented: Mariinsky playbill, Fokine memoir excerpt].

### The world it built
There is no plot and no other character. Fokine's 1925 description sets it out: at curtain rise the dancer stands still at the back of a dark stage, lit as if by moonlight, head bowed. She travels in continuous bourrées across and around the stage, arms rippling like wings, strives upward, weakens, falters toward the footlights, sinks on one knee and folds over her extended leg [documented: Fokine 1925, archive.org; Levinson's description quoted in Ferrier 2021]. Fokine insisted the arms matter as much as, "if indeed not more" than, the legs [documented: Fokine 1925]. The music is a single cello line over arpeggios; the bourrées match the rippling accompaniment and the arms sing the melody [probable: Russian Wikipedia]. Fokine asked for the curtain to rise before the music begins and to fall "very slowly" so the audience sees the dancer lie still for about five seconds [documented: Fokine 1925]. Silence and stillness are part of the choreography [argued].

### Design and art
Fokine specified the costume: the traditional ballet tunic "trimmed with swan down, feathers and wings", all white except very light pink tights and slippers, and preferred cello with harp to the original pianos [documented: Fokine 1925]. Pavlova's costumes were remade before each run by her wardrobe mistress, Madame Manya (Kharchevnikova) [documented: London Museum]. The London Museum's example, given by Pavlova's partner Victor Dandré in 1931 and probably from the late 1920s, is a cream silk boned bodice with a green glass gem at the centre, a cotton net skirt sewn with gelatine sequins, goose-feather "wings" on the skirt's plate, and a diamanté headdress of green glass gems trimmed with white feathers [documented: London Museum object 31.81/1a]. A second, said to be Bakst's of 1907, is in the Bibliothèque-musée de l'Opéra, Paris, and a third in a Californian collection [documented: Hamilton 2020]. The green stone reads as a wound or a heart; it is often described as a ruby brooch, though the surviving gem is green [argued; colour documented]. The famous images are studio photographs of Pavlova with arms crossed over her head or folded in the final pose.

### Premiere and reception
Pavlova danced it first; it was, in Fokine's words, a "great success" and became her "favorite number" [documented: Fokine 1925]. Reviews of the first night: not found in this pass. Its United States debut was at the Metropolitan Opera House, New York, 18 March 1910 [probable]. The critic Carl Van Vechten called it "the most exquisite specimen of her art" [probable: quoted on Russian Wikipedia]. André Levinson's description, quoted above, is the fullest eyewitness reading [documented as quoted: Ferrier 2021]. The economics are the point: a four-minute solo with one costume and one cellist could tour to Lima, Buenos Aires or a provincial music hall, so it carried ballet to audiences who never saw a company [argued].

### Versions and lineage
1. Fokine for Pavlova, 1905 or 1907.
2. Other dancers took it up "almost immediately" and changed it, so Fokine published an authorised text in 1925, with a stage diagram and 36 photographs of his wife Vera Fokina posing it [documented: Fokine 1925, archive.org].
3. Pavlova filmed it in 1925 (silent); this is the only moving record of her in it [probable: Wikipedia filmography].
4. Galina Ulanova, Natalia Dudinskaya, Plisetskaya (from 1944, taught by her aunt Sulamith Messerer), Lopatkina and others in the Soviet and Russian line; Plisetskaya's version, with its rippling back and boneless arms, is the one most copied [documented for the list: *Russkii balet* 1997; New Republic 2001].
5. Les Ballets Trockadero de Monte Carlo's comic version, in which the swan moults feathers, as a drag parody [probable].
6. Alexei Miroshnichenko's duet version for the Arabesque competition, Perm 2014 [documented: Lyubimov 2025].
7. Dada Masilo's Saint-Saëns finale in *Swan Lake* (2010), see W3 §7 [documented in W3].

### Readings and disputes
- **About death, not a swan.** Fokine's granddaughter Isabelle Fokine argues the dance "is not about a swan, but it is about death, and the swan is simply a metaphor"; modern dancers, she says, turn it into another Odette variation [documented as quoted: interlude.hk 2024]. Fokine himself called it "a symbol of the perpetual longing for life by all mortals" [documented as quoted: New Republic 2001].
- **Owned by the dancer.** Against that, the record shows the solo was always remade by each ballerina, Pavlova first; by this reading the Dying Swan is a vehicle, not a fixed text, and its life is in the variants [argued, with evidence in Fokine's own complaint of 1925].
- **The date.** Fokine (1905, Hall of Nobility) against the Soviet reference record (22 December 1907, Krasovskaya's dating; Mariinsky stage in some sources, Hall of Nobility in others). Dobrovolskaya kept 1907 even after working with Fokine's draft [documented: gramota.net 2026]. Fokine's memory was written twenty years later; the 1907 date rests on programmes Krasovskaya consulted [probable]. The register should carry both.
- **The title.** It was announced as *The Swan*; "Dying" was added later, as Pavlova darkened the ending [documented: Lyubimov 2025; *Russkii balet*].
- **Myth.** Pavlova's last words, "Get my Swan costume ready", come from Dandré's account [documented as Dandré's claim: Wikipedia]. Treat as reported, not verified.

### Facts worth telling
- Fokine was practising the music on a mandolin when he chose it [documented: Lyubimov 2025].
- It was made in minutes; Fokine called it "almost an improvisation" [documented: Mariinsky playbill].
- Pavlova danced it about four thousand times [probable].
- The surviving London costume has a green, not red, stone [documented: London Museum].
- Fokine's own instructions end with five seconds of the dancer lying still before the curtain closes [documented: Fokine 1925].
- Fokine published the dance as a booklet with 36 photographs so that "anyone versed in the rudimentary technic" could perform it [documented: editor's note, Fokine 1925].
- Saint-Saëns's swan is in a major key and does not die [probable].

### Set it beside
- `swan-lake-1877-1895` (W1 §10): the source of the image, cut from four acts to four minutes.
- `swan-lake-bourne-1995`: the swan as male, muscular and aggressive; the same bird reversing the gender of the role.
- `swan-lake-masilo-2010` (W3 §7): Saint-Saëns's music turned into a requiem for people killed by stigma around AIDS.
- `autumn-leaves-pavlova-1918`: Pavlova's own choreography, the dancer turned author.

### What a maker can take from it
- The smallest complete world: one figure, one action, one light, one costume. Scale down until only the essential remains, and see whether the idea still holds.
- A portable form travels further than a grand one. The solo carried a whole art form round the world because it could be packed in one trunk.
- If others will remake your work, publish a reference version, as Fokine did in 1925, or accept that the variants become the work.

### Beyond the stage
Pavlova's 1925 film; *The Immortal Swan* (1935), a compilation film on Pavlova [probable]; Emil Loteanu's biopic *Anna Pavlova* (1983) re-enacts its making [documented: Lyubimov 2025]; Sonja Henie skated a "swan" on ice [probable: skating history pages]. The image of the dying swan is a fixed metaphor in popular culture for exaggerated, theatrical collapse (in football commentary, for instance) [probable].

## Шопениана / Chopiniana, then Les Sylphides / The Sylphs (1907, 1908, 1909)
`id: les-sylphides-1909` · threads: T02, T10, T13, T01 · confidence of the header: documented for 1909; contested for the 1908 date and the costume credit

**Header.** Three stages. (1) *Chopiniana*, a charity performance at the Mariinsky Theatre, St Petersburg, 10 February 1907 (Old Style; 23 February New Style), five genre scenes to Glazunov's orchestration of Chopin [documented: Bolshoi Theatre page; Concise Oxford Dictionary of Music via encyclopedia.com]. (2) A plotless second version, often called *Rêverie romantique: ballet sur la musique de Chopin*, Mariinsky, 8 March 1908 (ABT and Bolshoi; Old Style presumed) or "6 April 1908" (EBSCO), with Preobrazhenskaya, Pavlova, Karsavina and Nijinsky [contested date; documented cast: ABT archive]. (3) *Les Sylphides*, Ballets Russes, Théâtre du Châtelet, Paris, 2 June 1909, five performances that season [documented: Les Archives du spectacle; ABT]. Choreographer Mikhail Fokine. Music Frédéric Chopin, orchestrated in 1909 by Glazunov (Polonaise or Prelude, the C sharp minor Waltz), Stravinsky (Nocturne op. 32 no. 2 and Grande Valse brillante op. 18), and Liadov, Taneyev, Tcherepnin and Sokolov for the rest [documented: Concise Oxford; Dialnet article citing Walsh 2002]. Conductor Nikolai Tcherepnin. Set Alexandre Benois; costumes Benois or Léon Bakst (contested, below). One act, about 30 minutes. The version most often seen: Fokine's 1909 order, under the title *Chopiniana* in Russia (with Keller's 1908 orchestrations) and *Les Sylphides* elsewhere (with later orchestrations, often Roy Douglas's) [documented: Dialnet; Sarasota Ballet].

### In one line
A young poet in black among women in long white skirts, by moonlight, dances to Chopin with no story at all: a ballet about the memory of the Romantic ballet.

### How it came to be
In 1907 Fokine, aged 26, found in a music shop Glazunov's orchestral suite *Chopiniana* (op. 46, 1892 to 1893) and asked Glazunov to orchestrate one more piece, the C sharp minor Waltz [documented: EBSCO; Wikipedia]. For a charity night he made five scenes: Poles dancing a polonaise in a ballroom; Chopin himself at the piano in a ruined monastery, tormented by dead monks and saved by his muse; a peasant girl escaping a marriage to an old man in a mazurka; the Waltz, a classical duet for Pavlova as a sylph; and a tarantella under Vesuvius [documented: Bolshoi; EBSCO]. The Waltz was the seed. A year later Fokine rebuilt the whole ballet round it, dropping national costumes, plot and the character of Chopin, and asking the Mariinsky's répétiteur Maurice Keller to orchestrate new pieces [documented: Wikipedia; davidnice 2012 summarising Taruskin 1996]. In 1909 Diaghilev took this second version to Paris. He disliked Keller's arrangements and commissioned new ones, partly to show off Russian composers, including the young Stravinsky, whom he was testing before *Firebird* (W2 §0.1) [documented: Dialnet; W2]. Against Fokine's wishes he renamed it *Les Sylphides*, to call up *La Sylphide* (1832) [documented as claim: Dialnet; EBSCO].

### Roots and reference points
- **The Romantic ballet.** The title, the long skirts and the moonlit setting quote *La Sylphide* (Paris 1832; W1 §4) and the white acts of *Giselle* (1841; W1 §5). The costume quotes the lithographs of Marie Taglioni [probable: Wikipedia; Frolova 2020].
- **The Petipa and Ivanov white acts.** The corps patterns descend from the Kingdom of the Shades (*La Bayadère*, 1877) and *Swan Lake* Act II (1895) [probable: Sarasota Ballet note].
- **Retrospectivism.** The *Mir iskusstva* (World of Art) circle of Benois and Diaghilev loved the past remade with modern taste; *Les Sylphides*, *Le Pavillon d'Armide* and the 1910 *Giselle* all belong to that programme [probable: Frolova, "The Ballets Russes: Playing with the Past", Gresham College 2020; Garafola 1989].
- **Chopin as a figure.** In 1907 Chopin was a character; in 1909 he survives only as the music and, some say, in the Poet [argued].
- **Reform.** Fokine claimed the ballet expressed his reforms "more clearly than in any other, more clearly even than in my own programme of reforms" [documented as quoted: Frolova 2020]. Compare his five principles of 1914 (W2 §0.2).

### The world it built
The 1909 order: an overture (Prelude op. 28 no. 7, or the Polonaise in the earliest runs); Nocturne for all; Waltz (female soloist); Mazurka (female soloist, with big crossing leaps); Mazurka (the Poet); Prelude (female soloist, a listening, hand-to-ear figure); Waltz pas de deux for the Poet and a sylph; Grande Valse brillante for all [documented in outline: Concise Oxford; Dialnet]. The corps of sylphs frames the soloists in still tableaux, then moves in lines and garlands, so the group is part of the composition, not a background [probable: EBSCO]. Fokine's movement softened the Petipa body: rounded arms, inclined heads, a forward tilt from the waist, long held balances, steps that flow rather than stop for applause [probable]. There are no virtuoso tricks. Each piece of music gets one mood, and the dance follows its phrasing; this is why later writers call it a "mood ballet" and a forerunner of plotless ballet (T10) [probable: Sarasota Ballet; argued].

### Design and art
Benois painted a "mournful, romantic setting", a deserted ruin by night, its darkness relieved by a glowing dark green and quivering patches of simulated moonlight [documented: EBSCO summary]. The NGA calls his sets "moody moonlit" [documented: NGA brochure 2013]. The sylphs wear long white Romantic tutus of tarlatan and cotton net to mid-calf, small double wings at the back, white flowers in their hair and small bunches of forget-me-nots at the breast [documented: V&A S.874-1980; EBSCO]. The Poet wears white tights and a black velvet tunic with a white open collar, the costume of the Romantic hero; Nijinsky wore a rather unflattering long fair wig [documented: Sarasota; probable: davidnice]. The look is monochrome: white bodies against a dark green-black ground, which is itself a design statement after the colour of Bakst's *Cléopâtre* in the same season [argued]. Later productions substituted sets after Corot's landscapes (Eugene Dunkel for Ballet Theatre) [documented: ABT archive].

### Premiere and reception
Paris, 2 June 1909: Pavlova (Mazurka), Karsavina (Waltz), Alexandra Baldina (Prelude) and Nijinsky (the Poet) [documented: ABT; Les Archives du spectacle]. It was close enough to French tradition not to shock and was praised as the season's most refined work [probable: EBSCO]. Fokine was said to have restaged it in three days [probable: EBSCO]. The season as a whole lost money (W2 §0.1). Diaghilev called it his favourite ballet; it was the company's second most performed work, and dancers thought it an honour to be cast [documented: V&A; Sarasota]. Its US premiere was by the Ballets Russes at the Century Theatre, New York, 20 January 1916, with Lopokova [documented: ABT].

### Versions and lineage
1. 1907 *Chopiniana*; never revived [documented: Dialnet].
2. 1908 second *Chopiniana*; still the Russian text, with Keller's orchestration, sometimes credited wrongly to Glazunov alone [documented: Dialnet].
3. 1909 *Les Sylphides*, in the Ballets Russes repertory until 1929; revived in Paris 1926 [documented: Les Archives du spectacle].
4. Ballet Theatre (ABT), New York, 11 January 1940; Benjamin Britten made an orchestration for it in 1941, thought lost, used again in 2013 [documented: ABT archive].
5. Kirov and Bolshoi as *Chopiniana*: Galina Ulanova danced it at her graduation and at her farewell [documented: Bolshoi page].
6. British stagings by Alicia Markova and others, with orchestrations by Roy Douglas, Gordon Jacob or Malcolm Sargent [documented: Sarasota; Concise Oxford].
Fokine's copyright passed to his estate, which still licenses performances [documented: ABT note "by kind permission of the Fokine Estate"].

### Readings and disputes
- **The earliest plotless ballet, a step to abstraction.** EBSCO's essay "Fokine's *Les Sylphides* Introduces Abstract Ballet" and many programmes read it as the start of the line that leads to Balanchine (T10) [documented].
- **Not classical, not abstract.** Fokine "always resented any reference to *Les Sylphides* as a pure classical ballet", because he made it as a revolt against classical rigidity (Bauer 1971, quoted in the Dialnet article) [documented as quoted]. On this reading it is a *Romantic* mood piece with a dreamer at its centre, not abstraction [argued]. Both readings use the same evidence: no story. They differ on whether the Poet's reverie counts as one.
- **A remake about a remake.** Frolova (Gresham College 2020) frames the Ballets Russes as "playing with the past": the ballet is nostalgia made modern, a white act about white acts [documented as her frame].
- **Costume credit.** Benois designed sets and costumes (V&A, ABT, BnF-derived Archives du spectacle) [documented]; or Bakst designed the long tutu, from Taglioni lithographs, first for Pavlova in 1907 to 1908, and the corps adopted it (Wikipedia; Frolova 2020) [documented as their claims]. Possible reconciliation: Bakst for Pavlova's St Petersburg costume, Benois for the 1909 production [argued].
- **The 1908 date.** 8 March (ABT, Bolshoi) or 6 April (EBSCO); possibly the same night in different calendars, though 8 March OS is 21 March NS [contested].
- **Who orchestrated what.** The Dialnet author found "no source" confirming which of Liadov, Taneyev, Tcherepnin and Sokolov scored which piece [documented].

### Facts worth telling
- The 1907 version had Chopin as a character, haunted by ghostly monks [documented: Bolshoi; EBSCO].
- Stravinsky's earliest work for Diaghilev was orchestrating two Chopin pieces for this ballet [documented: W2 §0.1; Concise Oxford].
- Russian companies still call it *Chopiniana*; the West uses Diaghilev's title [documented: Bolshoi].
- Six composers' hands were in the 1909 orchestra pit [documented: Les Archives du spectacle lists six].
- Ulanova began and ended her career in it [documented: Bolshoi].
- A Britten orchestration was lost for decades and found again [documented: ABT].
- The V&A's sylph costume, worn by Lopokova around 1916, has wire-framed wings [documented: V&A].

### Set it beside
- `sylphide-1832` (W1 §4): a full Scottish story becomes a mood; the sylph loses her plot.
- `serenade-1934`: Balanchine's women in long blue-white skirts and moonlight, with a story suggested but never told; the direct descendant.
- `giselle-khan-2016`: the white corps remade as ghosts of garment workers, so the white act carries a social history.
- `les-noces-1923` (W2 §7): the same company, fourteen years later; Nijinska's corps as one mass instead of a garland.

### What a maker can take from it
- Remove the story and keep the rules: a palette (white on green-black), one costume type, one musical source and one recurring figure can hold a world together.
- A second draft can be a different work. Fokine kept one number and threw away the rest, and the one number held the idea.
- A title is a frame. Diaghilev's renaming told Paris what to see (a homage to *La Sylphide*) before the curtain rose.

### Beyond the stage
The "sylph" look (long white tulle, wreath of flowers) passed into wedding dresses and children's ballet-school images [probable]. Chopin's pieces from the ballet are widely recorded in the Douglas orchestration [probable]. Film appearances: not found in this pass.

## Le Spectre de la rose / The Spirit of the Rose (1911)
`id: spectre-de-la-rose-1911` · threads: T20, T01, T18, T22 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 19 April 1911, Théâtre de Monte-Carlo, Ballets Russes; first London performance 24 June 1911, Royal Opera House [documented: V&A S.838-1983; LoC Nijinska Collection]. Choreographer Mikhail Fokine. Music Carl Maria von Weber, *Aufforderung zum Tanz* (Invitation to the Dance, piano, 1819), in Hector Berlioz's orchestration of 1841. Scenario Jean-Louis Vaudoyer after Théophile Gautier's poem "Le Spectre de la rose" (in *La Comédie de la mort*, 1838). Set and costumes Léon Bakst. One act, two dancers, about ten minutes [documented: Mariinsky playbill]. The version most often seen: Fokine's, passed down by coaching (Karsavina coached it in Britain), in Bakst-based designs [probable].

### In one line
A girl home from her first ball falls asleep in a chair; the rose she wore comes alive, dances with her in her dream, and leaps out of the window before she wakes.

### How it came to be
In 1910 the writer and critic Jean-Louis Vaudoyer, struck by a line of Gautier, "I am the spectre of a rose that you wore at the ball yesterday", proposed a ballet in a letter to Bakst, joining the poem to Weber's waltz [documented: Wikipedia; Opéra Royal de Wallonie-Liège 2026]. Nothing happened for months. In spring 1911 Diaghilev needed a short piece: Nijinsky's *L'Après-midi d'un faune* was not ready (W2 §4), and 1911 was the centenary of Gautier's birth [probable: Wikipedia, citing Buckle]. He remembered Vaudoyer's idea. Fokine made the dance in three or four rehearsals and later called much of it "almost an improvisation" [documented as Fokine's account: Wikipedia; Liège 2026]. Diaghilev thought it a trifle; it became one of the company's most loved works [probable: Grace Robert, *The Borzoi Book of Ballets*, 1946, cited by Wikipedia]. A gap in the season, a stray letter and a birthday produced the most famous male role of the century [argued].

### Roots and reference points
- **Poetry.** Gautier's poem is spoken by the rose itself, who has died on the girl's breast and is happier for it; he had also written the scenario of *Giselle* (1841; W1 §5), so the ballet quietly honours the Romantic ballet's own author [documented for Giselle; argued for the homage].
- **Music.** Weber's piece is the earliest well-known concert waltz with a narrative frame: a slow introduction (the invitation), the waltz, and a coda where the couple part. Berlioz orchestrated it to insert into a Paris staging of Weber's opera *Der Freischütz* in 1841 [probable]. The ballet follows that frame exactly [argued].
- **Earlier ballets.** The dream visitor who enters by the window and leaves by it repeats *La Sylphide* (1832; W1 §4) with the sexes reversed: here the supernatural being is a man [argued].
- **Taste.** Benois called it "a graceful little bagatelle in the style of the romantic salon ballads of the 1830s" [documented as quoted: Perm Opera]: the same retrospectivism as *Les Sylphides*.

### The world it built
One room, one night, two people. The girl enters in her ball gown and cloak, remembers the dance, smells the rose, sinks into an armchair and sleeps. The Spectre bounds in through the window; their waltz is the body of the ballet; he guides her back to the chair and leaves by the window in a single leap; she wakes, finds the fallen rose and presses it to her [documented in outline: Nureyev Foundation synopsis; LoC]. Fokine said the difficulty was confining the dancing to "a tiny room" whose two walls met upstage [documented: LoC]. For Nijinsky he dropped the classical arm positions and used curving, tendril-like arms and hands, so the role joins masculine power in the legs with delicacy in the arms [probable: Wikipedia, citing Ostwald 1991]. Fokine described the aim: "He is a spirit. He is a dream. He is the scent of a rose" [documented: Mariinsky playbill]. The dance never stops to show off, and it is continuous across Weber's sections, so it reads as one breath rather than a suite of numbers [probable: Robert 1946].

### Design and art
Bakst's room is a Biedermeier girl's bedroom of the 1830s: pale walls (blue-white in surviving designs), two tall French windows at the back open on a summer night, a dressing table, a bird cage, an armchair [probable: from photographs and the Perm reconstruction]. The Spectre's costume is the famous object. The V&A's example is a sleeveless painted silk-jersey leotard in deep pink and violet with green tendrils, overlaid with silk petals graded from pink to purple; footed tights painted pink with tendrils on the thighs; and a cap covered in petals from pale pink to mauve [documented: V&A S.838-1983, which notes it was probably made for Stanislas Idzikowski]. Nijinsky was stitched into it for each show, and the wardrobe mistress re-curled the petals with a curling iron afterwards [probable: Wikipedia; Liège 2026]. Romola de Pulszky, later Nijinsky's wife, said his make-up made his face "like that of a celestial insect" [documented as quoted: V&A]. The costume makes the man a flower: not a man dressed as a rose, but a rose shaped like a man [argued]. Jean Cocteau's 1911 poster of Karsavina and Nijinsky became an emblem of the company [documented: Mariinsky playbill].

### Premiere and reception
Karsavina danced the Young Girl and Nijinsky the Spectre [documented]. The success was immediate [probable: Robert 1946]. The leap became the story: Nijinsky took five running steps and leapt on the sixth; the low skirting under the window made the jump look higher, men behind the set caught him, and the conductor held the penultimate chord so the flight seemed longer [probable: Wikipedia, sources not checked]. No film exists of Nijinsky in it [documented: Liège 2026]. Nijinsky's dresser (or servant, Vasili) sold fallen petals to fans; the story that he built a house, "Le Château du Spectre de la Rose", on the profits is legend [documented as told: V&A; Wikipedia].

### Versions and lineage
1. Fokine 1911; in the Ballets Russes repertory to 1929.
2. Revived and coached in the West by Karsavina and others; Margot Fonteyn and Alexis Rassine at Sadler's Wells [documented: photograph credit, The Ballet Spot].
3. Nureyev: German television, Frankfurt, 1961, taught by Pierre Lacotte; on stage only in 1979, 24 times with the Joffrey Ballet's Diaghilev homage, then with Fonteyn and London Festival Ballet at the London Coliseum [documented: Nureyev Foundation].
4. Mariinsky Theatre since 1997; Perm Opera 2009 (staged by Elena Solovyova) [documented: playbills].
5. New choreographies under the same title and music by Angelin Preljocaj (Paris Opéra), Thierry Malandain, Benjamin Millepied, and a parody by Maurice Béjart [documented: Liège 2026].

### Readings and disputes
- **The androgynous body.** Peter Ostwald (*Vaslav Nijinsky: A Leap into Madness*, 1991) reads gestures that "lent a feminine aura" and a costume like a ballerina's [documented as quoted: Wikipedia]. Queer readings of the Nijinsky roles are developed by Kevin Kopelson, *The Queer Afterlife of Vaslav Nijinsky* (1997) [probable].
- **The girl's dream.** Against the focus on Nijinsky, the ballet can be read as the girl's: her desire, her first ball, her memory, with the Spectre as her projection. The Young Girl has been called "the forgotten woman of ballet" [documented as quoted: Wikipedia, source not traced]. Both readings fit the action; they differ on whose interior the ballet shows.
- **The leap as madness.** Perm's programme notes that historians later read the leap as "the dancer's leap into madness and immortality" [documented]. This is a retrospective myth built on Nijinsky's later illness, not evidence about 1911 [argued].
- **Contradiction.** Accounts disagree on whether Vaudoyer wrote to Bakst in 1910 or proposed it in conversation [contested: Wikipedia letter; Liège "mentioned it"]. Who sold the petals: "his dresser" (V&A) or "his servant Vasili" (Wikipedia) [contested].

### Facts worth telling
- The ballet lasts about ten minutes and has two dancers [documented].
- It existed because another ballet was late [probable].
- The surviving V&A costume bears Nijinsky's name but was probably made for his successor Idzikowski [documented: V&A].
- Petals were re-curled with a curling iron after each show [probable].
- The famous height of the leap was partly a stage trick [probable].
- Nureyev first danced it for television, the year he defected [documented: Nureyev Foundation].
- Gautier, whose poem it adapts, also wrote the scenario of *Giselle* [documented].

### Set it beside
- `sylphide-1832` (W1 §4): the window-borne spirit, sex-reversed.
- `apres-midi-faune-1912` (W2 §4): Nijinsky's next role, as his own choreographer, where the animal-man replaces the flower-man and the erotic charge is made explicit.
- `swan-lake-bourne-1995`: another male creature of desire whose costume (bare chest, feathered breeches) makes the man an animal.
- `jeune-homme-et-la-mort-1946`: a young man, a room, a window and a fatal visitor: the 1911 dream turned to a nightmare.

### What a maker can take from it
- One unforgettable exit can carry a whole work. Design the last image first.
- A costume can define a character so completely that the role cannot be recast without it: the petal suit is the Spectre.
- Constraint breeds invention: a tiny room and ten minutes forced Fokine to make the space and the music do the storytelling.

### Beyond the stage
Cocteau's 1911 poster; Nijinsky in the role is one of the most reproduced dance photographs [probable]. The Mariinsky notes Cocteau's image as a symbol of the new ballet for many Europeans [documented]. Weber's *Invitation to the Dance* became better known through the ballet [probable]. Perfumes and fashion named after the ballet exist (for example a perfume "Spectre de la rose"): not verified in this pass.

## Jeux / Games (1913)
`id: jeux-1913` · threads: T11, T09, T22 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 15 May 1913, Théâtre des Champs-Élysées, Paris, Ballets Russes, opening the company's eighth Paris season, on a bill with *The Firebird* and *Schéhérazade*; London as *Playtime*, Drury Lane, 25 June 1913 [documented: Théâtre des Champs-Élysées 2023; Hodson and Archer 2013]. Choreographer and scenario Vaslav Nijinsky. Music Claude Debussy, *Jeux, poème dansé*, composed August 1912 to April 1913, conductor Pierre Monteux [documented: Centre de documentation Claude Debussy]. Set and costumes Léon Bakst. One act, three dancers, about 18 minutes. The version most often seen: none of the original in repertory; the score lives in the concert hall, and the dance survives through Millicent Hodson and Kenneth Archer's reconstruction (Verona 1996) [documented].

### In one line
At dusk in a park, a young man and two women looking for a lost tennis ball flirt, pair off, and end in a three-way embrace, until a second ball scatters them: the earliest known ballet set in its own present.

### How it came to be
In the summer of 1912, in London, Nijinsky and Bakst watched the painter Duncan Grant playing tennis in Lady Ottoline Morrell's garden in Bedford Square, a young man in whites against darkening green; "Ah, quel décor," they sighed, as the story goes [documented as Hodson and Archer's account, DanceTabs 2013]. The idea came over lunch between Diaghilev, Nijinsky and the painter Jacques-Émile Blanche, who was asked to telegram Debussy; Debussy wired back "Subject ballet *Jeux* idiotic, not interested", then accepted when Diaghilev doubled the fee [documented: Pasler 1982, citing Blanche's memoir *La Pêche aux souvenirs*, 1949; Wikipedia]. Years later Nijinsky wrote in his diary that Diaghilev had wanted a ballet about a love affair between three young men, and that he himself had wanted an aeroplane crash in it [documented as diary claim: Wikipedia; Pasler]. Nijinsky turned the male trio into a man and two women, which gave him "more dramatic scope and no doubt less censorship" [documented as Hodson and Archer's reading]. He made *Jeux* while also rehearsing *The Rite of Spring* (W2 §5); his sister Bronislava Nijinska, his assistant and intended cast, became pregnant and was replaced by Ludmilla Schollar [documented: Hodson and Archer 2013].

### Roots and reference points
- **Life.** Tennis, then a fashionable sport of the leisured class; the turkey trot and tango, new social dances [documented: Hodson and Archer]. Grant is widely accepted as a model for the male role; Hodson and Archer also propose Virginia Woolf and Vanessa Bell for the women, on evidence from Nijinska's daughter Irina [documented as their claim; they call some links "unlikely" themselves].
- **Painting.** Nijinsky took poses from Gauguin and faces like Modigliani's, "empty of expression but full of meaning" [documented as Hodson and Archer]. Hodson's book sets the trio beside the Three Graces in Gauguin's *The Alyscamps* (1888) [documented: book contents].
- **Earlier works.** *L'Après-midi d'un faune* (1912; W2 §4) had the same composer, designer and choreographer; *Jeux* swaps antiquity for the present. A gesture from *The Firebird* (1910) is quoted when Debussy quotes Rimsky-Korsakov [documented: Hodson and Archer].
- **Machine age (T09).** Nijinsky said he wanted to show the man of the future, and Hodson and Archer read the stop-and-start phrasing as cinematic [probable].

### The world it built
Eight episodes in Hodson's reconstruction: Prelude, Entrances, Women's Secrets, First, Second and Third Coupling, Tripling, Interruption [documented: book contents]. Debussy's score has some sixty tempo markings and motifs often only two bars long, so the music is in constant flux, matched to the shifting relations of the trio; Jann Pasler shows it shaped as "psychological" time [documented: Pasler; Wikipedia]. Debussy joked that Nijinsky was "a very mathematical gentleman", always "adding up crotchets" [documented as quoted: Hodson and Archer]. The movement squares off ballet's curves: parallel feet, flexed wrists, flat-footed pliés, a *jeté* with the downstage leg closed to the audience and the arms in a tennis serve, and groupings held like sculpture; Diaghilev called the final interlaced embrace "the fountain" and declared it a masterpiece [documented: Hodson and Archer]. The women dance on pointe.

### Design and art
Bakst's set is a park at night: dark trees, flowerbeds, a white house or wall, and large electric lamps casting "fantastic rays", an evening lit by electricity rather than moonlight [documented: premiere scenario, Wikipedia; Bakst's charcoal design of 1912 with free-standing flats, listed in Hodson 2008]. Nijinsky asked Bakst to imagine tennis dress "in 1920": for the women knee-length A-line skirts with cropped jumpers; for the man, shorts with braces, which Diaghilev rejected, so Nijinsky wore a version of his rehearsal clothes, a silk shirt and close-cut breeches [documented: Hodson and Archer]. The women wore short white socks with their pointe shoes, an oddity then [documented: same]. The colour scheme was white clothes on green and blue-black night, the same picture that had struck Nijinsky and Bakst in Bedford Square [argued]. Images: Valentine Gross's pastels in *Comoedia illustré* (15 May 1913), Charles Gerschel's photographs of the trio, and Duncan Grant's *Tennis Player* design (1913) [documented: Hodson 2008].

### Premiere and reception
Nijinsky (the young man), Tamara Karsavina and Ludmilla Schollar [documented: Debussy catalogue]. There was "neither controversy nor success" [documented: Théâtre des Champs-Élysées]. Émile Vuillermoz wrote that "Nijinsky sinned against Debussy. His translation of *Jeux* was a betrayal" (*Revue musicale*) [documented as quoted: France Musique 2019]. A *Times* critic in London wanted "to unbuckle their limbs for them and set them free" [documented as quoted: Hodson and Archer]. Debussy disliked the result, and the erotic ambiguity troubled him [documented: same]. Critics on both sides of the Channel avoided the sexual content [argued by Hodson and Archer; developed by Järvinen 2009]. Two weeks later *The Rite* eclipsed it. After Nijinsky's marriage and dismissal in late 1913, Diaghilev dropped it for good [documented].

### Versions and lineage
1. Nijinsky 1913: a few performances in Paris and London, then lost.
2. Jean Börlin for the Ballets Suédois, Paris 1920, in a décor by Pierre Bonnard [documented: Théâtre des Champs-Élysées].
3. Other new choreographies to the score in the twentieth century, among them John Taras for New York City Ballet (1966) [probable]. 
4. Hodson and Archer's reconstruction from Nijinsky's annotations on Debussy's piano score (Serge Lifar collection, Yale), drawings, photographs and memoirs: Verona Ballet 1996 (Carla Fracci), Royal Ballet 2000 (Deborah Bull, Bruce Sansom, Gillian Revie), Rome Opera 2001, University of North Carolina School of the Arts 2013 [documented: Hodson and Archer; New Yorker 2001].
5. Score in concert from 1 March 1914 (Concerts Colonne, Pierné); first recording Victor de Sabata, 1947; critical edition Boulez and Chimènes, 1988 [documented: Debussy catalogue; Wikipedia]. Boulez and others made the score a founding text of musical modernism [probable].

### Readings and disputes
- **A ballet of modern life, silenced.** Hanna Järvinen, "Critical Silence: The Unseemly Games of Love in *Jeux* (1913)", *Dance Research* 27/2 (2009), reads the contemporary reviews and Nijinsky's notations to argue that the work disconcerted its public by addressing modern life, and asks why failure removed it from the canon [documented: abstract].
- **A premonition of war.** Hodson and Archer read the stray ball at the end as aerial bombardment and the 2/4 march as a generation walking to its doom (Hodson, *Nijinsky's Bloomsbury Ballet*, 2008; DanceTabs 2013) [documented as their reading]. This is a retrospective reading; the 1913 publicity called it a "children's flirtation in a park after a game of tennis" [documented].
- **Whose desire?** The diary's "three young men" is read by some as a coded portrait of Diaghilev and his lovers; Pasler notes the trio as "a symbolic representation of Diaghilev and his two lovers, according to Nijinsky" [documented]. The diary was written in 1918 to 1919, as Nijinsky's illness began, so its testimony is late [argued].
- **Reconstruction or new work?** The New Yorker critic (Joan Acocella, 2001) found the reconstructed *Jeux* made one "newly appreciative of *The Rite*" [documented]. Any reconstruction from annotations and pictures fills gaps by invention (see Fullington on notation, Raymonda above) [argued].
- **"First" claims.** Hodson and Archer call it the first ballet with a scenario from the choreographer's own imagination and the first in modern dress [documented as their claim]. Use "earliest known", and note *Le Train bleu* (1924; W2 §8) and earlier music-hall sport acts as rivals for the second claim [argued].

### Facts worth telling
- Debussy's first answer was a telegram calling the subject "idiotic" [documented: Pasler citing Blanche].
- He took the job when the fee was doubled [documented: Wikipedia].
- In London the title was softened to *Playtime* [documented].
- The women wore white socks with pointe shoes [documented].
- Diaghilev vetoed Bakst's shorts for Nijinsky [documented].
- Nijinsky wanted an aeroplane crash in the plot [documented as diary claim].
- Nijinsky's handwritten notes on the score include the word "sin" at the trio's embrace [documented as Hodson and Archer's report].

### Set it beside
- `train-bleu-1924` (W2 §8): Nijinska's sport-and-beach ballet in Chanel clothes, eleven years later; the sister finishes what the brother began.
- `les-biches-1924` (this file): another house-party triangle of ambiguous desire, by Nijinska.
- `in-the-upper-room-1986`: sportswear and sneakers as costume, with dancers grouped as athletes.
- `les-patineurs-1937`: Ashton's sport ballet (skating) as pure entertainment, without the undercurrent.

### What a maker can take from it
- Look out of the window: a ballet could be about this year's sport and this year's clothes. Topicality was the radical move.
- A designer can be asked to project forward ("how will this look in 1920?"), which gives a world a slightly unreal, future-facing modernity.
- A work that fails can still be seminal: the score outlived the dance, and the reconstruction proved there was something to recover. Keep good documentation.

### Beyond the stage
The score is a standard of twentieth-century orchestral repertory [probable]. Duncan Grant's 1913 *Tennis Player* design links the ballet to Bloomsbury painting [documented]. William Plomer's poem on *Jeux* ("the crystal moment") [documented as cited: Hodson and Archer]. A film of the 2013 UNCSA reconstruction was announced [documented].

## El sombrero de tres picos / Le Tricorne / The Three-Cornered Hat (1919)
`id: tricorne-1919` · threads: T14, T05, T07, T18, T09 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 22 July 1919, Alhambra Theatre, Leicester Square, London, Ballets Russes; six performances in the last week of the season [documented: Beechey and Shone 2006]. Choreographer Léonide Massine. Composer Manuel de Falla, conducted at the premiere by Ernest Ansermet because Falla had been called to his dying mother in Granada [documented: Wikipedia; interlude.hk]. Scenario by Gregorio Martínez Sierra and María Lejárraga, after Pedro Antonio de Alarcón's novella *El sombrero de tres picos* (1874) [documented: Ballet Nacional de España; Spanish Ministry of Culture]. Front curtain, set and costumes Pablo Picasso, curtain painted with the scene painters Vladimir and Elizabeth Polunin [documented: Beechey and Shone]. One act in two scenes (often billed as two parts), about 35 minutes. The version most often seen: Massine's, revived by Massine for other companies and by his son Lorca Massine [probable].

### In one line
In an Andalusian village a miller and his wife outwit the lecherous local magistrate, whose three-cornered hat is the badge of his office: a folk comedy staged as a summit of Spanish modernism.

### How it came to be
In spring 1916 the Ballets Russes, stranded by the war, danced in neutral Spain under the protection of King Alfonso XIII, and Stravinsky introduced Diaghilev to Falla [documented: Wikipedia; Ballet Nacional de España]. Diaghilev wanted Falla's *Nights in the Gardens of Spain*; Falla refused and offered instead a pantomime he was writing with the Martínez Sierras [documented: OCNE 2017; Archivo Manuel de Falla PDF]. *El corregidor y la molinera* (The Magistrate and the Miller's Wife) opened as a mimed farce at Madrid's Teatro Eslava on 7 April 1917, for chamber orchestra under Joaquín Turina [documented: manueldefalla.com; Wikipedia gives 6 April]. That summer Falla, Diaghilev and Massine toured Spain, from Zaragoza to Granada, guided by a young Sevillian dancer, Félix Fernández García, whom Massine hired in November 1917 to teach him the *farruca* and other dances [documented: Wikipedia; Horton Cemetery research page]. Falla spent two years expanding the score for full orchestra, to Massine's structural requests [documented: Archivo Manuel de Falla; Ballet Nacional de España]. Picasso was asked only in spring 1919, by letter of 15 April, for a fee of ten thousand francs [documented: Beechey and Shone; Archivo Manuel de Falla]. London's Alhambra manager Oswald Stoll required four new works that season, which is why it premiered in London [documented: Beechey and Shone].

### Roots and reference points
- **Literature.** Alarcón's novella (1874) retells an Andalusian ballad, *El molinero de Arcos* [documented: Archivo Manuel de Falla]. The same story became Hugo Wolf's opera *Der Corregidor* (1896) [documented: OCNE].
- **Painting.** Picasso's curtain of the bullring is a homage to Goya's tapestry cartoons, and Karsavina's dress follows Alarcón's description of the miller's wife dressed "in the style of Goya's women" [documented: Beechey and Shone].
- **Folk dance.** Fandango, seguidillas, farruca and a final *jota* [documented: Beechey and Shone quote Diaghilev]. Falla quotes popular songs and has a singer offstage [probable].
- **Ballets Russes model.** It repeats the pre-war formula of *Petrushka* (1911; W2 §3), a national folk world made modern by a great composer and painter, now with Spain in place of Russia [argued; Danza Ballet makes the same point].
- **Politics.** The Falla archive notes the team gave Alarcón's text a social reading, "the triumph of the people over the decadent monarchy", but only as a pretext for folklore [documented as their view].

### The world it built
After an introduction with castanets, shouts of "olé" and a singer behind the curtain, scene one shows the miller and his wife at their mill, teasing a caged bird; the Corregidor passes with his wife and retinue, ogles the miller's wife, and is mocked by her fandango. Scene two, at night: the neighbours' party; the miller dances his farruca; the Corregidor's constables arrest him; the Corregidor returns to seduce the wife, falls into the millstream, hangs his wet clothes and climbs into the miller's bed; the miller, escaped, finds the clothes and swaps; at the end the villagers toss an effigy of the Corregidor in a blanket during the *jota* [probable: synopsis from Wikipedia and NGA]. Massine fused ballet with Spanish steps "adapted and somewhat simplified" [documented: Wikipedia]. The miller's farruca, a solo of stamped heelwork, snapping fingers and sudden falls to the knee, became Massine's signature [documented: Horton Cemetery page; probable for description].

### Design and art
The curtain, unveiled before the overture, shows spectators in a box at the bullring watching a dead or dying horse dragged out, in ochre, pale pink, white, blue and grey; Falla wrote new music so the audience could look at it [documented: interlude.hk]. The image occupied a small central part of a huge grey canvas, its outlines kept heavy so they could be read from the back of the house [documented: Beechey and Shone]. Picasso painted it flat on the floor of the Polunins' studio at 48 Floral Street, Covent Garden, with long-handled brushes, for over a fortnight, painting the women's eyelashes himself, and signed it "PICASSO PINXIT 1919" [documented: Beechey and Shone]. The set is a pale Andalusian village under a great arched bridge, an echo of the Pont du Diable at Céret, with simple Cubist distortions of perspective; under red-tinted light it looked to Polunin like a Japanese print [documented: same]. Against this pale ground the corps wore bristling bright costumes; the principals' were quieter [documented: same]. Karsavina's dress was "a supreme masterpiece of pink silk and black lace of the simplest shape; a symbol" [documented as quoted: Karsavina's memoirs, via Beechey and Shone]. Picasso wanted knee-breeches for Massine; Diaghilev insisted on tight long trousers for the farruca, so Massine wore breeches first and trousers for the finale [documented: same]. Picasso sat in rehearsal sketching, to make costumes "round" the moving dancer [documented: Karsavina interview]. The curtain hung in the Four Seasons Restaurant in the Seagram Building for 55 years and has been at the New-York Historical Society since 2015 [documented: NY Historical].

### Premiere and reception
Massine (the Miller), Tamara Karsavina (the Miller's Wife), Leon Woizikowsky (the Corregidor), Stanislas Idzikowski (the Dandy) [documented: Archivo Manuel de Falla PDF]. *The Times* reported a tremendous ovation from a full house; Massine was showered with flowers, wreaths and a large cake [documented as reported: Beechey and Shone]. The curtain drew near universal praise; Ernest Newman in *The Observer* was a lone dissenter, mocking "the gentleman with the bad squint in his calves" [documented as quoted: same]. John Richardson later called it Picasso's "supreme theatrical achievement" [documented as quoted: NY Historical].

### Versions and lineage
1. Falla, *El corregidor y la molinera*, Madrid 1917 (pantomime).
2. Massine 1919; revived by Colonel de Basil's Ballets Russes de Monte Carlo, Chicago 1934, in the original designs [documented: NGA Australia]; later stagings for many companies including the Joffrey (1969) [probable].
3. Antonio Ruiz Soler's version for his company (1958), later the basis of Ballet Nacional de España's stagings, which made it "triumph as choreography in the language of Spanish dance" [documented as BNE's claim; date probable].
4. Javier Latorre, *El loco*, Ballet Nacional de España 2004, revived 2022 to 2023: a ballet about Félix Fernández [documented: BNE].
5. Falla's two orchestral suites are concert staples [documented: interlude.hk].

### Readings and disputes
- **Who made the dances?** Massine is credited; Félix Fernández taught him the Spanish material. Before the premiere Félix broke down. The story told in Spain: he learned he would not dance the Miller, vanished, and was found dancing almost naked in the church of St Martin-in-the-Fields, then committed to Long Grove Asylum, Epsom, where he died on 18 March 1941 [documented as told: Ballet Nacional de España; death date from Long Grove register via Horton Cemetery page]. The Horton Cemetery researcher notes that researchers Estrada and Hernández question the church story, and that the contract and death records give different birth years (1893, 1896, 1897) [documented]. A Seville thesis places his admission in May 1919, nearly three months before the premiere, and cites Matisse's account of visiting him with Massine and Diaghilev in autumn 1920 [documented: idus.us.es thesis]. So the "breakdown on hearing he was not cast" may compress events [argued].
- **Who wrote the scenario?** It was signed by Gregorio Martínez Sierra; the critic Gonzalo Lahoz (OCNE 2017) and the Spanish Ministry of Culture credit María Lejárraga, who wrote much of what her husband signed [documented]. Give both names, as Ballet Nacional de España now does.
- **Spain by Russians.** Danza Ballet and BNE read it as a successful importation of Spanish idiom; BNE adds that Félix, a soloist improviser, was "less apt" for academic choreography [documented as BNE's view]. The opposing view treats it as one more outsider's Spain, staged in London by a Russian company (T05) [argued]. Unlike *Raymonda* or *Création du monde*, Spaniards were central on the team: Falla, Picasso, the Martínez Sierras, Félix [documented].
- **Dates.** Eslava premiere 6 or 7 April 1917 [contested].

### Facts worth telling
- Falla missed his own premiere; his mother was dying [documented].
- Falla added music so the audience would have time to look at Picasso's curtain [documented].
- Picasso painted the women's eyelashes on the curtain himself, reportedly with toothbrushes [documented as Massine's account, via Beechey and Shone].
- The Miller wore two costumes because painter and impresario disagreed [documented].
- Massine was given a large cake on stage on the first night [documented].
- The curtain hung for 55 years in a New York restaurant [documented].
- The Spanish dancer who taught the steps died in an English asylum in 1941 [documented].

### Set it beside
- `petrushka-1911` (W2 §3): the same folk-modernist recipe applied to Russia, by Russians.
- `raymonda-1898` (this file): national dance as decoration in an imperial house, against national dance taught by a native dancer on the team.
- `estancia-1952`: Argentine gaucho life by an Argentine composer (Ginastera), the national ballet from inside.
- `carmen-suite-1967`: Spain again, seen from Moscow and Havana (Alberto Alonso), the *españolada* reworked.

### What a maker can take from it
- Go and look: the 1917 journey through Spanish cities, with a local guide, gave the work its material. Field research is part of design.
- Credit the source person. The people who carry a tradition into a production disappear from the poster unless the maker names them.
- A curtain can be the overture: a static image shown before the action sets mood, period and stakes.

### Beyond the stage
The curtain's restaurant life made it a famous New York object [documented]. Falla's suites are used widely in film and television [probable]. Spanish film versions of *El sombrero de tres picos* exist [probable: Wikipedia section "Film versions"].

## La Boutique fantasque / The Magic Toyshop (1919)
`id: boutique-fantasque-1919` · threads: T04, T06, T14, T13 · confidence of the header: documented; scenario credit contested

**Header.** Premiere 5 June 1919, Alhambra Theatre, Leicester Square, London, Ballets Russes; Paris, 24 December 1919 [documented: NGA Australia; V&A; Wikipedia]. Choreographer Léonide Massine. Music Gioachino Rossini, piano pieces from *Péchés de vieillesse* (Sins of Old Age, 1857 to 1868), selected, arranged and orchestrated by Ottorino Respighi [documented]. Scenario credited variously to André Derain (Wikipedia, Philharmonie de Paris), or to Diaghilev, Massine, Serge Grigoriev and Derain (NGA) [contested]. Front curtain, set and costumes André Derain; sets painted by Vladimir and Elizabeth Polunin; costumes made by Alias Ltd [documented: V&A]. One act, about 40 minutes. The version most often seen: Massine's, as staged by him for Sadler's Wells Ballet (1947) and kept by the Royal Ballet companies (1968 touring company) [documented: Voices of British Ballet].

### In one line
In a toyshop of the 1860s two can-can dancer dolls, sold to different customers, elope overnight with the other toys' help, and the toys rout the angry buyers next morning.

### How it came to be
In 1917, waiting in Rome for Falla's *Tricorne* score (above), Diaghilev and Massine looked for a light ballet. They thought of modernising *Die Puppenfee* (The Fairy Doll), Josef Bayer's Viennese ballet of 1888, known in Russia from the Legat brothers' St Petersburg staging of 1903 [documented: Repertoire Explorer preface; NGA]. Respighi brought Diaghilev a set of Rossini's late piano pieces, and Diaghilev played them to Massine [documented as Massine's account: Wikipedia]. Bakst expected to design it, and made a set design (dated 1918 or 1924), but Diaghilev turned to Derain, a Fauve painter with no theatre experience: by one account because Bakst's sketches were "too pre-war", by another through a "misunderstanding" [contested: Repertoire Explorer; NGA]. Massine met Derain in Paris and they worked out the scenario with Derain's own marionette theatre at his home in the rue Bonaparte; they moved the period from 1832 to the 1860s [documented as Massine's account: Wikipedia]. The final battle between toys and humans was Derain's idea [documented as claim: Repertoire Explorer]. It became the company's earliest work made specifically for London [documented as claim: same].

### Roots and reference points
- **Earlier ballets.** *Die Puppenfee* (1888), and before it *Coppélia* (1870; W1 §6), the doll-maker's shop; and *Petrushka* (1911; W2 §3), whose puppets have inner lives. Beaumont compared Lopokova's rounded china-doll quality with the "angular, stiffly jointed puppet" of the Ballerina in *Petrushka* [documented as quoted: V&A].
- **Literature.** Andersen's *The Steadfast Tin Soldier* is often cited [documented as others' claim: Wikipedia].
- **Painting.** Toulouse-Lautrec shaped the period and Massine imagined a principal figure "quite Lautrec-like" [documented as Massine's account: Wikipedia]. The can-can is the dance of the Second Empire and of Offenbach's Paris.
- **Music.** Rossini wrote the pieces privately after retiring from opera; titles include jokes such as an "Asthmatic Study" and "Abortive Polka" [probable]. Respighi had just orchestrated *Fountains of Rome* (1916) [documented: Repertoire Explorer].

### The world it built
A shop opens; the Shopkeeper and his Assistant demonstrate their mechanical toys to customers: an English old maid, an American family, a Russian merchant's family. Each toy does a national or novelty turn: Tarantella dancers, a Mazurka of playing-card kings and queens, a Snob, a melon-seller, Cossacks, two dancing poodles, and finally the can-can dancers [documented: NGA synopsis; Philharmonie lists Overture, Tarantella, Mazurka, Cossack dance, Valse lente, Nocturne, Galop]. The Americans buy one can-can dancer, the Russians the other. At night the toys come alive and help the lovers escape. In the morning the boxes are empty, the customers riot, and the toys drive them out [documented: NGA; V&A]. Massine gave the toys staccato, jerky mechanical movement and made the humans mimed caricatures, so the toys are individuals and the humans types [documented: NGA; Repertoire Explorer]. The can-can pas de deux, with high kicks and splits, is the climax.

### Design and art
Derain's front curtain shows two figures with a guitar before a Mediterranean landscape and includes a three-cornered hat, a sly allusion to Picasso's ballet a few weeks later [documented: Beechey and Shone 2006 for the hat; description of the curtain probable]. His set is a bright shop interior opening on a harbour view [probable]. He supplied oil sketches with little technical detail, so the costumiers interpreted them; the poodles were made to look like real toys and survive like worn teddy bears [documented: NGA]. Lopokova as the can-can girl wore "a blue satin bodice and short, white satin skirt, fringed with black lace", curls of the Second Empire and a garland of cornflowers and marguerites [documented: Beaumont, quoted by V&A]. The Russian merchant's wife wore a painted silk shawl in pale green, turquoise, lemon, orange, pink and brown, fringed in blue, green and yellow [documented: V&A S.667-1996]. The Shop Assistant's jacket and checked trousers were made deliberately distressed [documented: V&A]. The look is soft, sunny and painterly, a modern painter's lightness applied to an old toy theme [argued].

### Premiere and reception
Lydia Lopokova and Massine (can-can dancers), Enrico Cecchetti (Shopkeeper), Nicolas Zverev [documented: Wikipedia]. Massine's biographer Vicente García-Márquez called the London reception perhaps "the greatest" outpouring of love for any theatrical event of the post-war years [documented as quoted: Repertoire Explorer]. Clive Bell recalled: "Never before or since have I beheld such a scene of white-tied, tail-coated enthusiasm" [documented as quoted: Beechey and Shone]. A critic wrote that old and new "all run together into a piece of merry nonsense" [documented as quoted: V&A]. Derain, frightened by the noise, had to be dragged on stage [documented as claim: Repertoire Explorer]. Picasso sketched Massine and Lopokova in their final pose [documented: Wikipedia]. The audience was war-weary London, and the ballet's lack of any deeper message suited it [argued by Repertoire Explorer].

### Versions and lineage
1. Massine 1919; in the Ballets Russes repertory to the company's last performance, Vichy, 4 August 1929 [documented: Wikipedia; V&A].
2. Colonel de Basil's Ballet Russe, Covent Garden 16 July 1934; first Massine ballet performed in Australia, Adelaide, 13 October 1936 [documented: V&A; Wikipedia].
3. A copyright dispute over the ballet when Massine left de Basil in 1937 [documented: Wikipedia]: an early test of who owns a choreography.
4. Sadler's Wells Ballet 1947, Massine dancing the can-can again; Alexandra Danilova as guest in 1948; Royal Ballet Touring Company 1968 [documented: Voices of British Ballet; Dancers Forum].
5. Respighi's concert suite, later also arranged by Malcolm Sargent [documented: Philharmonie].

### Readings and disputes
- **Pure escapism.** The V&A and García-Márquez read it as a balm for a public after the war: charm without message [documented].
- **A satire of buyers.** The toys are the individuals and the customers the puppets; the shop sells feelings to the rich and the toys refuse to be sold [argued, from the Repertoire Explorer observation that the humans are caricatures]. A design-course reading: it is a ballet about a product line and its consumers [argued].
- **National stereotype.** The customers (the English spinster, the American, the Russian merchant) and the toys' national dances turn nations into toys; the joke is kind but it is a catalogue of types (T14) [argued].
- **Authorship.** Derain alone (Wikipedia), a committee (NGA), or Massine with Derain's ending (Repertoire Explorer) [contested]. Why Bakst was dropped: "too pre-war" or a misunderstanding [contested]. The Bakst rejection marked the shift of Diaghilev's design from Russian painters to the School of Paris [probable: NGA calls it "a new connection" to contemporary painters].

### Facts worth telling
- The ballet used music Rossini never published [documented].
- Derain had never designed for the stage [documented: NGA].
- The scenario was worked out with Derain's toy marionette theatre [documented as Massine's account].
- The poodle costumes survive and look like old toys [documented: NGA].
- Cyril Beaumont sold small wooden figures of Lopokova as the can-can dancer in his Charing Cross Road shop [documented: V&A O63036].
- It was on the bill at the Ballets Russes' very last performance before Diaghilev died [documented].
- Lopokova later married the economist John Maynard Keynes [probable].

### Set it beside
- `coppelia-1870` (W1 §6): the toyshop as workshop; there a man makes a doll, here the dolls make their own escape.
- `nutcracker-1892` (W1 §9): toys alive at night, for a child; Boutique is for adults.
- `triadic-ballet-1922` (this file): the doll idea made abstract and geometric, three years later.
- `hard-nut-1991`: a toy ballet retold with irony about consumer America.

### What a maker can take from it
- A shop is a world-building device: each product is a character with a turn; the catalogue becomes the structure.
- Bring in someone from outside the form. A painter who had never designed for theatre gave the work its freshness.
- Light work can be strong work. Comic timing and character economy (Massine's Snob) are skills, not a lesser art.

### Beyond the stage
Respighi's suite is a concert and recording favourite [probable]. Beaumont's wooden figures show early ballet merchandise [documented: V&A]. The title "The Magic Toyshop" was later used by Angela Carter for her 1967 novel; any link to the ballet is not established [probable for the novel; link not found].

## Das Triadische Ballett / The Triadic Ballet (1922)
`id: triadic-ballet-1922` · threads: T09, T10, T04 · confidence of the header: documented; premiere date and authorship contested

**Header.** Complete premiere Saturday 30 September 1922, Kleines Haus of the Württembergisches Landestheater, Stuttgart [documented: Staatsgalerie Stuttgart; *Schwäbischer Merkur* notice of 29 September 1922, reproduced by Loderer 2022; the Schlemmer estate's site gives 22 September]. Danced by Albert Burger, Elsa Hötzel and Oskar Schlemmer, who appeared under the pseudonym "Walter Schoppe" [documented: Staatsgalerie]. Choreography and design Oskar Schlemmer, developed from 1912 with Burger and Hötzel and the craftsman Carl Schlemmer, his brother [documented: Schlemmer, *Die Bühne im Bauhaus*, 1925]. Music in 1922: a piano compilation of Tarenghi, Bossi, Debussy, Haydn, Mozart, Paradies, Galuppi and Handel; a prologue spoken by the actor Kurt Junker [documented: Loderer 2022]. New score in 1926 by Paul Hindemith for mechanical organ, Donaueschingen [documented: Frankfurter Personenlexikon; Deutschlandfunk 2022]. Three parts, twelve dances, eighteen costumes, three dancers (one woman, two men) [documented: Schlemmer 1925]. Length about 75 to 90 minutes in reconstruction [probable]. The version most often seen: Gerhard Bohner's reconstruction (Akademie der Künste, Berlin, 2 September 1977; music Hans-Joachim Hespos; costumes Ulrike Dietrich), revived 2014 by Ivan Liška and Colleen Scott for the Bayerisches Junior Ballett München [documented: Heinz-Bosl-Stiftung].

### In one line
Three dancers wear eighteen sculptural costumes of padded cloth, papier-mâché, metal and wire that turn the body into geometry and dictate how it can move: a ballet made from the costume outward.

### How it came to be
Two stories again. In Klaus Loderer's account the initiative came from Burger and Hötzel, dancers at the Stuttgart court theatre, who had met new dance theatre at Émile Jaques-Dalcroze's institute in Hellerau and wanted a modern ballet; they recruited Schlemmer, then a master student of the painter Adolf Hölzel [documented: Loderer 2022]. In the Schlemmer estate's account, the young Schlemmer, dreaming of being a dancer, wrote a "Dance Vision" in 1912 that became the ballet [documented: schlemmer.org]. They tried and failed to get Arnold Schoenberg to write the music. Three dances to music by Marco Enrico Bossi were shown at a Stuttgart charity evening in 1916 (1915 in Schlemmer's own chronology) [documented: Loderer; Schlemmer 1925]. The war interrupted. Schlemmer joined the Bauhaus in Weimar in 1921 to run the mural workshop, and some costumes were made in the Bauhaus stage workshop [documented: Loderer, quoting the 1922 review]. The Burgers paid for the costumes [documented: Loderer]. After the premiere, Schlemmer claimed to be the work's "spiritual father"; there was a legal dispute, settled in 1923, by which both sides might perform it and the costumes were divided [documented: Loderer; Frankfurter Personenlexikon].

### Roots and reference points
- **Art school.** Hölzel's colour and composition theory in Stuttgart; then the Bauhaus programme of unity of art and craft, the *Gesamtkunstwerk* [probable: schlemmer.org].
- **Geometry.** Schlemmer's own triads: form, colour, space; height, depth, width; sphere, cube, pyramid; red, blue, yellow; dance, costume, music [documented: Schlemmer diary, 5 July 1926]. "Three is an eminently important dominant number", where "the monomaniac self" and "the dualistic opposition" are overcome and "the collective begins" [documented, translated].
- **Puppet theatre and the marionette.** Heinrich von Kleist's essay "On the Marionette Theatre" (1810) and Edward Gordon Craig's "Über-marionette" stand behind the idea of the artificial figure [probable: standard reading; Schlemmer discusses the *Kunstfigur* in *Die Bühne im Bauhaus*].
- **Against.** Schlemmer meant it as an alternative both to classical ballet and to the story ballets of the Ballets Russes, and to the expressionist *Ausdruckstanz* then dominant in Germany [documented: schlemmer.org; Deutschlandfunk].
- **Dalcroze.** The Burgers' Hellerau training in rhythm and movement [documented: Loderer].

### The world it built
Three parts that rise "from jest to earnest": a cheerful burlesque before a lemon-yellow stage; a festive, solemn part on a pink stage; a mystical fantasy on a black stage that seems to recede to infinity [documented: Schlemmer 1925; Getty]. Twelve dances, solos, duets and trios, rotate the three dancers through eighteen figures with names such as Round Skirt, Diver, Golden Sphere, Wire Figure, Disc Dancer, Spiral, the Abstract and "Dancer, Turkish" [documented: Deutschlandfunk; Staatsgalerie; Loderer]. There is no story [documented: schlemmer.org]. The costume sets the movement: a hooped skirt allows only gliding turns; spherical hands bounce; the Spiral's dance is wide turning, which Schlemmer called "space-sounding, striding, sleepwalking" [documented: Staatsgalerie]. Schlemmer's own figure, the Abstract, has asymmetrically draped limbs that force weighty walks along straight lines, diagonals and curves [documented: Deutschlandfunk]. Schlemmer said the "seemingly violated body" reaches new forms of dance the more it grows together with the costume [documented, translated: Deutschlandfunk quote].

### Design and art
The costumes are the work. They are made partly of padded fabric and partly of rigid moulded forms (papier-mâché), painted in colour or metallic finishes, with steel sheet, plywood and wire [documented: Schlemmer 1925; Staatsgalerie]. Schlemmer called them "space sculptures": "coloured and metallic sculptures" carried through space by dancers [documented: Staatsgalerie, translated]. Colour is structural: one ground colour per part, with the figures in strong primaries, gold, silver and black against it [documented in principle; specific palettes probable]. The faces are masked or blanked, so no individual expression competes with form [probable]. Seven of the nine surviving original costumes have been in the Staatsgalerie Stuttgart since the 1970s [documented: Loderer; Heinz-Bosl-Stiftung]. Schlemmer's 1926 costume-design sheet with typed labels (Harvard Art Museums, Busch-Reisinger) is the best-known diagram of the whole cast [documented: Getty]. The Bohner reconstruction costumes are in the Akademie der Künste, Berlin [documented].

### Premiere and reception
The front stalls cost 230 marks, a sign of inflation [documented: Loderer, from the *Schwäbischer Merkur* notice]. The *Schwäbischer Merkur* review (2 October 1922) praised the Burgers' marionette skill and the costumes ("decidedly original and imaginative"; "But it is art!") but mocked Schlemmer's dancing, "of an almost touching unawareness of technical incapacity", and found the whole too long [documented: Loderer, translated]. The audience, "stirred up by the 'congregation'", applauded enthusiastically [documented, same]. At the Bauhaus Week in Weimar, 16 August 1923, Arthur Holitscher reported: "The theatre cheered, laughed and was merry" [documented as quoted: Deutschlandfunk, translated]. Further performances in Dresden (1923), Donaueschingen (1926), Frankfurt and Berlin [documented: schlemmer.org].

### Versions and lineage
1. 1916 (or 1915): three dances, Stuttgart charity evening.
2. 1922 Stuttgart; 1923 Weimar and Dresden.
3. 1926 Donaueschingen with Hindemith's "Music for small mechanical organ"; Schlemmer first planned three composers (Stuckenschmidt, Toch, Hindemith) [documented: Frankfurter Personenlexikon]. Hindemith's score is lost [probable].
4. Bauhaus stage, Dessau, to 1929: individual figures reused in Schlemmer's dance programmes [documented: schlemmer.org].
5. Gerhard Bohner, Akademie der Künste, Berlin, 1977, toured for twelve years; Bayerisches Junior Ballett München from 2014 [documented: Heinz-Bosl-Stiftung].
6. Museum life: the costumes as sculptures in Bauhaus exhibitions (1968 "50 Years of Bauhaus") and "Moved by Schlemmer", Staatsgalerie 2022 [documented].
Schlemmer, labelled "degenerate" by the Nazis, died in 1943; the work had no performing tradition between 1929 and 1977 [probable].

### Readings and disputes
- **Machine aesthetic, the dehumanised body.** Deutschlandfunk (Stöckmann 2022) presents it as a "new machine aesthetic" of cones, spheres and cubes (T09) [documented]. On this reading the body is subjected to the costume, as the worker to the machine [argued].
- **A humane geometry.** The Schlemmer estate stresses that the number three overcomes the ego and founds "the collective", and that the work moves from humour to the mystical; the figure is a new human type, not a robot [documented as their framing]. Schlemmer's own phrase about the "seemingly violated" body supports both readings at once.
- **Is it a ballet?** The register calls it a boundary case. The estate says Schlemmer used "ballet" while meaning a new form of dance opposed to classical ballet [documented]. The 1922 critic called it a costume dance [documented: "theatralischer Kostümtanz" in Frankfurter Personenlexikon].
- **Whose work?** Schlemmer, as posterity has it, or a collaboration initiated and paid for by Burger and Hötzel, who were the trained dancers [contested: Loderer 2022 against schlemmer.org]. The 1923 settlement shows that both sides had a claim [documented].
- **Dates.** 30 September 1922 (Staatsgalerie, Loderer, newspaper) against 22 September (schlemmer.org) [contested; the newspaper evidence favours 30 September]. 1915 against 1916 for the partial showing [contested].
- **Music.** Getty says the music was Hindemith's; that applies only to 1926 [documented error in a secondary source].

### Facts worth telling
- Schlemmer danced in it under a false name, Walter Schoppe [documented].
- A critic said he danced with "touching unawareness" of his lack of technique [documented].
- The dancers, not Schlemmer, paid for the costumes [documented: Loderer].
- The partners went to law over who owned it and split the costumes [documented].
- Hindemith wrote music for a mechanical organ, so no human musician would be needed [documented for the instrument; intention argued].
- The work was almost forgotten until 1977 [documented].
- The Spiral costume forces the dancer to turn [documented: Staatsgalerie].

### Set it beside
- `coppelia-1870` (W1 §6): the doll as a story about a doll; Schlemmer makes the dancer into the doll with no story.
- `parade-1917` (W2 §6): Picasso's Managers, cardboard constructions worn by dancers, five years earlier, as comic intrusions; Schlemmer makes the whole cast such figures.
- `in-the-middle-1987`: Forsythe's ballet bodies pushed to geometric extremes in plain practice clothes, the geometry now in the movement, not the costume.
- `relache-1924`: the Ballets Suédois' Dada evening, the same years' other answer to ballet.

### What a maker can take from it
- Design the constraint, and the behaviour follows: a costume that limits movement generates a movement language. This is a direct lesson in interaction design.
- A system can be written as numbers: three parts, three colours, three dancers, twelve dances, eighteen costumes. Rules like these make a world recognisable at once.
- Keep the collaboration contract clear. The quarrel over who owned the work nearly stopped it.

### Beyond the stage
The figures are among the most reproduced images of the Bauhaus, on posters, book covers and fashion (designers have cited them in runway collections) [probable]. David Bowie's 1980 *Saturday Night Live* costume, a stiff tuxedo-like shell, is often traced to Schlemmer [probable]. Museum display has made the costumes sculptures in their own right [documented: Staatsgalerie].

## La Création du monde / The Creation of the World (1923)
`id: creation-du-monde-1923` · threads: T05, T23, T09, T12, T13 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 25 October 1923, Théâtre des Champs-Élysées, Paris, Les Ballets Suédois (director and patron Rolf de Maré), on the same night as Börlin's *Within the Quota* to Cole Porter [documented: Routledge Encyclopedia of Modernism (Bellow 2016); Musée national Fernand Léger 2026; Ohio thesis 2007]. Choreographer Jean Börlin, who also danced. Composer Darius Milhaud, for an orchestra of 17 or 18 players with saxophone [probable]. Scenario Blaise Cendrars, from a creation story in his *Anthologie nègre* (1921). Curtain, set and costumes Fernand Léger [documented]. One act in several scenes, a little under twenty minutes; twelve performances in all [documented: Dorris 1999 via Ohio thesis; Cendrars/Léger statement quoted by Boulbès 2012]. The version most often seen: Millicent Hodson and Kenneth Archer's reconstruction (Geneva 2000), and Faustin Linyekula's *La Création du monde 1923–2012* for CCN-Ballet de Lorraine, which embeds that reconstruction [documented: artpress 2012; Fluxum].

### In one line
Three African creator gods raise plants, animals and the first man and woman out of chaos, in a Parisian ballet that built "Africa" from museum sculpture, a missionary's tales and Harlem jazz, without an African on the team.

### How it came to be
In 1921 Cendrars, a Swiss-born poet, and Léger, a painter of machines and cities, proposed a "ballet nègre" to de Maré and Börlin [documented: Bellow 2016]. Börlin had already danced *Sculpture nègre* (1920), a solo after African carvings then on view in the Musée d'Ethnographie and Paris galleries [documented: same]. Cendrars took the story from his *Anthologie nègre* (1921), a compilation of African tales drawn from missionary and ethnographic publications [documented: Bellow; Boulbès 2012; DanceTabs gives 1919, contested]. Milhaud had just returned from Rio de Janeiro and, in 1922, had heard jazz in Harlem; he wrote the score in the jazz idiom with a fugue [documented: DanceTabs 2012; probable for Harlem date]. Léger had designed *Skating Rink* (1922) for the same company, which was whistled [documented: Le Temps 2000]. He wrote to de Maré that he wanted to make "the only possible 'negro' ballet in the whole world", one that would "remain as a type of the genre" [documented as quoted: Musée Léger 2026]. Milhaud recalled that the four collaborators worked more closely than on any other of his works [documented as quoted: Ohio thesis, from *My Happy Life*].

### Roots and reference points
- **Myth.** The scenario purports to draw on Fang creation myths from French Congo (now Gabon and Equatorial Guinea): the gods Nzame, Mebere and N'kwa [documented: Bellow; Ohio thesis]. Its shape resembles Genesis, ending in a first couple [documented: Bellow].
- **Sculpture.** Léger worked from reproductions in Carl Einstein's *Negerplastik* (first published 1915, re-issued in the early 1920s), particularly Bamana (Mali) and Songo (Angola) figures [documented: artpress and Boulbès 2012; Le Temps says he relied "only" on Einstein's book]. Richard Brender writes that his figures are "a depiction of African masks" and "weird hybrids" assembled like synthetic Cubism [documented as quoted: Ohio thesis, citing Brender 1986].
- **Music.** Harlem jazz and blues, Brazilian music, and, Milhaud said, Jewish chant from his childhood for the solemn opening [documented: DanceTabs 2012].
- **Art movement.** The "negrophilia" of 1920s Paris, which Cocteau called the "crise nègre", from Picasso's *Demoiselles d'Avignon* (1907) to Josephine Baker's *Revue nègre* (1925), which de Maré also promoted [documented: Bellow; Le Temps; Boulbès].
- **Earlier ballets.** *The Rite of Spring* (1913; W2 §5) had shown an imagined primitive rite on the same stage ten years before [argued].

### The world it built
Scenes in sequence: a curtain; darkness and a mass of bodies; three giant deities circle on stilts to form the primordial mass; a tree grows and sprouts more trees and an elephant; monkeys, crabs, tortoises and birds burst out, each with a solo; the first man and woman emerge; a mating dance draws in all creatures; a final dance, then the couple alone [documented in outline: Ohio thesis, citing Brender]. Cendrars summed it up: "a slow explosion of forms from which a couple emerges", turning "by melancholy zigzags" to calm [documented as quoted: Boulbès]. Börlin used stilt-dancing and four-legged walking under the costumes [documented: Brender via Ohio thesis]. The dancers' bodies were almost hidden; the dance was the movement of painted shapes [argued; Linyekula's inversion confirms this reading]. Milhaud's score runs as a jazz fugue, a blues for the couple and a final cakewalk-like section [probable].

### Design and art
Léger designed the curtain, a set of flat painted cut-outs of mountains, clouds and gods in black, ochre, brown, white and blue, and the costume-sculptures for gods and animals [probable: from surviving maquettes; documented that he designed curtain, sets and costumes: Musée Léger]. The costumes were made of wood and cardboard; Hodson and Archer found them so heavy and awkward that their reconstruction used lighter modern materials [documented: Fluxum]. Milhaud recalled that Léger "was never satisfied that his sketches were terrifying enough", and wanted animal skins filled with gas to float up at the moment of creation [documented as quoted: Ohio thesis]. Léger made no claim to authenticity; he wanted "an evocative atmosphere" [documented as quoted: same]. The look is Léger's own: thick black contour, flat colour, mechanical joints, applied to wooden African figures he knew from photographs [argued]. The maquette and designs are in the Dansmuseet, Stockholm (de Maré's foundation) and the Musée national Fernand Léger, Biot, which mounted an exhibition on the ballet in 2026 [probable for Dansmuseet; documented for Biot].

### Premiere and reception
Börlin led; the original cast also included Ebon Strandin [probable]. A critic in *Les Nouvelles littéraires* wrote that the audience did not quite grasp it and that many heard "only noise, baroque, improvised and without expression" [documented as quoted: Le Temps 2000, translated]. It did better than *Skating Rink* [documented: same]. Milhaud's score won a life in the concert hall almost at once; in 1970 he named it one of his three most performed works [documented: Ohio thesis]. The Ballets Suédois made 24 ballets and nearly 3,000 performances between 1920 and 1925 [documented as the museum's figure: Musée Léger].

### Versions and lineage
1. Börlin 1923; the company dissolved in 1925 and Börlin died in 1930, so the ballet was lost [documented: Musée Léger dates].
2. Many new choreographies to Milhaud's score over the century, among them by Ninette de Valois (Camargo Society, 1931) and Kenneth MacMillan (Royal Ballet, 1964) [probable].
3. Hodson and Archer's reconstruction, Ballet du Grand Théâtre de Genève with Ensemble Contrechamps, December 2000, tied to the exhibition *La Création du Monde: Fernand Léger et l'art africain* at the Musée d'art et d'histoire, Geneva [documented: Le Temps; Boulbès].
4. Faustin Linyekula, *La Création du monde 1923–2012*, CCN-Ballet de Lorraine, 2012: the reconstruction framed by two new tableaux in which the bodies are visible and the décor stripped bare [documented: Boulbès 2012].
5. Hodson and Archer's duet version for the First Man and First Woman, Biot 2012; Athens 2018 with the National School of Contemporary Dance [documented: Fluxum].

### Readings and disputes
- **Primitivism as projection.** Juliet Bellow (Routledge Encyclopedia of Modernism, 2016) places it within European primitivism and 1920s "negrophilia", and links the creation theme to France's colonial project and national regeneration after the war [documented]. Richard Brender, "Reinventing Africa in Their Own Image: The Ballets Suédois' 'Ballet nègre'", *Dance Chronicle* 9 (1986), reads it as Europeans remaking Africa in their own image [documented: title and quotation via Ohio thesis].
- **A post-war utopia.** Hodson and Archer (DanceTabs 2012) read it as a collage of post-trench Paris, where "Africa" stood for a cultural alternative to a discredited West and the ballet's subtext is that "love and creativity are always under threat" [documented as their reading].
- **The answer from inside.** Linyekula, from Kisangani, asked how to make a ballet "after" rather than "from" *La Création*; his version sets the Western yearning for renewal against "a continent forced to bear the cost" of empire [documented: artpress; Fluxum]. The article "Afro-Futurism or Lament? Staging Africa(s) in Dance Today and in the 1920s", *Dance Research Journal*, 2017, reads Linyekula's piece as undoing the "cannibalistic" Eurocentrism of the original [documented: abstract; author not confirmed in this pass].
- **Contradictions.** *Anthologie nègre*: 1919 (DanceTabs) or 1921 (Bellow, Musée Léger) [contested; 1921 is the first edition date in most sources]. Which edition of *Negerplastik* Léger used (1915 or a 1920s re-issue) [contested].
- **Myth against evidence.** Milhaud's jazz is often called "African"; it is African American and Brazilian, learned in Harlem and Rio [documented: DanceTabs].

### Facts worth telling
- No African artist was on the team; the design came from photographs in a German book [documented: Le Temps].
- Léger wanted gas-filled animal skins to rise into the air [documented as Milhaud's memory].
- The gods walked on stilts [documented: Brender via Ohio thesis].
- The ballet had twelve performances, the score thousands [documented for twelve; argued for the rest].
- Milhaud's score is often cited as one of the earliest symphonic works to use jazz, a year before Gershwin's *Rhapsody in Blue* (1924) [probable].
- A Congolese choreographer remade it in 2012 by showing the bodies Léger hid [documented].

### Set it beside
- `rite-of-spring-1913` (W2 §5): the imagined pagan rite, Russian instead of African, same theatre.
- `rites-page-1997`: Stephen Page and Bangarra co-authoring a *Rite* with Aboriginal dancers: the T23 contrast of who is on the team.
- `raka-1967`: an African subject staged in South Africa under apartheid by a white team.
- `skating-rink-1922`: the same company and designer two years earlier on the modern city; Léger's two worlds, the machine and the "primitive".
- `common-grounds-acogny-2021`: Germaine Acogny's *Rite* line, African authorship of the modernist inheritance.

### What a maker can take from it
- Ask who is in the room. A world built from secondhand images of another culture will show its sources, and later makers will answer it.
- A remake can be an argument: Linyekula kept the old work inside the new one, so the audience sees both and judges.
- Costume as sculpture can bury the dancer. Decide whether you want the body visible or not, and know what that choice says.

### Beyond the stage
Milhaud's score is a concert and recording staple, and widely taught as an early jazz-classical fusion [documented: Ohio thesis]. Léger's designs appear in major exhibitions on primitivism and on Léger [documented: Geneva 2000; Biot 2026]. Popular-culture uses: not found in this pass.

## Les Biches / The Does, or The House Party (1924)
`id: les-biches-1924` · threads: T11, T22, T10, T19 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 6 January 1924, Théâtre de Monte-Carlo, Ballets Russes; Paris, Théâtre des Champs-Élysées, 26 May 1924; London as *The House Party* [documented: Garafola 2022; French Wikipedia; Argyrides 2025 gives London 25 May 1925]. Choreographer Bronislava Nijinska. Composer Francis Poulenc, "ballet avec chant" for orchestra and mixed chorus singing texts after seventeenth-century popular songs; conductor Édouard Flament; score dedicated to Josep Maria Sert [documented: French Wikipedia]. No scenario in the end (see below). Curtain, set and costumes Marie Laurencin [documented: LoC Nijinska Collection]. One act, nine numbers, about 27 minutes of music by Poulenc's count [documented: Garafola]. The version most often seen: Nijinska's own revival for the Royal Ballet, Covent Garden, 2 December 1964, in Laurencin's designs [documented: Voices of British Ballet; French Wikipedia].

### In one line
At a fashionable house party in a white salon with a blue sofa, a hostess, a dozen pink-clad girls, three athletes, two inseparable friends and an androgynous girl in blue flirt in code: a ballet of modern pleasure, not love.

### How it came to be
In May 1921, after hearing Poulenc's music for a Cocteau and Radiguet play, Diaghilev commissioned a ballet from the 22-year-old composer [documented: Garafola, citing Kochno]. The first plan had a libretto by Germaine Bongard, a fashion designer ("Mother Bongard", Poulenc called her), who also meant to design the costumes; she refused to let them use her libretto without her costumes, so the project lost both [documented: Garafola]. Diaghilev then "betrothed" Poulenc to the painter Marie Laurencin, and Poulenc decided the ballet would be "absolutely Marie Laurencin" [documented as quoted: same]. Nijinska, who arrived in Monte Carlo in autumn 1923 as the company's choreographer, argued for a plotless "pure dance" work, descended from the abstract pieces she had made in Kyiv [documented as Garafola's reading]. She began staging it at the end of October from Poulenc's only manuscript; he arrived in November and marked changes in red ink: four men became three, two female soloists became four, and the setting changed from a summer forest, as in an eighteenth-century *fête galante*, to Laurencin's salon [documented: same]. Poulenc counted "at least 72 rehearsals or close to 250 hours of work" [documented as quoted: same].

### Roots and reference points
- **Painting.** Poulenc imagined "those *fêtes galantes* where one could, as in certain paintings by Watteau, see nothing or imagine the worst" [documented as quoted: French Wikipedia, translated]. The title, "the does" (young female deer), was also period slang for young women [probable].
- **Ballet.** The opening loosely recalls *Les Sylphides* (1909, above), and Christopher Moore notes the Garçonne's blue tunic is "practically identical" to the Poet's costume in *Les Sylphides* [documented as Moore's claim, via Garafola]. The Andantino parodies the Petipa adagio [documented: Garafola].
- **Fiction and society.** Victor Margueritte's novel *La Garçonne* (1922), the scandalous story of a Paris flapper, gave the type its name [documented: Garafola note]. Poulenc said his women were "used to Rolls-Royces and pearl necklaces from Cartier" [documented as quoted].
- **Music hall and social dance.** The Hostess reminded critics of an American music-hall star; Nijinska's notes in the score call for a "shimmy of the legs on pointe" and a woman turning "like a mannequin modeling dresses" [documented: Garafola]. Poulenc compared one sofa pose to the drag trapeze artist Barbette [documented].
- **Proust.** Poulenc called the duet of the two friends "very secretly Proustian" [documented]; Argyrides (2025) sets the ballet beside *Sodom and Gomorrah* and *Mrs Dalloway* [documented].

### The world it built
Nine numbers: Overture; Rondeau (twelve women in salmon-pink flapper dresses slink on pointe); Chanson dansée (three Athletes in bathing costumes flex and show off, danced "weightily, like three cannon", Diaghilev said); Adagietto (the Garçonne, or Girl in Blue, a solo on pointe); Jeu (a game of hide-and-seek round the blue sofa, which the girls drag about and peer over); Rag-Mazurka (the Hostess with two Athletes); Andantino (pas de deux for the Garçonne and the lead Athlete); Petite chanson dansée (two Girls in Grey, "inseparable friends"); Finale [documented: French Wikipedia; Garafola]. The Hostess is a tour de force of men's petit allegro (brisés, entrechats) danced in heeled shoes, with a long cigarette holder [documented: Garafola]. Poulenc's chorus sings old song texts in the pit, which gives the action a mocking, knowing frame [argued]. Poulenc: "one does not love for life; one goes to bed!" [documented as quoted: Garafola].

### Design and art
Laurencin's palette is her painting's: soft pink, powder blue, grey and white, with black accents. The set is a white salon with a huge blue sofa; the curtain shows doe-eyed women and animals in her manner; its model is in the Musée de l'Orangerie, Paris (Walter-Guillaume collection) [documented: French Wikipedia for the curtain; probable for the description]. The Girl in Blue wore a fitted blue velvet tunic over briefs, white tights, white gloves and pointe shoes [documented: Garafola]. The Hostess wore a dropped-waist cocktail dress and long ropes of pearls (Chanel's, by Garafola's account) [documented]. Poulenc described an early Laurencin costume for the star: a short, low-cut dress of grey-white tulle with rose moiré panels and "a pancake hat with aigrettes" [documented as quoted]. Women's ostrich-feather headdresses for the Rondeau [probable]. Critics saw the salon as halfway between "the Casino and the brothel" (Claude Rostand) or "the lobby of a fashion house" (Robert Brussel) [documented as quoted: Garafola]. With Goncharova's *Les Noces* (1923), it is one of two Nijinska masterpieces designed by a woman painter [documented: Macaulay 2023].

### Premiere and reception
The first cast included Nijinska (Hostess), Vera Nemtchinova (Girl in Blue), Anatole Vilzak, Leon Woizikovsky and Nicolas Kremnev (Athletes), Lubov Tchernicheva and Lydia Sokolova (the two friends), with Alexandra Danilova, Felia Doubrovska, Ninette de Valois and Anton Dolin among the company [documented: Garafola; French Wikipedia citing *Comoedia*, 21 January 1924]. De Valois learned the Hostess role first so Nijinska could work on it on her [documented]. Poulenc called it a "triumph", with eight curtain calls, rare in Monte Carlo [documented as quoted]. André Levinson dissented: "a true spirit of perversity" moves the choreographer; "she dissects the classical steps with the will of a torturer" (*Comoedia*, 18 May 1924) [documented as quoted]. Sokolova found it "quite the most chic ballet ever invented" but thought the two friends' duet "wrongly conceived" [documented as quoted: Argyrides; Garafola].

### Versions and lineage
1. Nijinska 1924, Ballets Russes, then other companies [documented].
2. Royal Ballet, 2 December 1964, staged by Nijinska at Ashton's invitation, with Svetlana Beriosova (Hostess), Georgina Parkinson (Girl in Blue), David Blair, Keith Rosson and Robert Mead [documented: Voices of British Ballet].
3. Stagings by Nijinska's daughter Irina Nijinska, including Dance Theatre of Harlem (1980s) [documented: French Wikipedia picture caption "Dance Theatre of Harlem, 1989"].
4. Ballet de Lorraine under Pierre Lacotte for the 70th anniversary (1994) [documented: French Wikipedia].
5. Thierry Malandain's new version, 2002 [documented: same].
6. Poulenc's orchestral suite without chorus (1939 to 1940): Rondeau, Adagietto, Rag-Mazurka, Andantino, Finale [documented: same].

### Readings and disputes
- **Camp and queer code.** Christopher Moore ("Camp in Francis Poulenc's Early Ballets", *Musical Quarterly*, cited by Garafola) reads cross-dressing, androgyny and same-sex desire in the Adagietto, the Rag-Mazurka and the Petite chanson; he sees the Garçonne as a man in drag [documented as summarised by Garafola]. The LoC describes "gigolos, a hostess, and a pair of sapphists" and themes of narcissism and voyeurism [documented].
- **The choreographer's own selves.** Lynn Garafola (*La Nijinska: Choreographer of the Modern*, OUP 2022) warns against explaining it only by Poulenc's and Laurencin's sexuality, and reads the women as projections of Nijinska: the girl she had been (the Girls in Grey), the "masculine" woman she had become (the Hostess) and the ballerina she refused to be (the Garçonne) [documented]. Both readings see the ballet as about gender; they differ on whose.
- **A modernist text among texts.** Patty Argyrides, "'Secretly Proustian'", *Modern Fiction Studies* 71/3 (2025), argues for Nijinska and Woolf as women writing back to Proust [documented].
- **Credit errors.** French sources note that the frequent attribution of the scenario to Cocteau is wrong [documented: French Wikipedia citing Gullentops and Haine 2005]. The register's "none (sung texts)" is right, but Germaine Bongard's abandoned libretto should be recorded [documented].
- **Nijinska's own view.** Poulenc called her a "genius full of unconsciousness" and "an extraordinarily pure being"; Garafola calls that "odd", since Nijinska knew the company's sexual world intimately [documented]. The myth of the innocent woman choreographer against the evidence of a knowing one.

### Facts worth telling
- A fashion designer, Germaine Bongard, wrote the first libretto and kept it [documented].
- A blue sofa is one of the main characters [documented].
- The Hostess, Nijinska's own role, dances men's beaten steps in heels [documented].
- Ninette de Valois, later founder of the Royal Ballet, was the body on which the Hostess was made [documented].
- Poulenc laughed till he cried at rehearsals [documented as quoted].
- The original setting was a summer forest, changed to a salon in rehearsal [documented].
- Nijinska told a 1960s dancer the Girl in Blue was like "an envelope", folded over what it contained [documented as quoted].

### What a maker can take from it
- A world can be built from a palette and a piece of furniture. Laurencin's pinks and the blue sofa carry the setting, the class and the mood.
- Coding can be richer than statement: the work let different audiences see "nothing or the worst", which kept it alive.
- Pay attention to rehearsal-room changes: the salon, the cast numbers and the tone were all decided in the studio, not on paper.

### Beyond the stage
Laurencin's designs fed into 1920s fashion illustration and her own society portraits [probable]. Poulenc's suite is concert repertory [documented]. The ballet's flapper look was noted to resonate with 1960s London fashion when the Royal Ballet revived it [documented as claim: SeeingDance 2022].
