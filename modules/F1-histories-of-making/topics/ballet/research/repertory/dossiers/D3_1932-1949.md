# D3: Western Europe and America, 1932 to 1949

Repertory dossiers, pass D3. Written 21 November 2026 under `../BRIEF_REPERTORY.md`. Thirteen works, one dossier each. Works already taken apart in `../../works/W2_modernism.md` are not repeated here; where a work overlaps, this file adds what W2 does not say.

## Der grüne Tisch / The Green Table (1932)
`id: green-table-1932` · threads: T08, T17 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 3 July 1932, Théâtre des Champs-Élysées, Paris, by the Folkwang Tanzbühne of Essen, at the Concours international de chorégraphie held in memory of Jean Börlin by the Archives Internationales de la Danse [documented: Wikipedia, ABT, 92NY programme 2022]. Choreography and book: Kurt Jooss. Music: Fritz (F. A.) Cohen, for two pianos. Costumes: Hein Heckroth. Masks and lighting: credited today to Hermann Markard (see disputes). Subtitle "A Dance of Death in Eight Scenes"; about 30 to 38 minutes [documented: 92NY programme; Takano 1992 timed a 1983 performance at 37 min 32 s]. The version seen today is Jooss's own, staged from his Labanotation score by his daughter Anna Markard until 2010 and since then by Jeanette Vondersaar [documented: Wikipedia; 92NY].

### In one line
Masked diplomats argue around a green table, war breaks out, Death dances with everyone in turn, and the diplomats sit down to argue again.

### How it came to be
Kurt Jooss (1901–1979), a German dancer trained by the movement theorist Rudolf Laban, ran the dance department of the Folkwang School in Essen from 1927 and a small company, the Folkwang Tanzbühne, from 1928 [documented: Elswit, *Routledge Encyclopedia of Modernism*; Polska Biblioteka Muzyczna]. He said the work had matured over more than ten years [documented as Jooss's claim: Romaeuropa 1990 programme]; one account dates active work to 1929, when he became ballet master at the Essen opera [probable: *Dance Teacher*, 2011]. In 1932 the Swedish patron Rolf de Maré, former head of the Ballets Suédois, invited him to enter the earliest known choreography competition in Paris [documented: Romaeuropa]. Jooss chose to confront the politics of the day directly, at a time of reparations, depression and rising extremism in Germany [documented: Birmingham Royal Ballet, 2026]. The work won first prize and an invitation to the Casino de Paris that autumn [documented: EBSCO Research Starters, Balkus 2021]. Within a year it nearly cost him his company. After Hitler took power in January 1933, Nazi officials demanded the dismissal of Jooss's Jewish colleagues, above all the composer Cohen; in September 1933 Jooss heard he was to be arrested and took his dancers to the Netherlands that day [documented: Suzuki, Osaka Shoin bulletin, citing Stöckemann]. From 1934 the company, now Ballets Jooss, lived at Dartington Hall in Devon as guests of Leonard and Dorothy Elmhirst [documented: *Oper & Tanz* review of Stöckemann 2002].

### Roots and reference points
The frame is the medieval Dance of Death, in which a skeleton leads pope, king, merchant and child into the same dance. Jooss named the *Lübeck Dance of Death* (Lübecker Totentanz), a painted frieze in the Marienkirche [documented: Romaeuropa; *Dance Teacher*]. Critics compare each scene to one of the 41 woodcuts of Hans Holbein's *Dance of Death* (1538) [documented as a comparison: Numeridanse note]. The political root is the League of Nations and the failed disarmament talks of 1932 [probable]. The movement comes from two sources Germans then thought opposed: Laban's analysis of effort (strong or light, sudden or sustained) and ballet, which Jooss had studied in Paris; Mary Wigman's circle thought that mixture close to treason [documented: *Dance Teacher*]. The music borrows the tango of the cabaret for the diplomats, so that statesmen move like dance-hall partners [documented: Balkus].

### The world it built
Eight scenes in an A–B–A shape: The Gentlemen in Black; The Farewells; The Battle; The Refugees; The Partisan; The Brothel; The Aftermath; The Gentlemen in Black [documented: Graça 2014]. Each scene runs about four and a half minutes [documented: Takano 1992]. Ten diplomats in masks and white gloves bow, threaten and pull pistols; a shot declares war [documented: Graça; Wikipedia]. Between the frames, stock figures meet Death on the terms by which they lived: the Standard Bearer, the Young Soldier, the Old Mother, the Young Girl, the Woman (a partisan) and the Profiteer, who robs the dead and survives every scene [documented: 92NY cast list; Wikipedia]. Death moves with "mechanistic precision and implacable weight" [documented: Sarah Crompton, *The Observer*, 27 June 2026]. A Japanese analysis reads Death's arms as a scythe and his stamping legs as a clock, and finds one corner of the stage (upstage left) reserved as the place of death [argued: Takano Makiko, *Buyōgaku* 1992]. Two pianos play throughout; the sound is dry and percussive rather than orchestral [documented].

### Design and art
There is almost no scenery: black curtains, one green-covered table, hard lighting [documented: Polska Biblioteka Muzyczna on Jooss's practice]. Heckroth dressed the diplomats in black morning coats and spats, with grotesque bald masks and white gloves, so that their hands become the only living thing about them [documented: Balkus; Graça]. Death is a skeleton in Roman-style armour and helmet, a figure that combines the attributes of every war [documented: Numeridanse; Balkus]. The rest of the palette is "silvery greys, soft browns and subtle purple, with flashes of yellow and red" [documented: Crompton 2026]. The green of the title is the baize of a card table as much as a conference table [argued]. Heckroth (1901–1970) went into exile with Jooss, painted in a surrealist vein, and won the Oscar for colour art direction for Powell and Pressburger's *The Red Shoes* (1948) [documented: Kiefer and Ruby, *Giessener Universitätsblätter* 56, 2023].

### Premiere and reception
Jooss danced Death. The first cast included Karl Bergeest (Profiteer), Ernst Uthoff (Standard Bearer), Elsa Kahl (Woman) and Lisa Czobel (Young Girl) [documented: Wikipedia]. A Japanese account says the strongest rival was Oskar Schlemmer's Bauhaus group [probable: Suzuki, Osaka Shoin]. At the Casino de Paris in autumn 1932 the ballet followed a nude revue after the interval, and the uproar was so great that the music could not be heard [documented: Balkus]. In New York (31 October 1933) and London it made Jooss's name; the British tours gave audiences "a radical, communicative dance" beside the young Vic-Wells repertory [documented: Crompton 2026]. Prize money and box office: not found in this pass.

### Versions and lineage
Jooss restaged it in New York (1933) and Paris (1946) [documented: Wikipedia]. The Joffrey Ballet's revival (9 March 1967) put it into American ballet repertory; Jooss coached Christian Holder as Death in 1971, and Holder danced an impromptu memorial performance after Jooss died in 1979 [documented: *Dance Teacher*; Wikipedia]. Later stagings: Northern Dance Theatre 1973, Tokyo's Star Dancers Ballet 1977, Birmingham Royal Ballet 1992 and 2026, ABT 2005 (David Hallberg as Death), Paul Taylor Dance Company 2022 [documented: Wikipedia; Takano; BRB; ABT; 92NY]. It is one of four Jooss works that survive because they were notated [documented: Elswit]. A notated score with Ann Hutchinson Guest was published in 2003 [documented: Google Books listing].

### Readings and disputes
- *A timeless protest.* Suzanne Walther, *The Dance of Death: Kurt Jooss and the Weimar Years* (1994), reads it as the Weimar spirit made visible: "post-war trauma, political cynicism, social injustice, greed" [documented: quoted in Graça 2014]. The Numeridanse note (probably after Marcia B. Siegel) argues that it proposes no resolution: the conference brackets the war like stalemated parentheses [argued].
- *A political body.* Cristina Graça, "Latent tradition in modern dancing: the body politics of *The Green Table*" (Lisbon Summer School, 2014), argues that the universal setting hides a specific politics of gender and race carried in ballet and modern movement [argued].
- *Transnational survival.* Kate Elswit (Routledge Encyclopedia of Modernism, 2016) reads Jooss as a case of German dance kept alive abroad, mostly by ballet companies [documented].
- *Contradictions.* "Eight scenes" and "six scenes with prologue and epilogue" describe the same structure [documented: Balkus]. Wikipedia calls it the earliest work fully notated in Labanotation; we say "earliest known", and the claim is not checked here [contested]. BRB says Jooss founded Ballet Jooss in 1928 "after leaving the Folkwang School"; other sources say the 1928 company was the Folkwang Tanzbühne and the name Ballets Jooss dates from 1933 [contested]. Masks and lighting are credited to Hermann Markard, Anna Markard's husband, in current programmes; whether he designed the 1932 masks or recreated Heckroth's is not settled in sources seen [contested].

### Facts worth telling
- The choreographer danced Death himself at the premiere [documented: ABT].
- The only survivor of every scene is the Profiteer [documented: 92NY synopsis; Wikipedia].
- In Paris its first commercial run followed a nude revue, and the audience drowned out the pianos [documented: Balkus].
- A year after winning, Jooss fled Germany rather than sack his Jewish composer [documented: Suzuki, after Stöckemann].
- Its costume designer later won an Oscar for *The Red Shoes* [documented: Giessen 2023].
- Pina Bausch was Jooss's student at Folkwang [documented: Elswit].
- Each of its eight scenes lasts about four and a half minutes [documented: Takano 1992].

### Set it beside
- *flames-of-paris-1932*: the same year, the Soviet crowd as hero marching to victory; Jooss's crowd has no victory at all.
- *checkmate-1937*: also premiered at the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées; war as a game, with Death replaced by a seductive queen.
- *jeune-homme-et-la-mort-1946*: Death returns as a woman in a Paris garret, private rather than political.
- *sacre-bausch-1975*: Jooss's pupil turns dance theatre from political allegory to the body under pressure.

### What a maker can take from it
1. A frame can carry the argument: the same table opens and closes the work, so the audience feels that nothing has changed.
2. Stock types (the Profiteer, the Mother) let a world speak to any war; specificity can be left to the audience.
3. Notation and a family of stagers kept this work exact for ninety years. Decide early how your world will be handed on.

### Beyond the stage
Filmed in 1967 (director Peter Wright, Numeridanse collection) and broadcast on US public television in 1982 by the Joffrey [documented: Numeridanse; Takano]. Heckroth's later film design for *The Red Shoes* and *The Tales of Hoffmann* carries its painterly theatre into cinema [argued]. Popular-culture reuse: not found in this pass.

## Serenade / Serenade (1934)
`id: serenade-1934` · threads: T10, T02 · confidence of the header: documented (dates contested by one day, see below)

**Header.** First performed by students of the School of American Ballet on 10 June 1934, on an open-air stage at Woodlands, the estate of Felix M. Warburg at Hartsdale near White Plains, New York, in practice clothes; then by the school's Producing Company at the Avery Memorial Theater, Hartford, Connecticut, December 1934; professional premiere by the American Ballet, Adelphi Theater, New York, 1 March 1935 [documented: Balanchine Foundation catalogue no. 141]. Choreography: George Balanchine. Music: Tchaikovsky, Serenade for Strings in C, Op. 48 (1880), arranged in 1935 by George Antheil [documented: catalogue]. No scenario. Design: scenery "Gaston Longchamp" (a pseudonym of the painter Franklin Watkins), costumes Jean Lurçat (1935); Candido Portinari (1941); costumes by Barbara Karinska (1952); lighting Jean Rosenthal (1948), Ronald Bates (1964) [documented: catalogue; NYCB; Macaulay citing Simon Morrison]. One act, four movements, about 33 minutes, 26 to 28 dancers. The version seen today is New York City Ballet's, in Karinska's long blue tulle, licensed by the George Balanchine Trust.

### In one line
Seventeen women in blue light raise their hands against the moon: a ballet without a story that still feels like the memory of one.

### How it came to be
Balanchine (1904–1983), trained at the Imperial school in St Petersburg and lately ballet master for Diaghilev, reached New York in October 1933 at the invitation of Lincoln Kirstein, a young patron and writer. The School of American Ballet opened on 2 January 1934 [documented: Balanchine Foundation chronology]. Kirstein's diary records the first rehearsal on 14 March 1934 [documented: Macaulay 2017; Homans 2017]. Balanchine wanted to show students the difference between class and stage, and chose music they did not know [documented: EBSCO, Webb 2022]. "I made *Serenade* to show dancers how to be on a stage," he said later [documented: quoted in *Dance Magazine*, 2010]. He used whoever came: seventeen women the first evening, nine the next, then six, then a few men [documented: Homans; PNB]. When a student arrived late, and when another fell, he kept both in [documented: Balanchine Trust]. The first showing at the Warburgs' was cold and chaotic; Kirstein feared "a revolution" among hungry students, and one dancer, Gisella Caccialanza, tripped and fell [documented: Kirstein diary, in Macaulay]. Balanchine kept changing it for almost fifty years.

### Roots and reference points
- **Fokine's *Eros*** (St Petersburg, 1916) used the same three movements of the same score; Balanchine probably saw its 1922 revival. Both echo Canova's sculpture *Cupid and Psyche*, which Balanchine confirmed he quoted [documented: Macaulay, items 13–14, citing John Clifford].
- ***Les Sylphides*** (Fokine, 1909): the women in Romantic white, the "ballet of mood"; the 1940 Russian dance quotes it [documented: Macaulay; *Dance Magazine* quoting a critic].
- **The classroom itself**: the opening moves from parallel feet into turned-out first position, a lesson in becoming a ballet dancer [documented: Macaulay].
- **The Hitler salute** (contested, see disputes).

### The world it built
Four movements: Sonatina, Waltz, Russian Dance (Tema Russo) and Elegy. Balanchine reversed Tchaikovsky's last two, so the ballet ends in grief [documented: NYCB; Balanchine Trust]. Until 1940 there were only three movements; the Russian dance was added for the Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo that year [documented: Macaulay, from the 1940 Ann Barzel film]. The signature passages: the opening tableau of seventeen women, feet parallel, one arm lifted to shade the eyes; the latecomer threading through the lines to her place; the fall at the end of the Russian dance; and the Elegy, in which a man is led in, eyes covered, by a "Dark Angel", and the fallen woman is finally lifted high and carried off in a slow procession [documented: Homans; *Dance Magazine*]. In 1934 sixteen women were asked to do thirty-two fouettés in unison [documented: Annabelle Lyon and Kirstein, in Macaulay]. The ballet gives "unprecedented importance to the ensemble rather than to individuals" [documented: PNB]. The dance follows the music's structure closely but adds a drama the score does not state: Balanchine said he felt Tchaikovsky's help [documented: NYCB].

### Design and art
The costume history shows a ballet looking for its world. 1934: practice clothes. December 1934: pink latex with stiff black spirals, which tore, and soloists in "bath-toweling"; they were scrapped [documented: Kirstein diary in Macaulay]. 1935: Jean Lurçat, a French painter later famous for tapestry, designed tunics; surviving designs show both russet and two shades of blue [documented: Macaulay]. The 1940s films show short paneled dresses [documented: Macaulay]. In 1952 Karinska dressed the women in long pale-blue tulle skirts that recall the dead spirits of *Giselle*, and the men in blue with gold chains, the "Blueberry" costumes [documented: Homans; Macaulay]. There has been no décor since about 1941: a blue cyclorama and a diagonal of light from upstage right (the "moon") do the work [documented: NYCB credits; argued for the reading]. From around 1981 the three leading women let down their hair for the Elegy [documented: *Dance Magazine*; Homans says "at least from the 1970s"].

### Premiere and reception
The 1935 Adelphi cast included Leda Anchutina, Holly Howard, Elise Reiman, Kathryn Mullowny, Heidi Vosseler and Charles Laskey [documented: catalogue]. Kirstein wrote that the house "started the applause at the start" but the lights and costumes gave "no atmosphere of mystery" [documented: diary, in Macaulay]. John Martin of *The New York Times*, the most powerful American dance critic, praised only "the work" and said he disliked Tchaikovsky [documented: diary]. By 1944 he called it "this charming and characteristic little ballet" [documented: Martin, *NYT*, 15 April 1944]. Edwin Denby (1945) saw a classic style that "makes many of its points by gestures and arrangements" with "a sort of pictorial symbolism" [documented: in Macaulay]. In London in 1950 it was nearly the only NYCB work the critics liked [documented: Macaulay, citing Duberman]. Money: Edward Warburg was the school's first patron [documented: chronology].

### Versions and lineage
1935 American Ballet; 1940 Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo (Russian dance added); 1941 American Ballet Caravan (Portinari, South American tour); 1947 Paris Opéra; 1948 New York City Ballet; 1952 Karinska costumes; then dozens of stagings (Royal Danish 1957, La Scala 1960, Royal Ballet 1964) [documented: catalogue]. "The" version is the late NYCB one, but répétiteurs stage it as they danced it, so stagings differ in detail [documented: *Dance Magazine*]. Oldest film: Ann Barzel, Chicago 1940 (Newberry Library and NYPL) [documented].

### Readings and disputes
- *A prayer, not a story.* Jennifer Homans, "Hail Balanchine", *New York Review of Books* (21 Dec 2017), reads it as "more of a prayer or ritual than a theatrical spectacle", moving towards a transcendent realm [documented].
- *An inherited form.* Alastair Macaulay, "The genesis of Balanchine's *Serenade*" (2017), roots it in Fokine's *Eros* and *Les Sylphides*: a Petersburg memory rebuilt by Americans [argued from his evidence].
- *The gaze on women.* Ann Daly, "The Balanchine Woman: Of Hummingbirds and Channel Swimmers", *The Drama Review* (1987), argues that Balanchine's women are ideals made for a male viewer; the blind man led by the Dark Angel can be read either way [probable for the article; argued for the application].
- *The Hitler salute.* Ruthanna Boris remembered Balanchine demonstrating the Nazi salute at the first rehearsal and turning it into the opening gesture; another account says Eddie Warburg thought the stiff arms looked like it and Balanchine softened them [documented as two accounts: Macaulay]. Both say the gesture was changed; they differ on who saw the salute in it.
- *Dates.* 10 June 1934 (Balanchine Foundation, Trust, PNB) against 9 June (NYCB website); Hartford 8 December (catalogue) against 6 December (EBSCO); the Russian dance in 1940 (Barzel film) against 1941 (some sources) [contested]. The register's place, "White Plains", is the postal town; the estate was at Hartsdale.

### Facts worth telling
- The first rehearsal had seventeen women, and the ballet still opens with seventeen [documented: Homans].
- A fall and a late arrival in class are now fixed choreography, re-enacted every night [documented: Balanchine Trust].
- Kirstein misread his own handwriting: a "hymn to ward off the sun" became "sin" [documented: Homans].
- The 1934 costumes were pink latex and towelling, and were thrown out [documented: Kirstein diary].
- The scenery designer "Gaston Longchamp" was a pseudonym [documented: Simon Morrison, via Macaulay].
- Balanchine reversed the order of Tchaikovsky's movements [documented: NYCB].
- It is the most performed Balanchine ballet in the world [documented as a company claim: Kansas City Ballet].

### Set it beside
- *les-sylphides-1909*: the mood ballet in white it inherits; Fokine's poet becomes Balanchine's anonymous blind man.
- *giselle-1841*: the white corps of dead women, here without a plot to explain them.
- *symphony-in-c-1947*: Balanchine's other answer to "a ballet to a symphony", all daylight and order where *Serenade* is moonlight and loss.
- *les-presages-1933*: Massine's symphonic ballet of the year before, which gives Tchaikovsky's Fifth a programme of Fate; Balanchine refuses the programme.

### What a maker can take from it
1. Keep the accidents. A world built with real people can absorb what happens in the room, and those traces give it life.
2. A work can be revised for fifty years and still be "the same". Decide which images are fixed (the raised hand, the fall) and let the rest move.
3. Costume can carry a whole reading: Karinska's long tulle turned an exercise into a ghost story.

### Beyond the stage
NYCB treats it as a signature and teaching piece; the School of American Ballet's students still learn it [probable]. Film: Barzel 1940 and 1944; later broadcasts not checked in this pass. Fashion, products and popular music: not found in this pass.

## The Rake's Progress / The Rake's Progress (1935)
`id: rakes-progress-1935` · threads: T18, T15 · confidence of the header: documented (first Rake contested)

**Header.** Premiere 20 May 1935, Sadler's Wells Theatre, London, by the Vic-Wells Ballet [documented: ROH Collections work 769]. Choreography: Ninette de Valois. Music and scenario: Gavin Gordon (1901–1970) [documented: V&A S.1644-1982]. Sets and costumes: Rex Whistler "after William Hogarth" [documented: ROH Collections]. One act in six scenes, about 38 to 44 minutes (timed 7.03–7.41 pm in 1951, 7.31–8.15 pm in 2006) [documented: ROH stage-manager reports]. The version seen today is the Royal Ballet's (and Birmingham Royal Ballet's), in Whistler's designs as remade from 1942, with a false proscenium added by Oliver Messel at Covent Garden in 1946 as a tribute to Whistler [documented: ROH Collections].

### In one line
Eight satirical paintings of a young heir's ruin, turned back into a story that moves: the Rake rises, spends, gambles, goes to prison and dies mad, while the girl he betrayed keeps faith.

### How it came to be
Ninette de Valois (1898–2001), born Edris Stannus in Ireland, had danced for Diaghilev's Ballets Russes and in 1931 founded the Vic-Wells Ballet at Lilian Baylis's Sadler's Wells Theatre, the company that became the Royal Ballet [probable]. Her aim was a national ballet with English subjects and collaborators: *Job* (1931, after William Blake) and *The Haunted Ballroom* (1934) came before this [documented: Sarasota Ballet programme note]. The composer Gavin Gordon, who had written earlier ballets that failed, proposed Hogarth's series and wrote the scenario himself; he "persuaded" de Valois to choreograph it [documented: Wikipedia, citing Grove 1954]. She engaged Rex Whistler (1905–1944), a society muralist known for the Tate Gallery restaurant murals (1927) [documented: Buckinghamshire Gardens Trust]. The ballet was "an instant hit" [documented: Wikipedia]. In May 1940 the company was touring the Netherlands when the Germans invaded; it escaped, but the scenery and costumes were left behind and lost [documented: Salisbury Museum; Wikipedia]. Whistler redesigned the production in 1942 [documented: Salisbury Museum, act-drop design dated 1942; V&A costume dated 1942]. Two years later he was killed in Normandy as a tank officer [probable].

### Roots and reference points
The source is William Hogarth's *A Rake's Progress*, eight oil paintings of about 1733–35, engraved and sold by subscription from 1735; the paintings have been in Sir John Soane's Museum, London, since 1802 [documented: Tate Research Feature, Griffin, Macaro and Townsend 2021]. Hogarth called such series "modern moral subjects" [documented: Tate]. The ballet follows the images closely: the heir Tom Rakewell, the dancing master and other parasites, the orgy at the Rose Tavern, arrest for debt, the gaming house, the Fleet prison and Bedlam [documented: Voices of British Ballet; Dancers Forum synopsis]. The paintings themselves passed through the collection of William Beckford the Elder, whose fortune came from Jamaican plantations worked by enslaved people [documented: Tate]. Gordon's score imitates eighteenth-century dance forms (menuetto, rondo, loure, gigue, sarabande) and ends in a quodlibet, a jumble of tunes, for the madhouse [documented: Wikipedia]. It even uses a swanee whistle [documented: Wikipedia citing Baines]. W. H. Auden and Stravinsky's opera on the same series came later (1951) and borrowed far less from the pictures [documented: Wikipedia citing Eric Walter White].

### The world it built
Six scenes: The Reception; The Orgy; The Street (or "Virtuous Interlude"); The Gaming House; Near the Prison Gates; The Madhouse [documented: ROH cast sheets 1951 and 2006]. The method is mime and character dance set into ballet: the hangers-on (Tailor, Jockey, Bravo, Fencing Master, Hornblower) are caricatures; the Dancing Master's solo is a showpiece [documented: ROH; Voices of British Ballet]. Two claims from a secondary source: the Betrayed Girl is the only woman on pointe, which sets her apart as the one innocent; and the Rake's movement begins turned-out and classical, with lifted elbows and downturned palms for period, then loses its classical form as he degenerates, becoming "wild and uncontrolled like an animal" [probable: Dancers Forum, 2021]. In the last scene the madmen include a King, a Pope, a Violinist and a Gentleman with a Rope, all taken from Hogarth's Bedlam [documented: ROH cast sheets].

### Design and art
Whistler worked "after Hogarth": eighteenth-century London rooms and streets painted in a light, theatrical hand, with an act drop between scenes [documented: Salisbury Museum, "act drop design", pencil, sepia ink and wash heightened with white]. The Rake's first costume (1942 version) is a scarlet taffeta frock coat and knee breeches with gold braid, floral froggings and bullion tassels, a white shirt and jabot; it weighs 3 kg [documented: V&A S.1644&B-1982]. The look is a reproduction of a reproduction: a painter of the 1930s re-drawing an engraver's world for a stage [argued]. Messel's 1946 false proscenium framed the whole ballet as a picture, which fits a work made from pictures [argued].

### Premiere and reception
The Betrayed Girl was created by Alicia Markova, then the company's ballerina [documented: Voices of British Ballet; Wikipedia]. The Rake was created by Walter Gore, a character dancer from Marie Rambert's company [documented: Voices of British Ballet, Walter Gore page], and the Dancing Master by Harold Turner [probable: Dancers Forum]. Robert Helpmann later made the Rake his own and took it to Australia in 1956 [documented: Wikipedia]. A secondary account calls it "intensely English" and "not at all like the style of the Russian Ballet" [probable: Dancers Forum, echoing older criticism]. Named 1935 reviews: not found in this pass.

### Versions and lineage
1935 Vic-Wells; 1940 designs lost in Holland; 1942 redesigned by Whistler; 1946 Covent Garden with Messel's proscenium; regular Sadler's Wells Ballet revivals into the 1950s (Henry Legerton, Alexander Grant and Harold Turner as the Rake) [documented: ROH performance database]; Sadler's Wells Royal Ballet 1982 (David Morse, with David Bintley as the Man with a Rope) [documented: Wikipedia]; Royal Ballet 1998 for de Valois's hundredth birthday and 2006 (Johan Kobborg, Viacheslav Samodurov) [documented: ROH]. With *Checkmate* it is one of two de Valois ballets still in repertory [documented: Voices of British Ballet, Checkmate page]. Notation: not found in this pass.

### Readings and disputes
- *A national style.* Programme writers from the 1930s onward read it as the proof that English ballet could stand apart from the Russians: English painting, English music, English types [documented: Sarasota note; Dancers Forum]. Kathrine Sorley Walker, *Ninette de Valois: Idealist Without Illusions* (1987), is the standard life [probable].
- *Choreography from pictures.* Beth Genné, *The Making of a Choreographer: Ninette de Valois and "Bar aux Folies-Bergère"* (Studies in Dance History, 1996), argues that de Valois composed from paintings, treating the stage as a sequence of composed images [probable for the book; argued for this application].
- *The madhouse as spectacle* [argued, this file]: Hogarth's Bedlam shows fashionable women visiting the mad for amusement. A ballet audience watching the Rake's death stands where those visitors stood. Whether the ballet critiques that gaze or repeats it is open.
- *Who created the Rake?* Voices of British Ballet names Walter Gore; Wikipedia lists Robert Helpmann in the "original 1935 cast" [contested]. The weight of the dance-history sources seen favours Gore at the premiere and Helpmann soon after.
- *Paintings or engravings?* The register says "engravings, 1735"; ROH and the Tate say paintings. Both exist; the ballet's designer worked from the series as a whole [documented].

### Facts worth telling
- The composer, not the choreographer, had the idea and wrote the story [documented: Wikipedia citing Grove].
- The first designs were lost when the company fled the German invasion of the Netherlands in 1940 [documented: Salisbury Museum].
- The designer redrew the whole ballet in 1942 and was killed in action two years later [documented for 1942; probable for his death].
- The Rake's coat in the V&A weighs three kilograms [documented: V&A].
- The score uses a swanee whistle [documented: Wikipedia].
- Hogarth's paintings have hung in the Soane Museum since 1802 [documented: Tate].

### Set it beside
- *la-coronela-1940*: Mexico City also built a ballet from prints (Posada's engravings), for a revolutionary rather than a moral argument.
- *petrushka-1911* (W2 §3): a crowd of caricatures around one doomed figure, set at a fair instead of a city.
- *fall-river-legend-1948*: another descent to destruction told in mime and dance, with a woman at its centre.
- *checkmate-1937*: de Valois's next large work replaces the painted city with an abstract board.

### What a maker can take from it
1. A ready-made sequence of images can be a script. The task is to find the movement between the frames.
2. Give the one sympathetic figure a different physical language (here, perhaps, pointe shoes), and the audience will know whom to follow.
3. Keep your designs in more than one place: a war took this world's whole wardrobe.

### Beyond the stage
Gordon's music has been recorded by Constant Lambert and Barry Wordsworth [documented: Wikipedia]. The Stravinsky–Auden opera (1951) is a separate work on the same source. Soane Museum merchandise of 2019 reworked the Rake's faces as design objects [documented: Vogue, Suzy Menkes, 2019]. Film of the ballet: not found in this pass.

## Jardin aux lilas / Lilac Garden (1936)
`id: jardin-aux-lilas-1936` · threads: T24 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 26 January 1936, Mercury Theatre, Notting Hill, London, by Ballet Rambert [documented: Antony Tudor Ballet Trust; NYCB]. Choreography and libretto: Antony Tudor. Music: Ernest Chausson, *Poème* for violin and orchestra, Op. 25 (1896) [documented: NYCB]. Scenery and costumes: Hugh Stevenson [documented: Tudor Trust]. One act, about 18 to 20 minutes [probable]. US premiere: Ballet Theatre, Center Theatre, New York, 15 January 1940 [documented: Tudor Trust]. The version seen today is staged by the Tudor Trust's répétiteurs from Labanotation scores of 1967 and 1981 (Dance Notation Bureau), usually with sets after Stevenson or new ones (Tom Lingwood at Pittsburgh) [documented: Wikipedia; PBT].

### In one line
At a garden party on the eve of an arranged marriage, a woman tries to say goodbye to the man she loves, and cannot.

### How it came to be
Antony Tudor (1908–1987), born William Cook in London, the son of a butcher, came to ballet late and trained with Marie Rambert, a Polish-born teacher who had worked with Nijinsky on *The Rite of Spring* [probable]. Rambert ran the Ballet Club at the Mercury, a converted church hall with a very small stage [probable]. Tudor tried other music before he heard in Chausson's *Poème* the situation he wanted [documented: Wikipedia, citing Tudor literature]. He cast himself as The Man She Must Marry and his partner, Hugh Laing, as Her Lover; Maude Lloyd, a South African dancer, was Caroline; Peggy van Praagh was An Episode in His Past [documented: Tudor Trust]. Rambert later wrote that each situation was made clear "without any recourse to mime or gesture" [documented: quoted by the Tudor Trust]. The ballet established Tudor; in the next three years he made *Dark Elegies* (1937) and two more works in London [documented: EBSCO, Sim 2023]. In 1939 he left with Laing for New York and the new Ballet Theatre, where the work entered the repertory at its inaugural season [documented: Wikipedia; NYT 1985].

### Roots and reference points
- **Chausson's source**: the *Poème* was prompted by Ivan Turgenev's story *The Song of Triumphant Love* (1881), a tale of a love that will not stay buried inside a marriage [documented: Wikipedia].
- **Edwardian manners**: the ballet is set among the English upper classes of 1901–1910, where feeling had to be hidden under decorum [documented: Wikipedia; PBT note]. One recent article places it in the "Victorian era" [documented as a discrepancy: Macrothink 2024].
- **Cinema**: critics compare its freeze and its fleeting encounters to film close-ups, freeze-frames and slow motion [documented as a reading: EBSCO, Sim 2023].
- **Fokine and *Les Sylphides***: the moonlit garden and the women in long dresses belong to the Romantic picture Tudor quietly turns upside down [argued].
- **Tudor's own interest in Zen**: Maude Lloyd recalled that Tudor spoke of "Zen irony" [documented: Houseal 2020, from his 1994 interview].

### The world it built
One continuous scene, no numbers. Tudor chose music without clear beats or bar-lines and choreographed along its long phrases, moving "from psychological moment" to moment rather than from dance to dance [documented: Sim 2023]. Four principals, the only one with a name being Caroline [documented: Houseal]. Guests keep interrupting every private meeting, so the corps works as society itself [argued]. Gesture is small and everyday: fingers to a temple, a reach that stops, a head turning back [documented: Sim]. Caroline stands with arms straight to her sides and back stiff, "charged with the effort of self-control" [documented: Sim]. The turning point: as Caroline swoons into her fiancé's arms, all action stops while the music goes on; she alone moves, reaching toward her lover like a sleepwalker, then returns to the swoon and the party resumes [documented: Sim]. The ballet opens with Caroline's intake of breath and closes on her exhale, according to the stager Donald Mahler [documented: PBT note]. At the end Her Lover gives her lilacs at the last moment and is left alone [documented: Wikipedia].

### Design and art
A garden at dusk, hedged with lilac in full bloom; the women in long Edwardian dresses, the men in evening dress and uniform [documented: PBT; Wikipedia]. The colour is mauve and violet under moonlight, and the scent of lilac is implied, so that a smell becomes the ballet's atmosphere [argued]. Joseph Houseal compared it to the last line of the Heian-period poem *Iroha*, "all that remains is the fragrance of the colour" [documented: Houseal 2020]. Stevenson's 1936 design was small, made for a stage barely larger than a drawing room [probable]. Ballet Theatre redesigned it for larger stages; current American stagings use costumes by Raymond Sovey after Stevenson's sketches [documented: PBT credits].

### Premiere and reception
"Immediately recognized as a masterpiece" [documented: Sim 2023]. Later critics kept the view: *The New York Times* (1985) called it "a great dramatic ballet" [documented]. Mikhail Baryshnikov, as director of American Ballet Theatre, said "Tudor is our conscience" [documented: quoted in PBT note]. Takings and house size at the Mercury: not found in this pass.

### Versions and lineage
Ballet Rambert 1936; Ballet Theatre 1940 (Viola Essen as Caroline, Tudor and Laing in their roles, Karen Conrad as the Episode) [documented: Wikipedia]; New York City Ballet 1951 (Nora Kaye, Tanaquil Le Clercq, Laing, Tudor) [documented: NYCB]; later Paris Opéra Ballet, Royal Ballet, Pittsburgh (1987, 2013, 2016), Sarasota [documented: Wikipedia; PBT; Sarasota]. Labanotation 1967 and 1981 [documented: Wikipedia, DNB]. The Tudor Trust controls stagings [documented].

### Readings and disputes
- *The first psychological ballet.* The standard reading, held by most programme writers and by Judith Chazin-Bennahum, *The Ballets of Antony Tudor: Studies in Psyche and Satire* (1994) [probable for the book]: interior life shown through classical steps [documented as the common label: Wikipedia; Sim].
- *The first ballet about real people.* Maude Lloyd rejected the label: "*Giselle* is as psychological as anything"; what was new was "real people" and a story that "belonged to no one" [documented: Houseal, *Buddhistdoor Global*, 2020, from a 1994 interview].
- *A repressed love that cannot be named* [argued, this file]: Tudor and Laing were a couple, and a ballet in which lovers must hide behind society can be read as coded. This reading is not stated in the sources seen; Donna Perlmutter, *Shadowplay: The Life of Antony Tudor* (1991), is where it should be checked [probable for the book].
- *Contradictions.* The designer appears as Hugh and as "Hugo" Stevenson [documented: Tudor Trust; Wikipedia]. The Tudor Trust page gives the *Poème* as Op. 28; NYCB and Wikipedia give Op. 25, which is correct for Chausson's *Poème* [documented]. "Edwardian" against "Victorian" [documented].

### Facts worth telling
- Only one of the four principals has a name [documented: Houseal].
- The choreographer danced the man nobody wants [documented: Tudor Trust].
- The whole party freezes while Caroline reaches for her lover, then moves again as if nothing happened [documented: Sim].
- It begins with an in-breath and ends with an out-breath [documented: PBT, after Donald Mahler].
- It was made for a stage in a converted church hall in Notting Hill [probable].
- The last thing her lover gives her is a bunch of lilacs [documented: Wikipedia].

### Set it beside
- *giselle-1841*: a woman torn between two men, but Tudor needs no ghosts; the social world is the haunting.
- *pillar-of-fire-1942*: Tudor's next step, from suppressed feeling to a woman who acts on it.
- *romeo-juliet-lavrovsky-1940*: the arranged marriage as tragedy on the grand Soviet scale, against Tudor's twenty quiet minutes.
- *miss-julie-1950*: Cullberg's Swedish heroine breaks the class rules Caroline obeys.

### What a maker can take from it
1. Constraint can be the story. A world where people may not say what they feel makes every small gesture loud.
2. Stop time once. A single freeze at the right moment does more than any amount of spectacle.
3. Atmosphere can come from a sense the stage cannot deliver: the scent of lilac is in every viewer's head, not on the stage.

### Beyond the stage
Television and film recordings: ABT performances have been broadcast (not checked in this pass). Fashion, products and popular music: not found in this pass.

### Sources and open images
Scholarship: Judith Chazin-Bennahum, *The Ballets of Antony Tudor* (OUP, 1994) [probable]; Donna Perlmutter, *Shadowplay* (1991) [probable]; Joseph Houseal, "*Jardin aux Lilas*, the First Zen Ballet", *Buddhistdoor Global*, 6 March 2020 (https://www.buddhistdoor.net/features/jardin-aux-lilas-the-first-zen-ballet); Catherine Sim, EBSCO Research Starters (2023, https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/tudors-jardin-aux-lilas-premieres-london). Companies: https://www.antonytudor.org/archives/lilac-garden-jardin-aux-lilas; https://www.nycballet.com/discover/ballet-repertory/lilac-garden; https://pbt.org/the-company/artistic/repertoire/jardin-aux-lilas; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jardin_aux_lilas.

## Checkmate / Checkmate (1937)
`id: checkmate-1937` · threads: T11, T17 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 15 June 1937, Théâtre des Champs-Élysées, Paris, by the Vic-Wells Ballet, during the International Exposition, on the company's earliest known visit abroad, subsidised by the British Council [documented: ROH Collections work 514; Quinton, *Precarious Professionals*, UoL Press; Royal Ballet School timeline]. Choreography: Ninette de Valois. Music and scenario: Arthur Bliss, with advice from the theatre director W. Bridges-Adams [documented: Bliss, *As I Remember*, quoted by Hyperion]. Sets and costumes: E. McKnight Kauffer [documented: ROH]. Orchestre des Concerts Lamoureux conducted by Constant Lambert [documented: Chandos notes]. "Ballet in one scene with prologue," about 40 minutes [documented for the form: ROH; probable for length]. UK premiere 5 October 1937, Sadler's Wells [documented: Wikipedia]. The version seen today is Kauffer's 1947 revision, staged by the Royal Ballet and Birmingham Royal Ballet [documented: ROH production 6241].

### In one line
Love and Death play chess; on the board, the Black Queen seduces and kills the Red Knight and stabs the old Red King.

### How it came to be
Arthur Bliss (1891–1975), an English composer who had been dazzled by the Ballets Russes in London, kept a memory from a dinner given by the ballerina Tamara Karsavina in 1923: talk turned to "the drama of games", and someone imagined "the pitiless queen in chess" [documented: Bliss, "Death on Squares", quoted in Chandos notes]. When de Valois invited him to write a ballet in the mid-1930s, he proposed chess [documented: Chandos; Wikipedia]. Neither de Valois nor Kauffer played, so Bliss held sessions at his Hampstead home, moving pieces on a big board to show "the knight's jump, the bishop's diagonals, the queen's mobility, the king's tottering shuffle" [documented: Bliss, *As I Remember*, via Hyperion]. They debated who should play the game: giant shadows, Night and Day, Alpha and Omega; they chose Love and Death [documented: Bliss]. Bliss first wanted Love to set up the pieces again at the end, so that Death's win would not be final; that ending was dropped [documented: Bliss]. The Paris opening was nearly wrecked by a strike of the city's scene-shifters [documented: Bliss]. De Valois considered it her best ballet [documented: Royal Ballet School timeline].

### Roots and reference points
- **Chess** as an allegory of war and of courtly love, a tradition running from medieval moralised chess books to the painted chess games of Renaissance art [probable].
- **The Dance of Death**: the prologue shows "the skeletal hand of death" playing [documented: Voices of British Ballet]. Death as a chess player is a medieval image too [probable].
- **The news of 1937**: de Valois's choreography for the Black pieces drew on newsreels of goose-stepping troops [documented as the composer's notes' claim: Chandos]. The Royal Ballet School reads it as evil triumphing "in the face of weakness" during the rise of Fascism [documented].
- **English folk dance**: the knights' and pawns' dances draw on Morris and sword dancing [documented: Sarasota Ballet note].
- **Film**: the mechanical music for the Red Castles is borrowed from Bliss's own score for H. G. Wells's *Things to Come* (1936) [documented: MusicWeb 2001].

### The world it built
Twelve numbers: Prologue (The Players); Dances of the Red Pawns; Dance of the Four Knights; Entry of the Black Queen; The Red Knight's Mazurka; Ceremony of the Red Bishops; Entry of the Red Castles; Entry of the Red King and Queen; The Attack; The Duel; The Black Queen Dances; Finale, Checkmate [documented: Wikipedia, from the score]. The pieces enter one after another and "at one precise moment line up in the exact position for starting the game" [documented: Bliss]. Each piece moves as on the board: knights leap, bishops cross diagonally, castles move in straight mechanical lines, the King shuffles [documented: Bliss's instructions; argued that de Valois kept them]. The Black Queen tosses the Red Knight a rose; he could kill her in the duel but hesitates and is stabbed in the back [documented: Wikipedia; Sarasota]. The music is "diamond hard", often dissonant; the Bishops' ceremony has a tolling bell [documented: MusicWeb; Chandos]. Unusually for de Valois, the lead is a woman [documented: Sarasota].

### Design and art
E. McKnight Kauffer (1890–1954), an American who made his name in London as the star poster designer of the Underground from 1915, moved modernist painting (Cubism, Futurism, Vorticism, Surrealism) into public advertising [documented: London Transport Museum; Met Museum]. In 1937, the year of *Checkmate*, the Museum of Modern Art in New York gave him a solo poster exhibition [documented: Kauffer chronology]. Bliss liked "brilliant" colour: the opposing sets were clothed in red and gold and in black and silver [documented: Bliss]. The board is a raked chessboard in perspective; the costumes are geometric, armour-like, and the Black Queen wears a chequered costume [documented: Quinton, "a woman in a chequered leotard"]. A surviving gouache shows a white hand or glove holding fabric that turns from black to red, on a light-blue and black ground [documented: Met Museum 2021.54.24]. The look has been called "distinctive art deco designs by the leading commercial artist of the day" [documented: Sarasota]. The 1937 sets and costumes were abandoned in the Netherlands in 1940; Kauffer altered both set and costumes for the 1947 Covent Garden revival [documented: ROH production 6241].

### Premiere and reception
June Brae was the Black Queen; Harold Turner and William Chappell the Red Knights; Michael Somes a Black Knight; Frederick Ashton played Death; Robert Helpmann the old Red King; Pamela May the Red Queen; Margot Fonteyn led the Black Pawns [documented: Bliss; Wikipedia]. Front of house there was "considerable confusion" about protocol and bouquets [documented: Bliss]. The BBC broadcast the music on 15 October 1937 [documented: Wikipedia]. French press reaction: not found in this pass. The ballet "bolstered de Valois's position" at the head of British dance [documented: Quinton].

### Versions and lineage
1937 Paris and Sadler's Wells; 1940 designs lost in Holland; 1947 Covent Garden revival with Pamela May as Black Queen and Kauffer's revised designs [documented: ROH; RBS timeline]; regular Royal Ballet performances into the 1960s; revivals 1993 and 2007 (lighting John B. Read, staging Jacquie Hollander) [documented: ROH]; Royal Ballet School 2011 [documented: ROH]; Birmingham Royal Ballet, Sarasota Ballet [documented: Wikipedia; Sarasota]. Concert suite and a brass-band arrangement by Eric Ball (1978) [documented: Chandos]. Notation: not found in this pass.

### Readings and disputes
- *An allegory of appeasement.* The Royal Ballet School archive and the Chandos notes read it as a ballet of 1937 in which weakness invites aggression [documented]. This is a retrospective reading; Bliss's own account speaks of Love and Death and never of politics [documented: Bliss via Hyperion].
- *A woman's power.* Laura Quinton, "Ninette de Valois and the Transformation of Early-Twentieth-Century British Ballet" (in *Precarious Professionals*, University of London Press, open), opens with the Black Queen killing the King and reads de Valois as a woman exercising singular authority in a field where men were rising [documented]. Read beside it, the Black Queen is either a misogynist femme fatale or a portrait of female command [argued].
- *Who had the idea?* Bliss says the "pitiless queen" came from "someone's brain" at Karsavina's dinner, not his own; Wikipedia says the idea "was proposed by Bliss"; one review says de Valois's invitation came first [documented as three accounts]. The idea and the commission are separate events.
- *Who danced Death?* Bliss names Ashton; the register is silent [documented].

### Facts worth telling
- The choreographer and designer did not know how to play chess; the composer taught them [documented: Bliss].
- Frederick Ashton, the future Royal Ballet director, played Death at the premiere [documented: Bliss].
- The idea came from a dinner party given by Karsavina in 1923 [documented: Bliss].
- The Red Castles march to music recycled from a science-fiction film [documented: MusicWeb].
- The designer was London's most famous Underground poster artist [documented: LTM].
- The Paris premiere happened during a scene-shifters' strike [documented: Bliss].
- A first plan had Love calmly setting up the board again after Death's win [documented: Bliss].

### Set it beside
- *green-table-1932*: also premiered at the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées five years earlier; war seen as a diplomats' game, with Death dancing every part.
- *agon-1957* (W2 §11): another ballet as a contest under rules, but with no story and no costumes.
- *jeune-homme-et-la-mort-1946*: Death as a seductive woman again, now in a Paris attic.
- *sleeping-beauty-1890*: the court and its ranks; *Checkmate* makes the hierarchy a game board and kills the king.

### What a maker can take from it
1. A rule system you can borrow (chess) gives a world instant structure and legible movement: each piece's move is its character.
2. Ask the expert in the system to teach the team. Bliss's sessions at the chessboard are a model for briefing collaborators.
3. A designer from another field (advertising posters) can give a work a graphic clarity that theatre designers might not.

### Beyond the stage
The concert suite is in the British orchestral repertoire and on many recordings (Hyperion, Chandos) [documented]. Brass-band test piece 1978 [documented]. Kauffer's *Checkmate* designs are in the Met Museum and the Cooper Hewitt (Smithsonian) archive [documented]. Film and television: BBC pre-war ballet broadcasts included the Vic-Wells repertory, but a *Checkmate* broadcast was not confirmed in this pass.

Records: https://www.rohcollections.org.uk/work.aspx?genre=All&letter=C&page=1&row=14&work=514; https://rohcollections.org.uk/Production.aspx?production=6241; https://timeline.royalballetschool.org.uk/; https://voicesofbritishballet.com/podcast/celebrating-ninette-de-valois-checkmate/; https://www.hyperion-records.co.uk/dw.asp?dc=W1219_66436; https://www.chandos.net/translate/CHSA5344.

## Rodeo, or The Courting at Burnt Ranch / Rodeo (1942)
`id: rodeo-1942` · threads: T14, T11, T22 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 16 October 1942, Metropolitan Opera House (Broadway and 39th Street), New York, by the Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo, conductor Franz Allers [documented: agnesdemille.com; aaroncopland.com]. Choreography and scenario: Agnes de Mille. Music: Aaron Copland, commissioned by the Ballet Russe; ballet score about 24 minutes in five sections [documented: aaroncopland.com]. Set: Oliver Smith. Costumes: Kermit Love [documented: EBSCO Research Starters]. One act, about 32 minutes; cast of two female and two male leads, three "Kansas City Girls", five Womenfolk, eight Cowhands [documented: agnesdemille.com]. The version seen today is staged by the de Mille estate's répétiteurs (Ballet West 2022, American Ballet Theatre, others).

### In one line
A tomboy cowgirl tries to win the cowhands by out-riding them, fails, and finds love at the Saturday-night dance.

### How it came to be
Agnes de Mille (1905–1993), niece of the film director Cecil B. DeMille and daughter of the screenwriter William de Mille, danced against her father's wishes and spent the 1930s in London with Marie Rambert's company [documented: Library of Congress guide; NEH]. In 1938 she made riding and roping dances there on Rambert women, Peggy van Praagh, Charlotte Bidmead and Therese Langfield, who "performed everything the men did, but without the storyline" [documented: agnesdemille.com, citing Barbara Barker's 1995 interviews]. In 1942 Sergei Denham, director of the Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo, wanted an "American" ballet to compete with its rival, Ballet Theatre [documented: agnesdemille.com; Wikipedia]. De Mille "dug in my heels" for "the best American composer", Copland, whose *Billy the Kid* (1938) she admired [documented: aaroncopland.com, Vivian Perlis 1998]. At their first meeting Copland laughed at her scenario and she told him to "go straight to Hell"; he rang the next day [documented: LA Phil note, after de Mille's *Dance to the Piper*]. Copland suggested the 24-year-old designer Oliver Smith [documented: Wikipedia; EBSCO]. She rehearsed while the company was on tour, teaching European dancers to move like cowboys; the Head Wrangler, Casimir Kokitch, mocked her steps by miming a horse breaking wind [documented: NEH, quoting George Zoritch].

### Roots and reference points
- **De Mille's own childhood** in Los Angeles; the Cowgirl was "based on herself as a young woman" [documented: agnesdemille.com].
- **Earlier frontier ballets**: Catherine Littlefield's *Barn Dance* (1937) and Eugene Loring's *Billy the Kid* (1938) [documented: NEH].
- **Folk music**: Copland quotes "Sis Joe", "If He'd Be a Buckaroo", "I Ride an Old Paint", "Bonaparte's Retreat" in the Kentucky fiddler William Hamilton Stepp's version, "Miss McLeod's Reel" and "Gilderoy" [documented: LA Phil; Wikipedia]. Stepp's tune reached Copland through a Library of Congress field recording [probable].
- **Social and vernacular dance**: square dance, a "Texas minuet", a tap cadenza [documented: Wikipedia].
- **Film**: reviewers spoke of de Mille's "film sensibility" [documented: Wikipedia].

### The world it built
Five sections: Buckaroo Holiday; Corral Nocturne; Ranch House Party (ballet only); Saturday Night Waltz; Hoe-Down [documented: aaroncopland.com]. The men's signature is a riding step: legs bowed, bodies bouncing, invisible reins, lassos thrown and caught [documented: agnesdemille.com, "a unique style derived from horseback riding and cattle roping"]. De Mille wanted the "joy of effort" more than technical display [documented: NEH]. The Cowgirl is thrown by a bronco, runs alone "through the empty corrals intoxicated with space, her feet thudding in the stillness" [documented: de Mille's scenario, quoted in Wikipedia]. The Ranch House Party sets "Dance music inside. Night music outside": a honky-tonk piano and a lonely clarinet [documented: de Mille, in Wikipedia]. In the Hoe-Down she appears in a dress, the Roper claims her, and they out-dance everyone [documented]. Copland wrote with "economy of sound", solo instruments in place of whole sections [documented: Wikipedia]. De Mille heard in the score "enormous distance and space and strength and loneliness" [documented: NEH].

### Design and art
Oliver Smith (1918–1994), at the start of a career that would include *On the Town*, *My Fair Lady* and *West Side Story* [probable], painted the West as space: a huge sky over a low horizon of corral fences, then a ranch house interior [probable for description]. Kermit Love dressed the Cowgirl in trousers, boots and a Texas hat, and the women in long gingham-style frontier dresses; the famous change is her red dress for the Hoe-Down [probable: the dress colour varies by production]. De Mille chewed gum under her hat on the first night [documented: *Dance to the Piper*, quoted on agnesdemille.com]. Kermit Love later built Muppets, including Big Bird, for Jim Henson [probable].

### Premiere and reception
De Mille danced the Cowgirl; Frederic Franklin the Champion Roper; Kokitch the Head Wrangler; Milada Mladova the Rancher's Daughter [documented: agnesdemille.com]. De Mille wrote that her "hour struck at 9:40 pm, October 16, 1942" [documented: *Dance to the Piper*]. Curtain calls: de Mille claimed 22; other counts run from 17 to 23 [documented: NEH]. John Martin of *The New York Times* praised its wit and authenticity and called it a "little cow opera"; Edwin Denby called it "serious comedy" [documented: NEH]. Richard Rodgers and Oscar Hammerstein II saw it and hired de Mille for *Oklahoma!* (1943) [documented: agnesdemille.com; LoC].

### Versions and lineage
Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo 1942, with national tours; Ballet Theatre from 1950 [documented: NYPL ABT records]; Pina Bausch's Wuppertal company danced it in 1974 [documented: agnesdemille.com]; ABT, Joffrey, Ballet West (80th anniversary, November 2022) and many US companies [documented: agnesdemille.com]. Copland's *Four Dance Episodes* (suite, 1942–45) omits the Ranch House Party [documented: aaroncopland.com; LA Phil gives 1945 for the suite].

### Readings and disputes
- *The ending.* De Mille's own reading: the Cowgirl "acts like a boy, not to be a boy, but to be liked by the boys", and learns "a bitter lesson" when she puts on a dress [documented: agnesdemille.com]. Feminist critics read the same scene as a lesson in conformity: the woman who wanted the men's space is rewarded for giving it up [argued; see Kara Anne Gardner, *Agnes de Mille: Telling Stories in Broadway Dance*, OUP 2016, probable]. The ballet also lets her keep her riding: in many stagings she dances the final hoe-down with the Roper as an equal [argued].
- *America at war.* Elizabeth B. Crist, *Music for the Common Man: Aaron Copland during the Depression and War* (OUP, 2005), places Copland's Americana in the politics of the Popular Front and wartime unity [probable for the book]. NEH (2021) reads it as optimism that "assuaged the country's anxious mood" [documented].
- *Who wrote the ending?* De Mille first planned for the Cowgirl to go off with the Head Wrangler; Franklin threatened to leave unless she chose the Roper, and she agreed [documented: Jack Anderson, *NYT*, 12 Jan 2003, quoted on agnesdemille.com].
- *Who wrote the Ranch House Party?* Wikipedia says Leonard Bernstein wrote it for an overworked Copland; the Copland site lists it as Copland's [contested; not checked at the score].
- *A frontier without Native or Mexican Americans* [argued, this file]: the West of *Rodeo* is white ranchers only, as in most 1940s Americana.

### Facts worth telling
- The horse-riding steps now danced by men were first made on three women in London [documented: Barbara Barker interviews].
- The choreographer danced the lead herself at 37 [documented for her role; age calculated].
- She told the composer to go to hell at their first meeting [documented: de Mille].
- A dancer forced the change of ending [documented: Anderson 2003].
- "Hoe-Down" opens with a Kentucky fiddler's tune [documented: Wikipedia].
- Pina Bausch's company performed it in 1974 [documented: agnesdemille.com].
- It led straight to *Oklahoma!* and changed Broadway dance [documented].

### Set it beside
- *billy-the-kid-1938*: the same composer's earlier West, violent and male.
- *fancy-free-1944*: the other American "everyday" ballet of the war years, urban where *Rodeo* is rural.
- *giselle-1841*: the village girl who loves and is shut out; de Mille gives her a happy ending.
- *estancia-1952*: Ginastera's Argentine gauchos for Buenos Aires, the same cowboy material from another nation.

### What a maker can take from it
1. A national style can be built from ordinary bodies at work: riding, roping, square dancing.
2. Let the performers argue with the story. Franklin's objection improved the ending.
3. A work's meaning shifts with its audience: the same final dress reads as joy in 1942 and as a question now. Plan for the question.

### Beyond the stage
"Hoe-Down" became one of the most used pieces of American orchestral music: the US beef industry's long-running "Beef. It's What's for Dinner" campaign used it from 1992 [probable]; Emerson, Lake & Palmer recorded "Hoedown" (1972) [probable]. De Mille's riding style shaped the dream ballet of *Oklahoma!* and through it the Broadway musical [documented: LoC]. The 1942 Maurice Seymour photograph of de Mille and Franklin is in NYPL [documented].

## Pillar of Fire / Pillar of Fire (1942)
`id: pillar-of-fire-1942` · threads: T24, T18 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 8 April 1942, Metropolitan Opera House, New York, by Ballet Theatre (now American Ballet Theatre), in a spring season that opened on 6 April [documented: Antony Tudor Ballet Trust; NYPL ABT records]. Choreography and scenario: Antony Tudor. Music: Arnold Schoenberg, *Verklärte Nacht* (Transfigured Night), Op. 4, string sextet of 1899 in its version for string orchestra [documented for the work; probable for the orchestral version]. Scenery and costumes: Jo Mielziner; lighting: Jean Rosenthal [documented: ABT]. One act, 13 women and 8 men, average length 31 minutes [documented: Tudor Trust archive page; its ballet page says 18 minutes, which is wrong for the full score]. The version seen today is ABT's 2003 revival staged by Donald Mahler, with new scenery and costumes by Robert Perdziola [documented: ABT].

### In one line
Hagar, afraid of ending up like her spinster sister, gives herself to a man she does not love, is shunned by her town, and is rescued by the man she does.

### How it came to be
Tudor had moved to New York in 1939 to be a founding choreographer of Ballet Theatre, and *Pillar of Fire* was the earliest work he created in America [documented: Wikipedia]. He chose music written to a poem: Richard Dehmel's "Verklärte Nacht", in his collection *Weib und Welt* (1896), in which a woman walking at night tells her lover she carries another man's child, and he forgives her [documented: Rehearsal Studio 2020; Wikipedia]. Tudor had to turn a dialogue between two people into a world. He added two sisters, a seducer, a chorus of neighbours, and set it "in the period around 1900 because it was then that Schoenberg composed the music" [documented: Tudor Trust synopsis, after Chazin-Bennahum]. He cast Nora Kaye, a 21-year-old New Yorker who had danced in musicals and at Radio City Music Hall, as Hagar; Hugh Laing as the Young Man from the House Opposite; himself as the Friend; the company's co-director and patron Lucia Chase as the Eldest Sister; Annabelle Lyon as the Youngest Sister [documented: JWA; ABT]. Jerome Robbins, Sono Osato and Rosella Hightower were in the ensemble [documented: Tudor Trust]. Kaye became a ballerina "overnight" [documented: NYPL ABT records].

### Roots and reference points
- **Dehmel's poem and Schoenberg's sextet**: the music follows the poem's arc from darkness and confession to a transfigured night; Tudor's ballet follows the music [documented: Balanchine, *New Complete Stories of the Great Ballets*: "a story to a piece of music that was inspired by a story", quoted in Rehearsal Studio].
- **The title**: the pillar of fire that leads the Israelites by night in Exodus; Hagar is the name of Abraham's cast-out servant in Genesis [probable for Tudor's intent].
- **Freud and the case history**: critics from the 1940s called Tudor's ballets "psychological" [documented as the label]; the inner monologue and the crowd as conscience echo his own *Jardin aux lilas* [documented: Rehearsal Studio].
- **Modern dance**: Hagar's contractions have been compared to Martha Graham's [documented as a critic's comparison: ExploreDance 2004].

### The world it built
A small town of about 1900, two houses facing across a street [documented: synopsis]. Three sisters, three temperaments: the stern Eldest, the flirt Youngest, Hagar between [documented: ExploreDance]. Two ensembles: the respectable "Lovers-in-Innocence" and the licentious "Lovers-in-Experience" from the house opposite; plus "Maiden Ladies Out Walking" [documented: Rehearsal Studio; ExploreDance]. Only Hagar has a name [documented: Rehearsal Studio]. Signature passages: Hagar sitting rigid on the steps of her house at the opening; her violent pas de deux with the Young Man, who leaves her at once; her rejection by both groups; and the final duet with the Friend, in which they walk off through a grove [probable for staging details; documented for the plot]. Schoenberg's music has no regular dance beat; dancers had "to learn long-breathed phrases" rather than count [documented: Donna Perlmutter, *LA Times*, 31 March 2004].

### Design and art
Jo Mielziner (1901–1976), Broadway's leading scenic designer (later *A Streetcar Named Desire* and *Death of a Salesman*), designed a street of Victorian clapboard houses in night light, with the house opposite lit as a place of temptation [probable for the description]. Hagar wears a high-necked dress, her hair pulled back; the Youngest Sister is in lighter, younger colours [probable]. Jean Rosenthal, who would light Balanchine's *Serenade* in 1948, lit it [documented for credit: ABT]. The design is realist theatre rather than painted ballet: Broadway's language of interiors brought to the opera house [argued].

### Premiere and reception
*The New York Times* (John Martin) called Kaye's Hagar "superb" and wrote that she danced "so beautifully that it is impossible to conceive of anybody else doing it" [documented: JWA, citing *NYT* 9 April 1942]. A later critic called her performance one of "the great examples of tragic acting of its generation" [documented: JWA]. Tudor said: "there can be only one Nora Kaye" [documented: JWA]. The count of curtain calls on the first night is often given as about thirty [probable; not confirmed in this pass]. The ballet "played an important role in building the foundation" of the company [documented: Wikipedia].

### Versions and lineage
Ballet Theatre 1942; revival 1966 with Sallie Wilson or Veronika Mlakar as Hagar, "another triumph" [documented: NYPL ABT records]; PBS broadcast of ABT in 1973 [documented: Rehearsal Studio]; Royal Swedish Ballet, Stockholm, 30 December 1962 [documented: Tudor Trust]; Star Dancers Ballet, Tokyo, which holds its own costumes and scenery [documented: Tudor Trust]; ABT 2003 revival (Gillian Murphy, Marcelo Gomes), 2004 (Julie Kent, Angel Corella) [documented: ABT; ExploreDance]. Notation: Benesh by Elphine Allen (1969) and Agneta Stjernlöf (1971); Labanotation by Airi Hynninen (1982) [documented: Tudor Trust].

### Readings and disputes
- *A drama of repression and release.* Judith Chazin-Bennahum, *The Ballets of Antony Tudor: Studies in Psyche and Satire* (OUP, 1994), the source of the Trust's synopsis, treats it as Tudor's study of sexual fear and social judgement [documented as her synopsis; probable for the argument].
- *Is the ending a cheat?* The Dehmel poem forgives a pregnancy. Some versions of the plot make Hagar pregnant; Wikipedia notes that "in some versions Hagar becomes pregnant before her happy ending" [documented]. Read one way, the Friend's love redeems her; read another, a woman's sexual choice is still "fixed" by a man's acceptance [argued].
- *Is it American?* It was made in New York with American dancers but set in a Central European world of 1900; critics have read it both as Tudor bringing English reticence to America and as the start of American dramatic ballet [argued].
- *Contradictions.* Length 18 minutes against 31 on the same Trust website [documented]. The music's date: composed 1899; first performed 1902 (sextet) [probable]; Perlmutter writes "first performed in 1903" [documented as her claim]. The poem's book is *Weib und Welt*; Wikipedia's "Weib und die Welt" is a misquotation [documented].

### Facts worth telling
- Only the heroine has a name [documented: Rehearsal Studio].
- The company's director and chief patron, Lucia Chase, danced the spinster sister [documented: ABT].
- Jerome Robbins was in the ensemble two years before *Fancy Free* [documented: Tudor Trust].
- Nora Kaye later produced films including *The Turning Point* (1977) [documented: JWA].
- The dancers had to learn phrases, not counts [documented: Perlmutter].
- The work is notated in both Benesh and Laban systems [documented: Tudor Trust].

### Set it beside
- *jardin-aux-lilas-1936*: the same author six years earlier; Caroline obeys, Hagar acts.
- *giselle-1841*: a woman's sexual trust betrayed by a seducer, but Tudor gives her a living future instead of a ghostly one.
- *fall-river-legend-1948*: de Mille's New England spinster under the same small-town gaze, who ends at the gallows.
- *sacre-bausch-1975*: the community that watches and judges a woman's body, turned into ritual violence.

### What a maker can take from it
1. When you adapt, enlarge the cast to give the source's inner voice bodies: Tudor turned one woman's confession into a town.
2. Two choruses can stand for two moral worlds; the heroine's choice is where she stands between them.
3. A performer can make the work: write for a specific body and temperament.

### Beyond the stage
PBS broadcast (1973) survives on YouTube in poor copies [documented: Rehearsal Studio]. Schoenberg's score is a concert staple; Kaye's later film work (*The Turning Point*) carried the ABT world to Hollywood [documented: JWA]. Fashion and products: not found in this pass.

### Sources and open images
Scholarship: Judith Chazin-Bennahum (1994) [probable]; Donna Perlmutter, *Shadowplay* (1991) [probable] and "True to Tudor?", *LA Times*, 31 March 2004 (https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-2004-mar-31-et-perlmutter31-story.html); Walter Terry, *Ballet Guide* (1976) [documented as cited by Wikipedia]. Web: https://www.antonytudor.org/archives/pillar-of-fire; https://www.abt.org/ballet/pillar-of-fire; https://jwa.org/thisweek/apr/08/1942/nora-kaye; https://therehearsalstudio.blogspot.com/2020/05/frustrations-encountered-in-pillar-of.html; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pillar_of_Fire_(ballet); NYPL ABT records finding aid (https://nyplorg-data-archives.s3.amazonaws.com/uploads/collection/pdf_finding_aid/dan_abt.pdf), whose Pillar of Fire file is among the most extensive.

## Fancy Free / Fancy Free (1944)
`id: fancy-free-1944` · threads: T11, T14 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 18 April 1944, Metropolitan Opera House, New York, by Ballet Theatre; the Ballet Theatre Orchestra conducted by Leonard Bernstein [documented: leonardbernstein.com; ABT]. Choreography and scenario: Jerome Robbins. Music: Leonard Bernstein, about 27 minutes, opening with his song "Big Stuff" on a jukebox [documented: leonardbernstein.com]. Set: Oliver Smith; costumes: Kermit Love; lighting originally Nananne Porcher (ABT) or Ronald Bates (NYCB credits) [documented]. One act, seven dancers: three sailors, three women ("passersby"), a bartender [documented: ABT]. About 20 to 29 minutes depending on company [documented: PNB 20; NYCB 29]. The version seen today is Robbins's own, licensed by the Robbins Rights Trust; only his choreography may be used to the score [documented: leonardbernstein.com].

### In one line
Three sailors on 24-hour shore leave in wartime Manhattan meet two girls in a bar, show off to win them, fight, lose them, and run after a third.

### How it came to be
Jerome Robbins (1918–1998), born Rabinowitz in New York, was a dancer in Ballet Theatre who had worked under Fokine, de Mille and Tudor and danced in *Pillar of Fire* [documented: NEH, Pia Catton 2009]. His early ballet ideas were too grand for a poor company, and he was told to "think small" [documented: Jerome Robbins Foundation]. The painting *The Fleet's In!* (1934) by Paul Cadmus gave him the idea of sailors on leave [documented: leonardbernstein.com; US Navy Heritage Command]. No composer took it on until someone suggested Bernstein; at their first meeting in 1943, both about 25, Bernstein hummed a tune he had written that morning and Robbins loved it [documented: PBT audience guide 2018]. A commission from Ballet Theatre followed in December 1943 [documented: leonardbernstein.com]. Robbins choreographed on tour, in hotel lobbies and on buses; Bernstein mailed him home-made records of the score [documented: PBT guide]. Bernstein's sudden fame after conducting the New York Philharmonic for Bruno Walter in November 1943 helped: the impresario Sol Hurok bought the ballet [documented: EBSCO].

### Roots and reference points
- **Paul Cadmus, *The Fleet's In!*** (1934, tempera, Navy Art Collection): drunken sailors with women, and in the middle a man in a red tie offering a marine a cigarette, a coded gay pickup [documented: Met Museum Perspectives 2021; Philadelphia Museum]. The Navy removed it from the Corcoran Gallery before the show opened, after a retired admiral called it "a most disgraceful, sordid, disreputable, drunken brawl" [documented: Met; Navy Heritage Command].
- **Wartime New York**: the streets were full of servicemen on leave [documented: NEH].
- **Jazz and social dance**: the jitterbug, the lindy, boogie-woogie, the blues of Billie Holiday ("Big Stuff" was written partly in homage to her) [documented: EBSCO].
- **Ballet tradition**: the three male variations are a competition, like the suitors' dances in classical ballet [argued].

### The world it built
A street corner, a bar, a lamppost, the skyline at night [documented: Bernstein's own description, 1946]. The music starts with a jukebox "wailing behind the curtain", then "four brisk big-band drum taps" as the sailors "explode onto the stage" [documented: Bernstein; EBSCO]. Sequence: the sailors' entrance; a gum-chewing game of who pays for the beer; the first girl, whose bag they grab and toss; a second girl; a pas de deux; a fight over two girls between three men; a competition of three solos, made to suit each dancer: a galloping show-off (Harold Lang), a dreamy, clumsy romantic (John Kriza), and a danzón with Latin hips (Robbins) [documented for the solos fitting the dancers: NEH; probable for the characterisation]. The fight resumes, the girls leave, the men make up, and a third girl walks by [documented: PNB synopsis]. The movement mixes ballet with vernacular swagger: hitching trousers, leaning on the bar, slapping hands [documented as "modern, free-style, and jive", Bernstein blog].

### Design and art
Oliver Smith (1918–1994), then 25, made a set so ordinary that it shocked the opera-house audience: instead of Egyptian monuments, a bar such as they might have walked into outside the Met [documented: Robert Kapilow, NEA podcast 2022]. The skyscrapers behind are "pricked out with the crazy pattern of lights, making a dizzying backdrop" [documented: Bernstein 1946]. The bar is a tilted, open structure that leaves the dancing space clear [probable]. Kermit Love dressed the men in regulation US Navy whites and the women in 1940s street dresses, heels and handbags [probable]. Carol Oja argues that the set "positions the innocence and vulnerability of the soldiers against the alluring city" [documented: quoted on leonardbernstein.com].

### Premiere and reception
Cast: Robbins, Lang and Kriza as sailors; Muriel Bentley, Janet Reed and Shirley Eckl as the women; Rex Cooper as the bartender [documented: ABT]. "Two dozen curtain calls" [documented: Robbins Foundation; Bernstein site], also given as 22 [documented: PBT guide; Kapilow]. The Met run was extended two weeks and a summer tour added, which gave Bernstein his Hollywood Bowl debut [documented: Bernstein blog]. The ballet was danced more than 160 times in its first year [documented: EBSCO]. John Martin, *New York Times*: "a rare little genre masterpiece—young, human, tender and funny" [documented: Bernstein blog]. Edwin Denby: "a direct, manly piece" [documented: quoted in *Dance Magazine* 2020].

### Versions and lineage
Ballet Theatre 1944 to now (revived with new casts, e.g. Eliot Feld in 1965) [documented: NYPL ABT records]; New York City Ballet from 31 January 1980 (Peter Martins, Bart Cook, Jean-Pierre Frohlich) [documented: NYCB]; Pacific Northwest Ballet 2006; Paris Opéra, San Francisco Ballet; 96 performances in the 2018 Bernstein and Robbins centennials [documented: leonardbernstein.com]. A reduced orchestration was made in 2019 [documented]. The adaptation *On the Town* (Broadway, December 1944; book and lyrics Comden and Green, dances Robbins, sets and production Smith) kept the idea but not "a single note" of the score [documented: Kapilow; Robbins Foundation].

### Readings and disputes
- *Joy in wartime.* Carol J. Oja, *Bernstein Meets Broadway: Collaborative Art in a Time of War* (OUP, 2014), reads it as "about twentysomethings in a time of war, about transience, risk taking" and popular culture [documented: quoted on leonardbernstein.com].
- *A queer undertext.* Julia L. Foulkes (The New School, 2018 exhibition; 2025 lecture) argues that Robbins's and Bernstein's own homosexual lives "underlay" a ballet that played up heterosexual desire but "reinforced male camaraderie"; the Cadmus source supports her [documented: Foulkes PDF].
- *Harassment as comedy.* A 2020 *Dance Magazine* op-ed, by a Virginia Commonwealth University dance teacher, reports students "horrified": "He actually choreographed the women's fear"; the false contrition at the end "drives home the 'boys will be boys' lesson" [documented]. Defenders argue the ballet shows what it shows, and that the women's wariness is part of Robbins's truth [argued].
- *Contradictions.* Curtain calls 22 against "two dozen"; lighting credited to Porcher (ABT) or Bates (NYCB, PNB); the first meeting dated summer 1943 (PBT) or fall 1943 (Bernstein site) [documented as discrepancies].

### Facts worth telling
- It begins with a record on a jukebox, not an orchestra [documented: Kapilow].
- Its inspiration was a painting the US Navy confiscated [documented: Navy Heritage Command].
- The choreographer, composer and designer were all about 25 [documented: NEA podcast].
- It was danced over 160 times in its first year [documented: EBSCO].
- *On the Town* reused the story but none of the music [documented: Kapilow].
- The censored painting hung for decades in an all-male club in Washington [documented: Navy Heritage Command].

### Set it beside
- *rodeo-1942*: the same designer and costume-maker two years earlier; rural West against urban East.
- *train-bleu-1924* (W2 §8): sport and leisure clothes on the stage of the 1920s Riviera, made by a poet's idea; Robbins's is a dancer's.
- *giselle-1841*: the woman approached by a man who will not stay; here comedy replaces tragedy.
- *green-table-1932*: the war offstage again, but from the point of view of the men who will die in it.

### What a maker can take from it
1. Start from what is on the street outside. The everyday, staged with care, can be as surprising as a palace.
2. Build roles on the people you have: each sailor's solo is a portrait of its first dancer.
3. A world made for one moment will be judged by later ones. Decide what you want a future audience to see in its jokes.

### Beyond the stage
*On the Town* (1944) and the MGM film (1949, Gene Kelly and Frank Sinatra) [documented for the musical; probable for the film]; *West Side Story* (1957, film 1961) by the same team [documented: PBT guide]. Bernstein's *Three Dance Variations from Fancy Free* is concert repertory [documented]. Cadmus's painting now tours museums as a landmark of queer American art [documented: Crystal Bridges 2021].

Foulkes, "Curating Joffrey" (2025, open, http://juliafoulkes.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Curating-Joffrey-Nov25.pdf); Deborah Jowitt, *Jerome Robbins: His Life, His Theater, His Dance* (2004) [probable]; Pia Catton, "Dancing American", *Humanities* 30/1 (2009, https://www.neh.gov/article/dancing-american). Web: https://www.nycballet.com/discover/ballet-repertory/fancy-free; https://www.abt.org/ballet/fancy-free; https://leonardbernstein.com/works/view/7/fancy-free; https://leonardbernstein.com/news/blog/89/75-years-of-fancy-free; https://jeromerobbins.org/about/a-biography-in-brief/; http://www.arts.gov/stories/podcast/bernstein-broadway; https://dancemagazine.com/fancy-free-ballet; https://www.history.navy.mil/our-collections/art/travelling-exhibits/paul-cadmus.html; https://www.metmuseum.org/perspectives/paul-cadmus-the-fleets-in.

## The Four Temperaments / The Four Temperaments (1946)
`id: four-temperaments-1946` · threads: T10 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 20 November 1946, Central High School of Needle Trades, New York, on the opening programme of Ballet Society; conductor Leon Barzin, pianist Nicholas Kopeikine [documented: Balanchine Foundation catalogue no. 236]. Choreography: George Balanchine. Music: Paul Hindemith, *Theme with Four Variations (According to the Four Temperaments)* for string orchestra and piano (1940), commissioned by Balanchine [documented: catalogue]. Scenery and costumes: Kurt Seligmann; lighting: Jean Rosenthal [documented: catalogue]. No scenario. Five parts, about 30 to 33 minutes, 25 dancers [documented: Balanchine Trust; PNB]. The version seen today is New York City Ballet's since 1951: practice clothes (black leotards and white tights or T-shirts), no scenery [documented: catalogue; NYCB].

### In one line
A theme and four variations named after the medieval humours, danced in a new angular classicism that the costumes first hid and then revealed.

### How it came to be
In 1940, flush with money from Broadway and Hollywood, Balanchine commissioned from Hindemith a short piece he could play at home with friends; he was a capable pianist [documented: Balanchine Trust; PNB]. Hindemith (1895–1963), whose music the Nazis had banned and whom Goebbels called an "atonal noisemaker", had just left for the United States and taught at Yale [documented: Balanchine Trust; NYCB story, Sutton 2020]. In 1941 Balanchine began a ballet to the score, *The Cave of Sleep*, for the American Ballet Caravan's South American tour, with designs by Pavel Tchelitchew showing the human nervous system in saturated colour; it was never produced [documented: catalogue; NYCB story]. Madelyn Sutton suggests it was dropped because a German composer was awkward on a tour meant to counter Axis propaganda [documented as her suggestion]. The score had its first concert performance in 1944, with Lukas Foss at the piano [documented: NYCB]. In 1946 Balanchine and Lincoln Kirstein founded Ballet Society, a subscription organisation; unable to afford a theatre for one night, they used a trade-school auditorium [documented: EBSCO]. The curtain went up half an hour late [documented: EBSCO]. The evening also included Ravel's *L'Enfant et les sortilèges* [documented: NYCB].

### Roots and reference points
- **Humoral theory**: from Greek medicine through Galen and the Middle Ages, four bodily fluids (black bile, blood, phlegm, yellow bile) produce four temperaments, linked to earth, air, water and fire [documented: Balanchine Trust]. Balanchine wrote that the idea "was merely the point of departure for both composer and choreographer" [documented: *Complete Stories of the Great Ballets*, quoted by NYCB].
- **Theme and variations**, the oldest musical form for development, sets the structure: three themes stated by three couples, then four variations [documented: catalogue].
- **Balanchine's Broadway and jazz work** of 1936–45 (*On Your Toes*, *Cabin in the Sky*), source of the thrust hips and off-centre accents [probable].
- **Surrealism**: Seligmann (1900–1962), a Swiss painter in New York, wrote on magic and alchemy; he believed that "there cannot be a separation between man, the stars, and the invisible" [documented: Seligmann quoted by NYCB].

### The world it built
Theme (three pas de deux); First Variation, Melancholic (a male soloist and two women, with four more); Second, Sanguinic (a ballerina and partner with four women); Third, Phlegmatic (a male soloist with four women); Fourth, Choleric (a ballerina, then the whole cast) [documented: catalogue]. The vocabulary is "classical steps with a lean and angular style" [documented: Balanchine Trust]: hips pushed forward, flexed feet, wrists bent, women swung by the arms and lowered in splits, the Melancholic man sinking backward into a deep back-bend and crawling offstage [probable for the details]. The finale gathers all the motifs and ends in a vast lift and a line of women in arabesque surging forward like pistons [probable]. Music and dance develop together: as Hindemith's themes grow more complex in the variations, "the elemental dance vocabulary" undergoes "inexhaustible permutations" [documented: PNB note, reprinted from the Balanchine Foundation].

### Design and art
Seligmann's 1946 designs were a surrealist commentary on the humours: oversized costumes in vivid colours, mittens that made partnering hard, breastplates that clanged when dancers met, headdresses that blocked the view [documented: NYCB story]. Audiences, dancers and choreographer found them at odds with the dancing [documented: NYCB story]. In 1951 NYCB dropped them for practice clothes and no scenery [documented: catalogue]. Seligmann's designs and footage of a 1946 rehearsal were shown in MoMA's *Lincoln Kirstein's Modern* (2019) [documented: MoMA programme]. Tchelitchew's unrealised designs are in MoMA's collection [documented: NYCB story]. The stripping became a house style, continued in *Agon* (1957) [documented: W2 §11].

### Premiere and reception
Cast: Theme couples Beatrice Tompkins and José Martinez, Elise Reiman and Lew Christensen, Gisella Caccialanza and Francisco Moncion; Melancholic William Dollar; Sanguinic Mary Ellen Moylan and Fred Danieli; Phlegmatic Todd Bolender; Choleric Tanaquil Le Clercq [documented: catalogue]. Denby (*Dance News*, December 1946): "no choreography was ever more serious, more vigorous, more wide in scope or penetrating in imagination" [documented: EBSCO]. He recalled sitting "spellbound by the constant surprise of the dance development" [documented: PNB]. It was "widely hailed as the most exciting Balanchine ballet yet" [documented: EBSCO]. Money: Ballet Society was supported by subscription [documented: Balanchine chronology].

### Versions and lineage
Ballet Society 1946; NYCB premiere 25 October 1948 on its second programme ever [documented: NYCB; chronology]; 1951 practice clothes; 1964 lighting by David Hays [documented: catalogue]. Staged by dozens of companies (Paris Opéra 2020, Australian Ballet 2021, Dutch National 2022) [documented: catalogue]. Film of the premiere night survives at NYPL, with "the original ending", omitting Phlegmatic [documented: NYPL Digital Collections *MGZHB 12-1637]. Television: Dutch 1962, NBC 1963, CBC 1964, PBS *Dance in America* 1977 and 1984 [documented: catalogue]. Balanchine Foundation Interpreters Archive coaching films 1997, 1998, 2017 [documented].

### Readings and disputes
- *Pure form.* Marcia B. Siegel: "Balanchine is about form, not about the romantic expressiveness" of other theatre [documented: quoted by PNB]. On this reading the humours are titles, not characters.
- *A manifesto.* Arlene Croce calls it "a messianic work, which conveys to this day the sense of a brilliant and bold new understanding" [documented: quoted by PNB].
- *The Africanist presence.* Brenda Dixon Gottschild, *Digging the Africanist Presence in American Performance* (1996), argues that Balanchine's displaced hips and angular accents in works of this period drew on Black American dance [probable for her treatment of this ballet; she is cited on *Agon* in W2 §11].
- *Did the humours matter?* Balanchine said the idea was only a point of departure; NYCB's own writer says whether to take that "at face value is up to the interpreting listener" [documented].
- *Contradictions.* Commission fee: often given as $500 [not found in this pass]. The "original ending" of the 1946 film differs from today's (catalogue and NYPL) [documented]. Number of dancers 25 (Trust) [documented].

### Facts worth telling
- The music began as a commission for parlour evenings at Balanchine's home [documented: Balanchine Trust].
- The premiere was in a high-school auditorium in the garment district [documented: catalogue].
- An earlier version, with designs showing the nervous system, was abandoned in 1941 [documented: catalogue].
- The original costumes clanged and had mittens [documented: NYCB].
- From 1951 the dancers wore what they wore in class [documented: catalogue].
- A film of the first night survives [documented: NYPL].

### Set it beside
- *serenade-1934*: twelve years earlier, the same choreographer's plotless ballet with a ghost of story; here the story is stripped to a label.
- *agon-1957* (W2 §11): the black-and-white sequel, same refusal of costume, now with Stravinsky's serial music.
- *symphony-in-c-1947*: made months later in Paris, its opposite: tutus, jewels, a palace of crystal.
- *triadic-ballet-1922*: Schlemmer's Bauhaus figurines show costume as the whole concept; Balanchine shows its removal as the concept.

### What a maker can take from it
1. Sometimes the design is the problem. Taking it away can reveal what the work really is.
2. A classical system can be bent, not broken: turned-in hips and flexed feet read as new because the audience knows the rule they break.
3. A structural form from another art (theme and variations) gives a world without story its logic.

### Beyond the stage
Seligmann's designs entered museum shows of Kirstein's patronage [documented: MoMA 2019]. The practice-clothes look entered fashion and dance photography as the image of modern ballet [argued; see W2 §11]. Popular-culture reuse: not found in this pass.

## Le Jeune Homme et la Mort / The Young Man and Death (1946)
`id: jeune-homme-et-la-mort-1946` · threads: T15, T19 · confidence of the header: documented (orchestration and costume credit contested)

**Header.** Premiere 25 June 1946, Théâtre des Champs-Élysées, Paris, by the Ballets des Champs-Élysées [documented: CND médiathèque; Opéra de Paris MémOpéra]. Choreography: Roland Petit. Idea and libretto: Jean Cocteau. Music: J. S. Bach, Passacaglia in C minor, BWV 582, in an orchestral arrangement credited to Ottorino Respighi (Cocteau archive) or to Alexander Goedicke (Mariinsky) [documented as two credits]. Set: Georges Wakhévitch. Costumes: Karinska (most sources) or Cocteau [contested: Wikipedia]. "Ballet in two tableaux" [documented: Fonds Cocteau, Montpellier], about 13 to 20 minutes [documented: CND film 13 min; Mariinsky 20 min]. The version seen today is the Paris Opéra Ballet's (in repertory since 5 April 1990) and stagings by Luigi Bonino for the Petit estate [documented: MémOpéra; Mariinsky].

### In one line
In a Paris attic a young painter waits for a girl; she comes, taunts him and leaves; he hangs himself, and she returns as Death to lead him over the rooftops.

### How it came to be
Roland Petit (1924–2011), trained at the Paris Opéra school, left the Opéra in 1944 and in 1945, with the producer Roger Eudes, the writer Boris Kochno (once Diaghilev's secretary), the critic Irène Lidova and his father's money, founded the Ballets des Champs-Élysées [documented: Opéra de Paris biography]. Jean Cocteau (1889–1963), poet, film-maker and author of the ideas for *Parade* and *Le Train bleu* (W2 §6, §8), wanted to make a work for the young dancer Jean Babilée: "Nijinsky got his *Spectre de la rose*... I want to create your own *Spectre*" [documented: ENB, quoting Cocteau]. Cocteau told the story to Petit, who choreographed it; Cocteau wrote the libretto only after the first performances [documented: CND, Camille Riquier Wautier]. Petit rehearsed to improvised jazz (a popular jazz song in some accounts) [documented: ENB, citing Petit's autobiography; Mariinsky]. When the choreography was finished, about 18 minutes long, Cocteau asked the conductor André Girard for music; Mozart and Beethoven were proposed, and Bach's Passacaglia was chosen just before the premiere [documented: ENB]. On the first night the makers feared the music would run out before the dance [documented: Mariinsky note, Olga Makarova]. Cocteau called this the "mystery of accidental synchronism" [documented: Fonds Cocteau, "mystère du synchronisme accidentel"]. Money was short a year after the Liberation, so Wakhévitch brought the set from a film he was finishing, *Martin Roumagnac* (with Marlene Dietrich and Jean Gabin) [documented: ENB].

### Roots and reference points
- **Cocteau's personal myth** of the poet haunted by a Muse who is also Death, shared with his films *Le Sang d'un poète* (1932) and *Orphée* (1950) [documented: CND].
- **Le Spectre de la rose** (1911): a role made for one male star [documented: Cocteau via ENB].
- **The medieval Dance of Death** and the Romantic *Liebestod* [argued].
- **Existentialism**: some saw it as the danced expression of the Paris of Sartre and Camus [documented as a reception: Fonds Cocteau].
- **Cinema and silent film**: realist gestures and film sets gave "the allure of a silent film shot under the roofs of a contemporary Paris" [documented: CND].
- **Mime**: Cocteau wrote that it was "not a ballet" but "a mimodrama where pantomime exaggerates its style to that of dance" [documented: *La Difficulté d'être*, 1947, via Fonds Cocteau; our translation].

### The world it built
Two tableaux: the attic, then the rooftops [documented: Fonds Cocteau]. A single bed, a table, chairs, a beam with a rope and noose; the Young Man smokes, checks his watch, kicks the furniture, throws chairs [documented: ENB; CND]. Cocteau wanted to "heighten everyday gestures, up to the point where they become dance" [documented: ENB]. Babilée's role mixes ballet virtuosity with acrobatics: "bold jumps and slow-motion rolls" [documented: CND]. The Girl, in heels, slaps and teases him and points to the noose [probable for details]. He hangs himself; the walls of the attic fly away and the night skyline of Paris appears, lit, with the Eiffel Tower; the Girl returns in a white gown with a skull mask, places it on his face and leads him away across the roofs [documented for the skyline: ENB; probable for the mask]. The music never "illustrates" the action: Bach's repeating ground bass lifts it from melodrama [documented as Makarova's argument].

### Design and art
Georges Wakhévitch (1907–1984), a Russian-born film designer, made a realist garret with peeling walls and a skylight [probable], then the "coup de théâtre" of the Paris skyline [documented: ENB]. The Young Man's costume was Babilée's own choice: Cocteau wanted a white shirt and black trousers, but Babilée "was feeling so good in my dungarees" that he kept them [documented: ENB, quoting Babilée]. His wristwatch stayed because he needed it to keep time with no music [documented: ENB]. The Girl's short yellow dress and black wig belong to the image of the piece [probable]. Cocteau drew the dancers for *Art et style* (October 1946) [documented: Fonds Cocteau]. Costume credit: Karinska in most programmes; Wikipedia also names Cocteau and "Tom Keogh" [contested].

### Premiere and reception
Babilée (1923–2014) and Nathalie Philippart danced the premiere; they later married [documented for cast; probable for marriage]. The ballet was an "immediate success" [documented: France Culture 2024] and launched Petit, aged 22, internationally, especially in America [documented: CND]. The *New York Times* later called Babilée "a savage, unkempt virtuoso. He almost spat as he danced" [documented: quoted by ENB]. Box office: not found in this pass.

### Versions and lineage
Ballets des Champs-Élysées 1946; Ballet Theatre (ABT) 1951 [documented: Wikipedia]; film of 1955 with Babilée and Claire Sombert (CND médiathèque, 13 min) [documented]; film directed by Petit with Rudolf Nureyev and Zizi Jeanmaire, dated 1966 (Wikipedia) or 1967 (ENB) [contested]; ABT revival for Mikhail Baryshnikov 1975; the 1985 film *White Nights* opens with Baryshnikov dancing it [documented: Wikipedia]; Babilée danced it again in 1984 at 61 [documented: ENB]; Paris Opéra 1990 (Kader Belarbi), Mariinsky 1998, Bolshoi 2009, ENB 2018 [documented]. In about 1956 Babilée's revival at the Olympia led Eudes to sue, claiming that he owned choreography, sets and costumes because he had paid for them; ENB calls it the earliest dispute over ballet copyright in France [documented as ENB's claim].

### Readings and disputes
- *Cocteau's work or Petit's?* The CND treats it as an "étroite collaboration" and notes that Cocteau shaped choreography and design; the Opéra treats it as a Petit ballet [documented]. The libretto written afterwards complicates who "authored" the story [documented: CND].
- *Existentialist dance or Romantic myth?* One reading sets it in the postwar Paris of despair and freedom [documented: Fonds Cocteau]; another, Makarova's, says Bach turned it from realism to timelessness [documented: Mariinsky]. Both depend on the music chosen last.
- *A misogynist allegory?* [argued, this file] The woman is the cause and agent of the man's death, as in the femme fatale tradition from *Carmen* to film noir; feminist readings of Petit's women should be checked against Petit scholarship (not found in this pass).
- *Contradictions.* Respighi or Goedicke as orchestrator [contested]; Karinska, Cocteau or Keogh for costumes [contested]; Nureyev film 1966 or 1967 [contested]; Wikipedia says Petit "is purported" to have made it for Jeanmaire, which conflicts with Cocteau's account that it was for Babilée [contested; we favour Cocteau's].

### Facts worth telling
- It was rehearsed to jazz and performed to Bach [documented: Fonds Cocteau].
- The set came from a Marlene Dietrich film [documented: ENB].
- The dancer kept his watch on because he had to time himself without music [documented: ENB].
- Cocteau said it was "not a ballet" [documented].
- It led to an early French court case over who owns a ballet [documented: ENB].
- Baryshnikov dances it in the opening of *White Nights* (1985) [documented: Wikipedia].

### Set it beside
- *spectre-de-la-rose-1911*: the model Cocteau named, a dream visitor in a bedroom for one male star.
- *green-table-1932*: Death as a dancer in a political world; here Death is a private seduction.
- *giselle-1841*: a woman who returns from death; here the woman returns as Death.
- *carmen-petit-1949*: Petit's next great success, the femme fatale with a story to carry her.

### What a maker can take from it
1. Build the action first and choose the soundtrack last: friction between them can create meaning neither had alone.
2. Reuse what exists. A borrowed film set became one of ballet's best-known reveals.
3. Write down who owns what before the work succeeds. This one ended in court.

### Beyond the stage
*White Nights* (1985) carried it to a mass film audience [documented]. The Nureyev–Jeanmaire film is widely circulated [documented: Wikipedia]. Cocteau's drawings of it belong to his graphic work [documented]. Fashion: Babilée's dungarees and bare torso became a template for the "rebel" male dancer [argued].

Web: https://www.ballet.org.uk/blog/five-things-le-jeune-homme-et-la-mort; https://www.ballet.org.uk/blog-detail/five-famous-jeunes-hommes; https://prim.mariinsky.ru/en/playbill/repertoire/ballet/yunosha; https://www.memopera.fr/oeuvre.php?code=JEU; https://www.operadeparis.fr/en/artists/roland-petit; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_jeune_homme_et_la_mort; France Culture podcast (2024) with Cocteau's 1954 radio presentation.

## Le Palais de cristal / Symphony in C (1947)
`id: symphony-in-c-1947` · threads: T10 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 28 July 1947, Théâtre National de l'Opéra (Palais Garnier), Paris, by the Paris Opéra Ballet, conductor Roger Désormière [documented: Balanchine Foundation catalogue no. 240]. Choreography: George Balanchine, as guest ballet master. Music: Georges Bizet, Symphony No. 1 in C major (1855), played complete [documented: catalogue; artlyrique.fr]. No scenario. Scenery and costumes: Leonor Fini [documented: catalogue]. One act, four movements and finale, about 32 to 33 minutes; 48 to 52 dancers [documented: Balanchine Trust gives both 48 and 52]. US version as *Symphony in C*: Ballet Society, City Center, New York, 22 March 1948, lighting Jean Rosenthal; costumes by Karinska from 1950 [documented: catalogue no. 244]. The version seen today is NYCB's, in white tutus and black for the men; since 2012 NYCB uses Marc Happel's Swarovski-crystal redesign [documented: NYCB].

### In one line
Four movements of a teenager's lost symphony, each led by a different ballerina, piled up in a finale where the whole company fills the stage.

### How it came to be
Bizet wrote the symphony at seventeen, as a pupil of Charles Gounod at the Paris Conservatoire, beginning on 29 October 1855 and finishing within a month [documented: artlyrique.fr, quoting the manuscript]. It lay unknown until 1933, when the musicologist Jean Chantavoine found it among Bizet manuscripts at the Conservatoire library [documented: artlyrique.fr]. Felix Weingartner conducted what appears to be its earliest performance, in Basel on 26 February 1935 [documented: artlyrique.fr]. Stravinsky told Balanchine about it [documented: Balanchine Trust]. In 1947 the Paris Opéra, whose ballet master Serge Lifar had been suspended after the Liberation for his conduct under the Occupation, invited Balanchine for six months [documented for the six months: catalogue; probable for Lifar's suspension]. He staged *Apollo*, *Serenade* and *Le Baiser de la fée*, and made one new work [documented: catalogue]. He choreographed it in two weeks, to show "the talent of the whole company" [documented: Balanchine Trust; Wikipedia]. A year later he restaged it, with simpler designs and a new title, for Ballet Society; it was on the first programme of New York City Ballet on 11 October 1948 with *Concerto Barocco* and *Orpheus* [documented: catalogue].

### Roots and reference points
- **The French school** of the Opéra, with its strict hierarchy of étoiles, premiers danseurs and corps; Balanchine is said to have paid homage to Léo Staats, the Opéra's ballet master and choreographer earlier in the century [documented as a claim: Wikipedia].
- **Petipa's grand ballets**: the multi-ballerina finale and the processional entries recall *The Sleeping Beauty*'s Act 3 and *Raymonda*'s divertissements [argued].
- **Earlier use of the score**: Andrée Howard's *Assembly Ball* for Sadler's Wells (April 1946) had already used the symphony [documented: artlyrique.fr].
- **Jewels as a palette**: the four movements were dressed as four gems, an idea Balanchine reused in *Jewels* (1967) [documented: Wikipedia].

### The world it built
First movement, Allegro vivo: formal and regal. Second, Adagio: a long pas de deux, considered one of the most privileged ballerina roles in the Balanchine repertory, the ballerina bending back over her partner's arm to the oboe melody. Third, Allegro vivace: bravura jumps. Fourth, Allegro vivace: speed. Each movement has a principal couple, two demi-solo couples and six women [documented: PNB; catalogue]. In the finale every group returns in order and the stage fills; at City Center the stage was too small for the full cast, which became possible only at the New York State Theater [documented: Wikipedia]. The structure follows the symphony bar for bar; the choreography is "class technique" displayed as spectacle [documented: NYCB, Teresa Reichlen interview]. The world's only rule is order: who enters when, in what number, in what line [argued].

### Design and art
Leonor Fini (1907–1996), an Argentine-born Italian surrealist painter in Paris, known for portraits of powerful women and theatrical masks, gave each movement a gem and a colour: rubies (red), black diamonds (black), emeralds (green), pearls (white) [documented: Wikipedia]. The title, "The Crystal Palace", made the whole a jewel box, with a décor of glittering architecture [probable for the set]. In New York the colours and the palace went: Karinska's white tutus and black men's costumes (1950) made the dancers themselves the jewels [documented: catalogue; Wikipedia]. NYCB's 2012 costumes by Marc Happel added Swarovski crystals, crowns, headpieces by Robert Sorrell and earrings by Jamie Wolf [documented: NYCB]. The Royal Ballet uses sets and costumes by Anthony Dowell [documented: Wikipedia].

### Premiere and reception
Paris cast: Lycette Darsonval and Alexandre Kalioujny (first movement); Tamara Toumanova and Roger Ritz (second); Micheline Bardin and Michel Renault (third); Madeleine Lafon and Max Bozzoni (fourth) [documented: catalogue]. Ballet Society cast 1948: Maria Tallchief, Tanaquil Le Clercq, Beatrice Tompkins, Elise Reiman with Nicholas Magallanes, Francisco Moncion, Herbert Bliss and John Taras [documented: catalogue; ABT lists Lew Christensen for the fourth movement, a discrepancy]. By 1956 the Paris production had passed fifty performances and was "always warmly received" [documented: artlyrique.fr, from a 1955–56 article]. Jerome Robbins saw it at NYCB's opening and decided to join the company [documented as a claim: Wikipedia]. Named 1947 Paris reviews: not found in this pass.

### Versions and lineage
Paris Opéra 1947 (as *Le Palais de cristal*, with Fini); Ballet Society and NYCB 1948; Karinska 1950; Royal Danish Ballet 1952; La Scala 1955; Kirov 1970; Paris Opéra again 1985 as *Symphonie en ut*; Royal Ballet 1991 [documented: catalogue; ROH Collections]. When the Paris Opéra Ballet danced it at the Bolshoi in Moscow in June 1958, it was the earliest ballet Balanchine had made in the West to be seen in the Soviet Union since he left in 1924 [documented: catalogue]. Licensed now by the George Balanchine Trust, with copyright listed to the School of American Ballet [documented: Balanchine Trust].

### Readings and disputes
- *Balanchine's homage to the Petersburg and Paris academies* [documented as the companies' reading: PNB; argued in detail]. It shows a company's hierarchy as a pleasure, not a burden.
- *A Cold War object* [argued, this file]: a Russian émigré's work, made in Paris and owned in New York, became the vehicle by which he returned to Moscow in 1958; read with *Agon* (W2 §11), it shows "American" ballet carried by Paris.
- *The ballerina as jewel* [argued]: Fini's gems and NYCB's crystals turn women into precious objects on display. Against that, the ballet gives four women equal stature and the men serve them; feminist readings of Balanchine (Ann Daly, 1987; see *serenade-1934*) apply.
- *Contradictions.* 48 or 52 dancers on the same Trust page [documented]. ABT and the catalogue differ on the fourth-movement man in 1948 [documented]. The discovery of the manuscript: Chantavoine 1933 from Bizet's widow's bequest, or via Reynaldo Hahn [documented as two accounts in artlyrique.fr]. The register describes the 1948 redesign as "white and black for Ballet Society 1948"; the catalogue dates Karinska's costumes to 1950 [documented: register correction].

### Facts worth telling
- Bizet wrote the music at seventeen, and nobody heard it for eighty years [documented: artlyrique.fr].
- Balanchine made the ballet in two weeks [documented: Balanchine Trust].
- Each Paris movement was a precious stone in its own colour [documented: Wikipedia].
- The first NYCB stage was too small for the whole finale [documented: Wikipedia].
- It was Balanchine's return to Moscow, by way of Paris, in 1958 [documented: catalogue].
- NYCB's current costumes are covered in Swarovski crystals [documented: NYCB].

### Set it beside
- *four-temperaments-1946*: made eight months earlier, its opposite: costumes stripped off, angular movement.
- *sleeping-beauty-1890*: the Petipa model of a court displayed through dancing; here the court has no king.
- *etudes-1948*: six months later in Copenhagen, the same idea of showing a company's whole technique, but as a lesson rather than a palace.
- *serenade-1934*: the same choreographer's Tchaikovsky in moonlight, against Bizet in daylight.

### What a maker can take from it
1. A clear structure (four movements, then all together) lets an audience follow a world with no story.
2. Colour can code a system. Fini's four gems are a design device anyone can borrow; *Jewels* borrowed it from her.
3. The same work can be re-dressed for a new home. Decide which layer is the work (here, the choreography and music) and which is a costume.

### Beyond the stage
The NYCB–Swarovski collaboration (2012) linked it to luxury brand design [documented: NYCB]. Bizet's symphony became concert and recording repertory after 1935 [documented]. Film: Victor Jessen's 1951 footage of NYCB [documented: Wikipedia]. Popular culture: not found in this pass.
