# D4. The Soviet Union and its republics, 1930 to 1967

Repertory dossiers, pass D4. Written 21 November 2026 under `../BRIEF_REPERTORY.md`. Ten works or families. *The Red Poppy*, *The Flames of Paris* and *Spartacus* are taken apart in `../../works/W2_modernism.md` §10 and `W3_remaking_global.md` and are not repeated here.

## Золотой век / The Golden Age (1930)
`id: golden-age-1930` · threads: T08, T11, T09 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 26 October 1930, Leningrad State Academic Theatre of Opera and Ballet (GATOB, the former and future Mariinsky, named the Kirov from 1935) [documented: Mariinsky exhibition page "Shostakovich at the Mariinsky", text by Olga Makarova]. Choreographers Vasily Vainonen (Act I), Leonid Yakobson (Act II) and Vladimir Chesnakov (Act III); overall staging by the opera director Emmanuil Kaplan with Vainonen [documented: Mariinsky; act division documented in Wikipedia]. Music Dmitri Shostakovich, Op. 22, his first ballet, written at 23 and 24. Libretto Alexander Ivanovsky, a film director. Sets and costumes Valentina Khodasevich. Conductor Alexander Gauk. Three acts (six or eight scenes; sources differ). The version most often seen now is not the 1930 ballet but Yuri Grigorovich's 1982 Bolshoi remake, with a new story, and the concert suite Op. 22a.

### In one line
A Soviet football team visits a capitalist city's industrial fair, resists its jazz, its fascists and its seductress, and is freed by the local workers: a political cartoon made into a dance revue.

### How it came to be
On 6 January 1929 the Leningrad paper *Zhizn' iskusstva* (Life of Art) printed the rules of a competition for "a Soviet ballet" that would give "material for a pantomime-choreographic experiment" [documented: conditions cited by Makarova from *Zhizn' iskusstva*, 6 Jan 1929, p. 2]. The theatre wanted to break "the domination of the decrepit classical repertoire" [documented: 1931 audience booklet quoted by Makarova]. Ivanovsky's entry, first called *Dinamiada* (after the Dynamo sports club), won [documented: Belcanto.ru]. The libretto was handed to Shostakovich, who wrote 37 numbers in the rest of 1929 [documented: Belcanto.ru]. A historian of Leningrad ballet notes that Shostakovich and the ballet chief Fyodor Lopukhov were unenthusiastic about the libretto but, after the Shakhty show trial of 1928, "in no position to refuse" [documented as a claim: Kate Lebere, UVic honours thesis 2019, citing Carolyn Pouncy 2005].

The theatre then did something new. It gave a full-length ballet to three young dancers who had never staged one, and put an opera director over them. Makarova calls this the earliest case at the theatre of a director supervising a ballet, a practice that became standard in the drambalet [documented: Makarova]. Rehearsals ran over a year. The premiere was planned for 28 June 1930 and moved to the autumn because the school children in the cast had a right to summer holidays [documented: Mariinsky].

### Roots and reference points
- **Sport and physical culture.** Shostakovich wrote that Soviet dances must be "saturated with elements of healthy physical culture and sport" [documented: 1931 booklet, quoted by Makarova]. He was a keen football fan [probable: MusicWeb review, 2018].
- **The West as entertainment.** Foxtrot, tango, cancan, tap ("chechotka") and music-hall stood for capitalism. The famous entr'acte *Tahiti Trot* is Shostakovich's orchestration of Vincent Youmans's "Tea for Two" (from *No, No, Nanette*, 1925), written for a bet with the conductor Nikolai Malko that he could not do it in under an hour; he took about 40 minutes [documented: LA Phil programme note, Dennis Bade]. The date of the bet is usually given as 1927; LA Phil says the piece was first heard in 1928 [contested in detail, probable 1927–28].
- **Agitprop and the revue.** The ballet borrows from the "living newspaper" and the workers' club show: quick scenes, types instead of characters (the Diva, the Fascist, the Negro boxer, the Komsomol girl) [argued]. Earlier Soviet works it follows: *The Red Poppy* (1927) for the decadent-West Charleston (see W2 §10a) [argued].

### The world it built
Three acts. Act I: the "Golden Age" industrial exhibition in a Western city, a boxing match "for advertising" in which a fascist referee rules against a Black boxer and a Komsomol girl slaps him; the fascist Diva tries to seduce the Soviet team captain and is refused [documented: Lebere 2019; Mariinsky photo captions]. Act II: provocations, planted leaflets, arrest. Act III: the workers free the team; a football match; a final dance of solidarity [documented: MusicWeb].

Two vocabularies carry the politics [argued]. The Soviets dance athletic, acrobatic, high-lifting movement; the bourgeoisie dances ballroom and cabaret with what Shostakovich called "the character of unhealthy eroticism" [documented: booklet via Makarova]. Gauk heard the score as "two warring planes", ironic West against energetic sport [documented: Gauk, quoted by Belcanto.ru]. Signature passages: Yakobson's "magnifying glass of time", in which a whole crowd of sportsmen suddenly froze and moved on in slow motion, "like a rapid" film effect, which audiences cheered [documented: Makarova]; the Pioneer dance, where children strip a boy dressed as a top-hatted capitalist to reveal a Pioneer underneath [documented: Shostakovich's memoir of Yakobson, TsGALI, via Makarova]; the "Pendulum", the dance of the Western Komsomol girl and four Soviet footballers. The score: saxophones, banjo, a symphonic adagio with solo saxophone, a fugue in Act III, polka, foxtrot, tango [documented: Wikipedia instrumentation; Belcanto.ru].

### Design and art
Khodasevich (1894–1970), a painter trained in Paris and Munich and an experienced drama designer, set out her rules in the 1931 booklet: break with "pictorial representational decorations"; maximum use of décor in the action; "entertainment; quick change of impressions"; place and situation told "by props and costumes, played with by the actor" [documented: quoted by Makarova]. Her sketches for the Diva and a Secretary survive in the Mariinsky collection [documented: captions]. The look was constructivist revue rather than painted fairyland [argued]. Grigorovich's 1982 remake was designed by Simon Virsaladze [documented: BelAir Blu-ray credits via Classical Net].

### Premiere and reception
The Diva was Olga Jordan (Iordan), with Elena Lyukom in another cast; the fascists included Leonid Lavrovsky, later the choreographer of *Romeo and Juliet*; Galina Ulanova, Olga Mungalova, Konstantin Sergeyev and Vakhtang Chabukiani danced in Yakobson's act [documented: Mariinsky photo captions]. Audiences loved it. At the dress-rehearsal debate it was called "un-Soviet", some dances "pitiful and wretched"; one speaker said the old ballet had been "laid flat on both shoulder blades" by it [documented: council minutes, TsGALI f. 337, via Makarova]. The *Krasnaya gazeta* critic blamed "formalism and aestheticism" and an "unjustified passion for music hall" [documented: SPb Vedomosti, 22 Nov 2024]. The labour-safety inspector wanted the "Pendulum" removed: "Once Ulanova's pulse was 170" [documented: minutes via Makarova]. The run was 18 performances by Makarova's journal article and 19 by her Mariinsky exhibition text, 26 Oct 1930 to 15 May 1931 [contested: same author, two counts].

### Versions and lineage
- 1930 Leningrad: lost as choreography; photographs, sketches, minutes and the répétiteur score survive [probable].
- 1982 Bolshoi, Grigorovich, new libretto with the musicologist Isaak Glikman, Shostakovich's friend: the action moves to a Soviet seaside town in the 1920s and a restaurant called "The Golden Age"; Boris the fisherman, Rita the cabaret dancer and Yashka the gangster; music padded with other Shostakovich works, including piano concerto movements [documented: Wikipedia; Mariinsky 2006 page; Classical Net]. Premiere 4 November 1982 [documented: Wikipedia; SPb Vedomosti] or 14 October 1982 [documented: Belcanto.ru] [contested]. The role of Boris was made on Irek Mukhamedov [documented: Wikipedia]. Revived 2016 and filmed for cinema [documented: MusicWeb].
- 2006 Mariinsky, Noah D. Gelber with the drama director Andrei Prikotenko, new libretto by Konstantin Uchitel, full original score [documented: Mariinsky 2006 page].
Grigorovich had once asked Shostakovich's permission to revive his ballets and was told "Don't reanimate these ballets, I wouldn't want that" [documented: Mariinsky 2006 page, quoting the anecdote].

### Readings and disputes
- **A failed experiment killed by politics.** The ballet was withdrawn in the turn against "formalism"; its music survived only in the suite [documented: Wikipedia; MusicWeb]. Makarova leaves the cause open: the failure of *The Bolt* (1931) or the campaign against "class enemies" [documented: Makarova 2017].
- **A school for choreographers.** Makarova's own thesis (Vaganova Academy *Vestnik* 2017, no. 2) is that the real "golden age" it opened was for young choreographers: Vainonen and Yakobson went on to *The Flames of Paris* and *Spartacus* [argued, Makarova].
- **Satire with two edges.** Laura E. Kennedy's book *Shostakovich's Ballets and the Search for Soviet Dance* argues that the three ballets offered three distinct answers to the demand for a Soviet ballet (modern, then romantic) [documented: catalogue abstract; publisher and year not confirmed in this pass]. Critics in 1930 complained that the "decadent" young people came out attractive rather than repellent [documented: SPb Vedomosti]. A reading: the satire let a Soviet audience enjoy the jazz it was told to despise [argued].
- **The 1982 version as a different work.** Arlene Croce wrote that Grigorovich "doesn't tell stories, he untells them" [documented: *New Yorker*, 27 July 1987].

### Facts worth telling
1. *Tahiti Trot* is "Tea for Two", orchestrated in about 40 minutes for a bet [documented: LA Phil].
2. The premiere was postponed because the child performers had a right to summer holidays [documented: Mariinsky].
3. A safety inspector took Ulanova's pulse in rehearsal: 170 [documented: Makarova].
4. Lavrovsky, later Bolshoi chief choreographer, danced a Fascist [documented: Mariinsky caption].
5. The ballet was performed 18 or 19 times and then not staged for 52 years [contested count; documented gap].
6. Shostakovich refused permission for revivals in his lifetime [documented: Mariinsky 2006, anecdote].
7. The orchestra includes a banjo and two saxophones [documented: Wikipedia].

### Set it beside
- *pas-dacier-1927* (Massine and Prokofiev for Diaghilev, Paris): the Soviet factory as seen from Paris, against the Paris café as seen from Leningrad.
- *train-bleu-1924*: sport and leisure as chic in the West; sport as political virtue here (T11).
- *flames-of-paris-1932* (W2 §10b): two years later the same theatre, with Vainonen, found the formula (history, folk, research) that *The Golden Age* lacked.
- *fancy-free-1944*: American popular dance used with affection, not satire.

### What a maker can take from it
- When the "villain" style is the most fun to make, it may become the audience's favourite. Decide which side the pleasure lies on.
- A suite can save a world when the world is cancelled: package a portable piece of your concept.
- A single device (the slow-motion freeze) can outlive a whole production in memory.

### Beyond the stage
The suite, the Polka (reused in Two Pieces for String Quartet, 1931, and arranged for piano) and *Tahiti Trot* are concert and encore standards [documented: Wikipedia]. Grigorovich's 2016 staging was released on Blu-ray by BelAir Classiques (BAC443) [documented].

## Бахчисарайский фонтан / The Fountain of Bakhchisarai (1934)
`id: fountain-bakhchisarai-1934` · threads: T18, T05, T15 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 28 September 1934, State Academic Theatre of Opera and Ballet (GATOB, Leningrad; named for Kirov from 1935) [documented: Mariinsky repertoire page]. Choreography Rostislav Zakharov, aged 27; stage director Sergei Radlov; music Boris Asafyev (Asafiev); libretto Nikolai Volkov "based on the poem by Alexander Pushkin"; sets and costumes Valentina Khodasevich; conductor Yevgeny Mravinsky [documented: Mariinsky; *Russkii balet* encyclopedia, 1997, via pro-ballet.ru]. Called a "choreographic poem" in four acts with prologue and epilogue; about 2 hours 45 minutes with two intervals. The version seen today is Zakharov's, still in the Mariinsky repertoire in Khodasevich's designs (performances November 2026) [documented: Mariinsky playbill].

### In one line
A Crimean khan who has taken everything by force is changed by the one thing he cannot take, the love of a captive Polish princess: Pushkin's poem turned into the model Soviet "ballet-play".

### How it came to be
The idea came from the librettist. Asafyev wrote that Nikolai Volkov "suggested, and not only suggested but convinced me" to write a Pushkin ballet; he hesitated for a year [documented: Asafyev, *O sebe* (memoir), via balletmusic.ru]. Volkov shaped his libretto as a "ballet play" in 1932 [documented: ru.wikipedia]. In the summers of 1932 and 1933 Asafyev watched the "romantic mood" of young couples in the parks of Pavlovsk and Detskoe Selo and his doubts fell away [documented: memoir]. He composed through 1933.

The team was a seminar of Leningrad's cultural elite: Asafyev, musicologist and the leading theorist of "choreodrama"; Radlov, the drama director who then set the theatre's policy; Mravinsky; Khodasevich; Volkov; the critic Ivan Sollertinsky [documented: Mariinsky programme text]. Zakharov had trained as a dancer and then in the directing faculty of the Leningrad Institute of Stage Arts, and was a devotee of Stanislavsky [documented: Mariinsky; Kommersant, Tatiana Kuznetsova, 19 July 2024]. He was a "debutant in grand ballet" [documented: Mariinsky]. He insisted on additions to Pushkin: a Polish act with Maria's fiancé Vaslav, the khan's return from his raid, the dance of Tatar warriors and Zarema's execution; at his request the warrior Nurali was added [documented: ru.wikipedia; Kommersant]. Two years later the ballet moved to the Bolshoi (11 June 1936) and Zakharov became the Bolshoi's chief choreographer [documented: *Russkii balet* encyclopedia; Kommersant].

### Roots and reference points
- **Pushkin, *Bakhchisaraiskii fontan* (1823)**, a "southern poem" written after Pushkin's 1820 visit to the Khan's palace; Byron's *The Giaour* and *The Corsair* lie behind it [probable]. The real Fountain of Tears in the palace courtyard dates from 1764 [probable, working knowledge].
- **Music of Pushkin's own time.** Asafyev refused "folk naturalist amalgam" and impressionism. He chose a song: Alexander Gurilyov's romance setting Pushkin's lines "To the Fountain of the Bakhchisarai Palace", itself drawn from Adolf Henselt's piano piece *La Fontaine*. This tune frames the ballet and, in variations, becomes Maria's theme [documented: Asafyev memoir; Krasnova and Lanina 2024]. The overture runs "from Mozart to Rossini with Beethoven cores", to show Pushkin's imagination sailing from the 18th century into Romanticism [documented: memoir].
- **Noverre and Stanislavsky.** Zakharov read Noverre's *Letters on Dancing*, newly translated with Sollertinsky's preface on dramatic dance [documented: Mariinsky]. The "table talk" of a drama rehearsal, where actors discuss motives, came into the ballet studio [documented: Mariinsky].
- **Earlier works it answers.** *The Flames of Paris* (1932, W2 §10b), the same theatre's first research-based drambalet, with Volkov and Asafyev; Asafyev wrote that he worked "as in *The Flames of Paris*, but bolder" [documented: memoir]. Fokine's *Schéhérazade* (1910, W2 §1) is the harem precedent it rewrites as drama rather than orgy [argued].

### The world it built
Prologue: Ghirei by the fountain. Act I: a Polish castle, Maria's birthday; polonaise, mazurka, krakowiak; a Tatar raid; Vaslav killed; the khan tears off Maria's veil and is "struck motionless by her beauty". Act II: the harem; Zarema, "the star of love", loses the khan. Act III: Maria's chamber, a harp her only memory of home; Zarema begs her and, finding the khan's cap there, stabs her. Act IV: the khan's court; Zarema is thrown from a cliff; warrior dances bring no relief. Epilogue: the fountain again [documented: Mariinsky synopsis].

The rule of the drambalet is visible here [argued from documented features]: every dance has a reason in the plot; mime and acting carry the story; each role is a psychological portrait. The Mariinsky's own text admits "dance at times remained on the periphery"; "the ballerina is an actress and the danseur an actor" [documented]. Two movement worlds meet: the Polish ball (classical dance plus national character dance) and the Tatar camp (massed, leaping male dance). Ulanova's Maria is almost a role of walking, listening and recoil; Zarema's is the virtuoso, passionate part [probable]. Asafyev treated the Tatars as "Ghirei's army, outside ethnology" [documented: memoir], and the harem as a mosaic of "ethno-intonations" (Italy, Slavs, Caucasus, Turkic-Tatar) [documented].

### Design and art
Khodasevich was responsible for "plausibility" of scenery and costumes [documented: Kommersant]. The look is painted historical realism: a moonlit Polish park and Gothic castle; the Bakhchisarai palace with lattices and carpets; the marble fountain as the closing image [probable, from current Mariinsky photographs]. Maria's white dress and small harp against Zarema's jewelled harem costume set out the two women in colour [argued]. Krasnova and Lanina note that the harp also points back to the Pushkin era and Didelot's ballets, such as *The Aeolian Harp* [documented: 2024]. Her designs have now lasted more than ninety years on one stage, a record few 1930s sets can match [argued].

### Premiere and reception
Galina Ulanova (Maria), Olga Jordan (Zarema), Konstantin Sergeyev (Vaslav), Mikhail Dudko (Ghirei) [documented: encyclopedia]. "Extraordinary success" [documented: Kommersant]. Musicians were hostile: Asafyev recalled that all of them "scolded" the score before and after its success, and that at a composers' union meeting he was refused a vote "as not a composer but a musicologist"; he wrote, "Success cost me dearly!" [documented: memoir]. Moscow had it twice in 1936: at the Nemirovich-Danchenko theatre on 20 April and at the Bolshoi on 11 June [documented: encyclopedia]. Ulanova kept Maria all her career and danced it at Covent Garden in 1956 [documented: Fjord Review].

### Versions and lineage
- 1934 Leningrad (Zakharov, Radlov). 1936 Nemirovich-Danchenko and Bolshoi. 1944 Bolshoi revival (Maria: Bogolyubskaya, then Ulanova). 1946 Kirov revival by Pyotr Gusev "after Zakharov". 1954 Bolshoi revival [documented: encyclopedia].
- 1953 film *Masters (Stars) of the Russian Ballet*, abridged, Ulanova (Maria) and Maya Plisetskaya (Zarema): the only known footage of the two dancing together [documented: Wikipedia].
- Export: staged in Japan 1957 and as the opening work of the Cairo company 1966, under Lavrovsky [documented: W3 §10, §11]. The register note also names Beijing [not confirmed in this pass].
- No reconstruction is needed: the work has never left the Mariinsky repertoire for long [probable].

### Readings and disputes
- **The model drambalet.** The Mariinsky calls it the "star hour" of the drama ballet and says "literary centrism" became Soviet ballet's mainstream "for the next twenty years" [documented]. Krasnova and Lanina treat it as a successful "intersemiotic translation" in Roman Jakobson's sense [documented: *Palimpsest* 2024].
- **A ballet of acting, not of dance.** The same Mariinsky text concedes dance was peripheral. Critics of the drambalet, and later Grigorovich's generation, called the genre "dance-less" pantomime [probable: Ezrahi, *Swans of the Kremlin*, 2012].
- **Orientalism and Crimea.** The Tatars are raiders, the khan's change of heart comes from a Christian European woman, and Asafyev took the Tatars "outside ethnology" [argued, from the documented memoir]. The Crimean Tatars were deported from Crimea by Stalin's government in May 1944 while the ballet stayed in the repertoire [probable, working knowledge]. Counter-reading: Pushkin's poem is itself a romantic fantasy and the ballet says so in its music; its subject is a "spiritual rebirth of a despot", not ethnography [documented: Asafyev's own framing].
- **Who made it?** Credit is split: Asafyev calls Volkov the originator; Zakharov added the plot; Radlov was director. Posters name Zakharov as choreographer and Radlov as director [documented: ru.wikipedia infobox].

### Facts worth telling
1. The idea was the librettist's, not the composer's [documented: Asafyev].
2. The theme tune is an 1820s romance on Pushkin's own lines [documented: Asafyev; Krasnova and Lanina].
3. Pushkin's poem has no Vaslav: the ballet invented a fiancé so the audience could see what Maria lost [documented: Mariinsky].
4. Zakharov was 27; the ballet made him chief choreographer of the Bolshoi [documented: Mariinsky; Kommersant].
5. Asafyev was denied a composers' vote because he was "only" a musicologist [documented: memoir].
6. The 1953 film is the only footage of Ulanova and Plisetskaya together [documented: Wikipedia].
7. Khodasevich's 1934 designs were still on stage in November 2026 [documented: Mariinsky playbill].

### Set it beside
- *scheherazade-1910*: the harem as erotic spectacle in Paris; here as tragic psychology in Leningrad (T05).
- *giselle-1841*: a woman's death changes a nobleman; Maria's death changes a khan (T15).
- *legend-of-love-1961*: Grigorovich's "Eastern" ballet, made against this model with dance rather than mime.
- *el-leila-el-kebira-2001* (W3 §10): Cairo began with this ballet in 1966 and later made its own.

### What a maker can take from it
- When adapting, add what the medium needs: the ballet could not narrate loss, so it built a whole act showing the life before.
- One tune of the period can carry a whole world's time and place better than a hundred "authentic" details.
- A method can travel further than a work: the "ballet-play" was exported with this ballet as its sample.

### Beyond the stage
The 1953 film; Soviet recordings of the score [probable]. The fountain itself, with a bust of Pushkin beside it, is now a visitor site in the Bakhchisarai palace [documented: Wikipedia caption].

## Ромео и Джульетта / Romeo and Juliet (1940, and the family after it)
`id: romeo-juliet-lavrovsky-1940` (with `romeo-juliet-macmillan-1965`) · threads: T18, T24, T15, T13 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Score: Sergei Prokofiev, Op. 64, piano score finished 8 September 1935 [documented: BSO programme note]. Earliest known staging: Brno, Mahen Theatre, 30 December 1938, a shortened version choreographed by Ivo Váňa Psota, who danced Romeo, with Zora Šemberová as Juliet [documented: BSO; culture.ru]. Full-length Soviet premiere: Kirov Theatre, Leningrad, 11 January 1940; choreography Leonid Lavrovsky; libretto Adrian Piotrovsky, Sergei Prokofiev, Sergei Radlov and Lavrovsky; sets and costumes Pyotr Williams; conductor Isai Sherman [documented: Mariinsky repertoire page and exhibition "Prokofiev at the Mariinsky"]. Three acts (in the 1940 form), about 3 hours 20 minutes with two intervals [documented: Mariinsky]. The version most often seen in the West is Kenneth MacMillan's (Royal Ballet, 9 February 1965, designs Nicholas Georgiadis) [documented: kennethmacmillan.com; Nureyev Foundation]. Bachtrack's 2023 count puts Prokofiev's *Romeo* fourth among ballets performed worldwide [documented: CENSUS §1.1].

### In one line
Shakespeare's feud told by an orchestra and bodies alone, a score once called undanceable that became the 20th century's most re-choreographed full-length ballet.

### How it came to be
In 1934 Prokofiev, then still based in Paris, was talking to Leningrad about his operas. Adrian Piotrovsky, director of the Lenfilm studio and adviser to the theatre, suggested Shakespeare's tragedy; by May 1935 it had become a ballet plan [documented: Mariinsky]. The second "godfather" was Sergei Radlov, a Shakespeare director, who proposed "radical re-workings", the wildest a happy ending [documented: Mariinsky]. Friar Laurence would stop Romeo in time [documented: Borrowers and Lenders essay, 2020]. Rostislav Zakharov briefly worked on the scenario too [documented: Bolshoi 2024 page]. After Kirov's murder in December 1934 Radlov lost his post, the Leningrad contract fell through, and the Bolshoi took the commission [documented: BSO]. Prokofiev wrote the score in less than five months [documented: BSO].

On 4 October 1935 Prokofiev played it on the piano at a Bolshoi audition in the Beethoven Hall: the room was full at the start and "almost empty by the end" [documented: Bolshoi 2024 page]. Dancers and musicians called the music "non-ballet" [documented: Bolshoi]. The happy ending was dropped; the Bolshoi abandoned the production. *Pravda*'s attacks on Shostakovich in early 1936 ("Muddle instead of music", "Ballet falsehood") made every theatre timid [documented: culture.ru]. Prokofiev made two orchestral suites (1936–37), which reached the public first [documented: Britannica]. Brno staged it in 1938; Prokofiev did not attend [documented as a claim: Tomsk Philharmonic, 2026]. The Kirov signed a new contract and Lavrovsky staged it in 1940. Piotrovsky did not see it: he was arrested in 1937 and shot [probable, working knowledge; not re-checked].

### Roots and reference points
- **Shakespeare**, through Radlov's 1930s Shakespeare productions, which read the play as young people against feudal marriage [documented: Radlov quoted by Mark Morris Dance Group: "a struggle for the right to love by young, strong, and progressive people"].
- **Prokofiev's own theatre.** His earlier ballets for Diaghilev and his film work; leitmotifs for each character; the Dance of the Knights (Montagues and Capulets) [probable].
- **Why the happy ending.** Prokofiev: "Living people can dance, but the dead cannot dance lying down" [documented: quoted by BSO]. Simon Morrison adds his Christian Science faith: he "wanted his music to look toward the light" [documented: Morrison 2009 via Borrowers and Lenders]. A third reading: Socialist Realism's demand for optimism [documented as a hypothesis: BSO].
- **Lavrovsky's research.** He "made a meticulous study of Veronese archives, medieval novels and descriptions of early dances" [documented: Mariinsky].

### The world it built
Lavrovsky's ballet is the drambalet at its fullest: mimed scenes, crowds of Veronese citizens, sword fights, and few "pure" dances. Its signature passages: the Capulet ball with the heavy Dance of the Knights; the balcony duet; Mercutio's death; Lady Capulet's mourning over Tybalt; Juliet's run to Friar Laurence, cloak flying; and the final reconciliation of the fathers over the bodies [documented: Mariinsky synopsis; Arts Desk]. The Mariinsky writes that Ulanova's arabesque "became a symbol of understatement in an era of silence and subtext", her run "a desperate flight for freedom" [documented].

Composer and choreographer fought. Lavrovsky demanded more dances, solo numbers, a thicker orchestration and even an unrelated Prokofiev piece as an extra folk dance; Prokofiev resisted, complied, and after the premiere "had a hard time recognizing his own music" [documented: Borrowers and Lenders citing Wilson 2003; Mark Morris Dance Group]. Two weeks before the premiere the orchestra passed a written resolution refusing to play it "to avoid a flop" [documented: Mariinsky exhibition].

### Design and art
Williams (1902–1947), a painter of the OST group, built a Renaissance Italy that "arbitrarily combined heterogeneous elements": the dome of Florence cathedral rising over Verona's squares, costumes with traces of Botticelli and Lucas Cranach the Elder [documented: Mariinsky]. Critics found it "restrained and not remotely southern" [documented: Mariinsky]. Georgiadis's 1965 London designs, by contrast, are dark, heavy and architectural, with deep reds and browns, and the market crowd is dirty and real, in the spirit of Franco Zeffirelli's 1960 Old Vic staging [probable; Zeffirelli influence documented by Arts Desk]. Preljocaj's 1990 version was designed by the comic-book artist Enki Bilal: a dilapidated Verona of walls, militia and searchlights [documented: Preljocaj site].

### Premiere and reception
Kirov 1940: Ulanova (Juliet), Konstantin Sergeyev (Romeo) [documented: Mariinsky photo caption; Nureyev Foundation text]. Ulanova is said to have toasted: "Never was a story of more woe than this of Prokofiev's music for Romeo" [probable: widely quoted; no primary source seen]. The production moved to the Bolshoi in 1946 [probable]. On the Bolshoi's 1956 London tour *Romeo* was "a smash hit" [documented: Arts Desk]. MacMillan's 1965 premiere had 43 curtain calls and the safety curtain had to come down to make the audience leave [documented: kennethmacmillan.com, the estate's figure].

### Versions and lineage
1. Psota, Brno, 1938 (one act or shortened) [documented].
2. Lavrovsky, Kirov 1940; Bolshoi 1946; film 1954 (Lev Arnshtam and Lavrovsky) [film probable].
3. Ashton, Royal Danish Ballet, 19 May 1955, designs Peter Rice, made before he had seen Lavrovsky's [documented: Ashton Foundation].
4. Cranko, 1958 for the La Scala company in an open-air Venetian theatre (Carla Fracci), then Stuttgart Ballet, 2 December 1962 (Marcia Haydée) [documented: Arts Desk; Stuttgart date probable]. It borrowed Lavrovsky's Lady Capulet mourning scene [documented: Arts Desk].
5. MacMillan, Royal Ballet, 1965. Made on Lynn Seymour and Christopher Gable (the balcony duet first for Canadian television in 1964), but the impresario Sol Hurok insisted on Fonteyn and Nureyev for the US tour [documented: kennethmacmillan.com; ROH]. Ends on the two bodies, with no reconciliation [documented].
6. Nureyev, London Festival Ballet, 2 June 1977 (Patricia Ruanne); then La Scala 1980 and Paris Opéra 19 October 1984, the reference version in France [documented: Nureyev Foundation]. The Arts Desk dates it 1976 [contested].
7. Preljocaj, Lyon Opera Ballet, 27 December 1990; Ballet Preljocaj 1996: a ruling class (Juliet's) against an exploited population (Romeo's) [documented: Preljocaj site].
8. Later: Mark Morris, *Romeo & Juliet, On Motifs of Shakespeare* (2008), the first staging of Prokofiev's 1935 happy-ending score from Morrison's archive research [documented: MMDG]; many others (Grigorovich, Neumeier, Maillot, Ratmansky, Bourne) not checked in this pass.
No single notation of Lavrovsky's is known to us; the 1954 film is the main record [probable].

### Readings and disputes
- **Restored intention or a curiosity?** MMDG calls the ballet one that "has never been performed as the composer intended" and its 2008 work an attempt "to correct a historic wrong" [documented]. The Borrowers and Lenders essay (2020) instead weighs the musico-dramatic "tensions" created by switching between happy and tragic endings [documented].
- **Who is the author?** Four names on the Kirov libretto; Arlene Croce called the result "a dramaturgical nightmare" [documented: quoted by BSO].
- **Sex and class.** MacMillan's Juliet is the girl who becomes a woman and defies her parents [documented: Nureyev Foundation text]; Nureyev's stresses "social sex and violence", perhaps from his Soviet memories [documented as a reading: Arts Desk]; Preljocaj makes it class war under a militia [documented]. T24 in three states.
- **Myths against the record.** The Royal Opera House says the first ballet adaptation dates "to 1935 at the Kirov"; the record shows no 1935 staging [documented contradiction]. The Mariinsky's English page gives the premiere as "11 January 1938", its Russian page 1940 [documented typo]. "World premiere" is usually Brno, but Brno used only part of the score [documented: Mariinsky].

### Facts worth telling
1. The Bolshoi audition hall emptied while Prokofiev played [documented: Bolshoi].
2. The original ending was happy [documented].
3. The Kirov orchestra voted in writing not to play it [documented: Mariinsky].
4. Its earliest known staging was in Czechoslovakia, not the USSR [documented].
5. 43 curtain calls for Fonteyn and Nureyev, 1965 [documented: MacMillan estate].
6. The lovers MacMillan made it for danced only the second night [documented].
7. Williams put Florence's dome in Verona [documented: Mariinsky].

### Set it beside
- *giselle-1841*: the archetype of the girl destroyed; Juliet chooses her death (T15).
- *onegin-1965* (Cranko): the same choreographer's next literary ballet, the Stuttgart model of the narrative ballet.
- *maiden-tower-1940*: the same year, Baku: a girl dies rather than marry the man her father chose (T24).
- *west-side-story* is not a ballet in the register; Robbins's *Fancy Free* (`fancy-free-1944`) is the nearest American root.

### What a maker can take from it
- A world that fails in one form can survive in another: the suites kept the score alive for four years.
- Every new reading of the same score changed the society around the lovers (feudal Verona, street life, class war). The setting is the variable; the love is the constant.
- Make the work for the people who will carry it, and fight for them: MacMillan's Juliet was Seymour's even when Fonteyn danced it.

### Beyond the stage
The Dance of the Knights is one of the best-known orchestral pieces of the century: used as the theme of the BBC's *The Apprentice* and in many adverts and films [probable]. Paul Czinner's 1966 film of the MacMillan version with Fonteyn and Nureyev [documented: kennethmacmillan.com].

## Qız qalası / The Maiden Tower (1940)
`id: maiden-tower-1940` · threads: T14, T24, T15, T05 · confidence of the header: contested (choreographic authorship)

**Header.** Premiere 18 April 1940, Azerbaijan State Theatre of Opera and Ballet (named for M. F. Akhundov), Baku [documented: AZERTAC; ru.wikipedia]. Music and libretto Afrasiyab Badalbeyli (1907–1976), who also conducted [documented: ru.wikipedia]. Dances credited to Sergei Kevorkov and Vakhtang Vronsky [documented: Soviet *Ballet Encyclopedia* entry "Azerbaijani ballet", via dancelib.ru]. Designer F. Gusak [documented: ru.wikipedia text; gallery-mt.narod.ru]; the ru.wikipedia infobox instead names "E. G. Almaszade" [contested]. Three acts with prologue and epilogue. Lead: Gamar Almaszade (Gulyanag). The version seen in Baku today descends from the 1999 revision (libretto changes, score revised by Farhad Badalbeyli) [documented: Wikipedia; AzerNews].

### In one line
Baku's own landmark legend, of a princess who leaps from a tower rather than marry the khan, made into the earliest known ballet of a Muslim-majority Soviet republic.

### How it came to be
By the late 1930s Baku had a professional ballet company that danced *Swan Lake* (1923), *The Red Poppy* (1928, 1936), *Don Quixote* and *Le Corsaire* [documented: *Ballet Encyclopedia*]. What it lacked was a ballet of its own. Moscow's system of "decades" (dekady), festivals of each republic's art in Moscow, pushed every republic to show a national opera and ballet [probable, working knowledge; see Scholl]. Uzeyir Hajibeyov had already done this for opera with *Koroğlu* (1937).

Badalbeyli was a composer, conductor and musicologist; his wife Gamar Almaszade (1915–2006), trained in Leningrad, was the earliest known professional Azerbaijani ballerina [documented: AZERTAC; AzerNews]. She later wrote that she "worked out the choreography" and that the couple "worked on the ballet at home between 1938 and 1940" [documented: Almaszade in *Azerbaijan International*, quoted by Scholl 2006]. AzerNews says Badalbeyli composed it "specifically for Gamar" [documented]. The theatre's artistic director from 1938, the drama director Ismail Hidayatzade, is named as *postanovshchik* (stager), responsible for "national details" and the "contrasting pompousness of the shah's court" [documented: Shikhlinskaya's history, quoted by Scholl]. An attempt at a national ballet had failed earlier at the same theatre; the institution was blamed [documented as a claim: Scholl citing Shikhlinskaya].

### Roots and reference points
- **The tower.** Qız qalası, in Baku's walled Old City (İçərişəhər), dates from at least the 12th century [documented: AZERTAC]. Many legends explain the name; in the one used here, a khan desires his own daughter, she asks for a tower to gain time, and leaps from it [documented: Scholl].
- **Literature and film.** Jafar Jabbarli's narrative poem (1922 per Scholl) and a film scenario of 1923; "the main narrative contours of the ballet closely follow those of the film" [documented: Scholl]. AZERTAC names Jabbarli's poem as the source [documented].
- **Music.** Badalbeyli used real Azerbaijani melodies: the lyric song "Ay, bari bax" (Act I Dance of the Girls), the dance tunes *Tərəkəmə* and *Kikijan*, and the mugham *rəng* "Bayatı-Şiraz" for Gulyanag's Act III dance; also Armenian, Uzbek and Persian melodies for guest dances [documented: ru.wikipedia, citing a Soviet source]. Mugham, the modal art music of Azerbaijan, meets the classical orchestra [documented: AZERTAC].
- **Petipa's frame.** A "purely classical" dream scene in Act II "exactly where Petipa might have placed it", national dances in the wedding act [documented: Scholl].

### The world it built
Plot (1940): Khan Jahangir, wanting a son, orders his newborn daughter killed; the nurse Aypari saves her. Gulyanag grows up and loves Polad. The khan, not knowing who she is, desires her. Aypari tells him the truth; he is unmoved. Polad is imprisoned; Gulyanag agrees to marry the khan if he frees Polad, and asks for a tall tower by the sea to delay the wedding. Polad, a slave on the building site, escapes. At the wedding Aypari denounces the khan before the guests; he stabs her. Polad kills the khan in a fight and runs up the tower; Gulyanag, hearing steps and thinking it is the khan, throws herself into the sea [documented: azerhistory.com, O. Bulanova].

Movement: "ritual dances performed on pointe" and "folk hand gestures grafted onto the classical port de bras" [documented: Scholl]. The folk-dance expert I. Arbatov advised [documented: Scholl]. Guest divertissements: Georgian, Armenian, Persian, Uzbek [documented: Scholl; photo captions, azerhistory]. Signature scenes: the building of the tower; the dungeon; the duel of Polad and the khan; the leap [documented: photo captions].

### Design and art
1940 photographs show a painted Old City, arched palace interiors, the tower as a cylinder rising at the side of the stage, and long silk dresses, veils and embroidered caps for the women [probable, from the azerhistory.com photo series]. Scholl calls the result a "postcard depiction" [documented]. The tower is both set and plot: the building scene puts the monument's making on stage [argued]. Designer credit is contested (F. Gusak against E. G. Almaszade).

### Premiere and reception
Gulyanag: Almaszade (then T. Aliyeva); Khan: A. S. Urvantsev; Polad: Konstantin Batashev; Aypari: M. Babayeva [documented: ru.wikipedia]. The composer Soltan Hajibeyov later wrote that it offered "an organic synthesis of folk dance with classical", a "progressive start" for national choreography [documented: quoted by AzerNews, 2017]. Reviews of 1940 not found in this pass. The ballet was revived in 1948 (new choreography by Almaszade) and 1958 (Leyla Vakilova) [documented: ru.wikipedia], so it held the stage for decades.

### Versions and lineage
1940 (Kevorkov, Vronsky; Almaszade by her own account); 1948 Almaszade; 1958; 24 October 1999 new version by Yulana Alikishizade with Medina Aliyeva and Gulagasi Mirzoyev, score revised by Farhad Badalbeyli, the composer's son (Wikipedia) or nephew (Scholl) [contested], with the State Song and Dance Ensemble [documented: Wikipedia; AzerNews]. Performed in April 2017 for the composer's 110th anniversary [documented: AzerNews]. No notation known to us; photographs from 1940, 1959, 1964 and 1966 are published by azerhistory.com [documented].

### Readings and disputes
- **What was "Soviet propaganda"?** *Azerbaijan International* (1999) said the new version "rids Soviet propaganda" [documented: headline]. Scholl points out that the propaganda, on inspection, turns out to be the incest at the heart of the old plot; in 1999 the love object became no relation to the khan [documented: Scholl 2006]. Wikipedia instead says the revision gave a "smart leading female character" who asks for the tower to gain time [documented]. But the 1940 libretto already had Gulyanag ask for the tower [documented: Bulanova]. The record contradicts the Wikipedia summary [argued].
- **Who choreographed it?** The encyclopedia credits Kevorkov and Vronsky; Almaszade credits herself; a current programme credits her as producer; Hidayatzade is called *postanovshchik*. Scholl concludes it "most likely ... involved all four" and that collective authorship "became an important model" [documented]. Scholl also reports that a Moscow historian doubted the authorship of Shikhlinskaya's history itself [documented].
- **First, or earliest known?** Azerbaijani sources call it the "first ballet of the Muslim East" [documented: AZERTAC; *Great Soviet Encyclopedia*]. Scholl notes that Georgia's *Heart of the Hills* (Chabukiani, Balanchivadze) premiered in Tbilisi the year before Almaszade's first attempt [documented]; Georgia is not Muslim-majority, so the claim survives in its narrow form. We write "earliest known" [rule].
- **Inside or outside view?** Compare Scholl's reading of Zarema in *Fountain* as "an archetypal Oriental" whose movement is "bestial" with this ballet, made in Baku largely by Baku artists [documented: Scholl; argued].

### Facts worth telling
1. Composer and ballerina were husband and wife, and worked on it at home [documented: Almaszade via Scholl].
2. Almaszade was 25 at the premiere [documented: AZERTAC].
3. A mugham mode carries the heroine's last dance [documented: ru.wikipedia].
4. The heroine asks the khan to build the very tower Bakuvians walk past today [documented].
5. The 1999 rewrite removed the father-daughter incest [documented: Scholl].
6. Four people could claim the choreography [documented: Scholl].

### Set it beside
- *fountain-bakhchisarai-1934*: Pushkin's Russian dream of a khan and a harem, against Baku's own legend of a khan (T05).
- *la-coronela-1940*: the same year in Mexico City, another earliest-known national ballet (T14).
- *romeo-juliet-lavrovsky-1940*: the same year, the same death rather than a forced marriage (T24).
- *raise-the-red-lantern-2001*: a woman trapped by a patriarch's household, in Beijing.

### What a maker can take from it
- A real landmark gives a world an anchor the audience already owns.
- Credit lists simplify; in a collective world, record every hand.
- When a story is rewritten for new politics, check what was actually changed: labels like "propaganda" can hide quite different edits.

### Beyond the stage
The tower is a UNESCO World Heritage site with the Walled City and Shirvanshahs' Palace [probable]. Recordings of "Dance of the Azerbaijani girls" circulate on YouTube [documented: Wikipedia references].

## Гаянэ / Gayane (1942)
`id: gayane-1942` · threads: T08, T14, T24 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 9 December 1942, Kirov Theatre, evacuated to Molotov (now Perm), on the stage of the Perm theatre [documented: *Balet. Entsiklopediia* (1981), via pro-ballet.ru and dancelib.ru]. Wikipedia's article on the ballet gives both 9 December and "3 December 1942" [documented contradiction]; another source says 1941 [documented error: classicalmusicnews.ru]. Choreography Nina Anisimova; music Aram Khachaturian, partly reusing his ballet *Happiness* (1939); scenario Konstantin Derzhavin, Anisimova's husband; sets Nathan Altman, costumes Tatyana Bruni; conductor Pavel Feldt [documented: encyclopedia]. Four acts. The version most often seen in Armenia today is Vilen Galstyan's (Yerevan, 1974), stripped of politics; elsewhere the ballet is mostly known through the "Sabre Dance" and the suites [documented: classicalmusicnews.ru 2018; Sofia Philharmonic].

### In one line
A farm girl on the Soviet Armenian border unmasks a saboteur at the height of the war, and the whole Union dances at her wedding: the national ballet as a show of the "friendship of peoples".

### How it came to be
The root is political patronage. Anastas Mikoyan, the Armenian Politburo member, asked Khachaturian in a friendly talk for a ballet for the coming "Ten Days of Armenian Art" in Moscow; the theme was born during that talk [documented: Khachaturian Virtual Museum]. *Happiness* (libretto Gevorg Hovhannisyan) was played by the Yerevan Spendiarov Theatre at the Bolshoi on 24 October 1939, the earliest national ballet shown at the prewar "Days of Art" [documented: Khachaturian Museum]. Khachaturian judged its libretto weak [documented].

The Kirov then offered to stage it with a new libretto. Derzhavin wrote one about collective farmers, shepherds, a saboteur arsonist, geologists and border guards, with a festive finale of guests from the "fraternal republics" [documented: *Muzykal'naia zhizn'*, 2018]. Khachaturian "disintegrated" his own score and rebuilt it [documented: Khachaturian Museum]. The war intervened. The Kirov was evacuated to Molotov in 1941; Khachaturian lived on the fifth floor of the hotel "Central" and wrote "about 700 pages" in half a year "in a cold small room" with a piano, a stool, a table and a bed [documented: Khachaturian, quoted by the Museum]. The premiere came during the Battle of Stalingrad [documented: Museum].

### Roots and reference points
- **Armenian folk music** and the ashugh song tradition, transformed rather than quoted; dances named for Armenian forms (Shalakho, Uzundara) and for neighbours (Lezginka, the Kurdish dance) [documented: Wikipedia "Sabre Dance"].
- **The spy plot of the 1930s**: the "wrecker" or saboteur of the Stalin-era press [argued]; the border-guard hero Kazakov, a Russian [documented: Wikipedia].
- **Petipa's last act**: the wedding with a parade of national dances, as in *Swan Lake* or *Raymonda*, reused as the Union's family photograph [argued].
- **Earlier Soviet works it answers**: *The Flames of Paris* (folk dance equal to classical, W2 §10b); *Laurencia* (Chabukiani, 1939), another village heroine [argued].

### The world it built
The 1942 story, as summarised from the Sabre Dance article: on a kolkhoz (collective farm), Gayane's husband Giko joins smugglers and sets fire to the farm; he nearly kills Gayane and their daughter; Kazakov, the border commander, rescues them and later marries her [documented: Wikipedia]. *Muzykal'naia zhizn'* adds that three couples were joined at the end, two of them across nations (Armenian Gayane and Russian Kazakov; Kurdish Aisha and Armenian Armen) [documented]. The plot was rewritten so often that sources disagree about who is whose brother, husband or lover [documented: same].

Movement: Anisimova was a character dancer and "thought in character dancing terms" while knowing classical dance [documented: Wikipedia]. The ballet's big numbers are mass folk dances made theatrical: the cotton harvest, the dance of the carpet-weavers, the Lezginka, the fire, and the "Sabre Dance", first called "Dance of the Kurds" [documented: Wikipedia; Sofia Philharmonic].

The Sabre Dance was written to order. After the score was finished the theatre director asked for one more fast, warlike dance in the last act. Khachaturian began at 3 p.m. with an ostinato chord, linked it to a lyrical saxophone theme from Act III, and finished at 2 a.m.; it was rehearsed at 11 the next morning and staged by evening [documented: Khachaturian, quoted by the Museum]. He wanted to end it with a diminuendo; Anisimova and the dancers persuaded him to end loud [documented: Wikipedia "Sabre Dance"].

### Design and art
Nathan Altman (1889–1970) was a Cubo-Futurist painter, famous for his 1915 portrait of Anna Akhmatova and for decorating Palace Square for the first anniversary of the Revolution [probable, working knowledge]. For *Gayane* he painted mountain landscape and farm architecture; Tatyana Bruni designed the costumes [documented: encyclopedia]. Later Kirov versions were redesigned by Vadim Ryndin (1945) and Valery Dorrer (1952) [documented: encyclopedia]. Visual details of the 1942 décor not found in this pass.

### Premiere and reception
Natalia Dudinskaya (Gayane), Konstantin Sergeyev (Armen), Tatyana Vecheslova (Nune), Nikolai Zubkovsky (Karen), Boris Shavrov (Giko); Anisimova danced a character role [documented: encyclopedia; Wikipedia]. The premiere was broadcast on radio [documented: Wikipedia]. Dmitry Kabalevsky praised Feldt's "inspired ardor" in the pit [documented: quoted by the Museum]. The suites, conducted in Moscow on 3 October 1943, produced, Khachaturian said, a burst of applause he never heard again [documented: Museum]. Khachaturian received a Stalin Prize [documented: Museum]. Wikipedia reports it was danced before Stalin with "modest" success, flagged "citation needed" [documented as unsupported].

### Versions and lineage
The encyclopedia lists: 1945 Kirov revival; 13 June 1952 Kirov new production (the version Wikipedia calls definitive); 22 May 1957 Bolshoi, new scenario by Boris Pletnev, choreography Vasily Vainonen, director Emmanuil Kaplan, in which Khachaturian reworked "almost a third" of the music [documented: encyclopedia; *Muzykal'naia zhizn'*]; 9 December 1972 Leningrad Maly, Boris Eifman's diploma work, a kulak's son Giko and no spies [documented: encyclopedia; Armenian Museum of Moscow]; 1972 Chichinadze one-act suite; 1977 Kasatkina and Vasilyov; Yerevan 1947 (Anisimova), 1971 (Martirosyan), 1974 (Galstyan) [documented]. Abroad: many East German stagings (Leipzig 1948 onwards), Czechoslovakia, Bulgaria, West Germany (Wiesbaden 1972), Poland [documented: encyclopedia]. In 2018 the Yerevan company brought Galstyan's version to the Bolshoi stage after a 57-year absence of the ballet there [documented: classicalmusicnews.ru]. Khachaturian said it was "the only ballet on the Soviet theme" to stay on stage for a quarter of a century [documented: Museum].

### Readings and disputes
- **A wartime morale piece.** The Wikipedia account stresses camaraderie: dancers who happened to be in Perm joined, and the message was that the company "was continuing to exist" [documented, unsigned]. The Sofia Philharmonic note frames the finale as Khachaturian's idea of "rapprochement and convergence of people" [documented].
- **A ballet the plot could not save.** Every librettist changed the story "to suit the historical moment" and the composer re-cut the score each time [documented: *Muzykal'naia zhizn'*]. A 2024 article by I. Pavlova compares the 1952 and 1957 stagings as markers of the decade's shift from "drambalet" to "choreodrama" [documented: abstract, *Vestnik GGU* 2024].
- **Friendship of peoples, or hierarchy of peoples?** The Russian border guard rescues and marries the Armenian heroine; the Kurds dance with swords [argued, from documented plot]. Compare Searcy's reading of *Spartacus* (W3 §2), where the 1968 version removed the national display altogether.
- **Woman as choreographer.** Anisimova is one of very few women credited with a full-length Soviet ballet of the period [probable].

### Facts worth telling
1. The Sabre Dance took about eleven hours to write [documented: Khachaturian].
2. Its first name was "Dance of the Kurds" [documented].
3. In 1948 it became an American jukebox hit; Khachaturian was nicknamed "Mr Sabre Dance" in the US [documented: Wikipedia; *Muzykal'naia zhizn'*].
4. The score was written in a cold hotel room in the Urals during the war [documented].
5. The ballet began as a Politburo member's suggestion [documented: Khachaturian Museum].
6. The Adagio is in Kubrick's *2001: A Space Odyssey* (1968) [documented: Wikipedia].
7. The Buffalo Sabres hockey team uses the Sabre Dance [documented: Wikipedia].

### Set it beside
- *flames-of-paris-1932*: the collective and folk dance as hero, in a foreign revolution; here at home on a farm (T08).
- *red-detachment-1964* (W3 §1): the same heroine-joins-the-cause plot in Beijing (T24).
- *rodeo-1942*: the same year, a folk-based national ballet in the USA, with a farm girl and no politics (T14).
- *spartacus-1968* (W3 §2): Khachaturian again, and a version that strips out the parade of nations.

### What a maker can take from it
- A world's best-known piece can be the one added last, at someone else's request.
- If the story keeps changing and the music stays, the music is the real concept. Build the parts that can survive a rewrite.
- A finale that parades every group sends a message; ask who leads the parade.

### Beyond the stage
The Sabre Dance: Woody Herman and Oscar Levant (1948), Jascha Heifetz, Dave Edmunds's rock cover, figure skating, cartoons; a "global musical shorthand for cartoonish urgency" [documented: Wikipedia, quoting a source]. The Adagio in *2001* (1968) [documented].

## Золушка / Cinderella (1945, with Ashton's Cinderella, 1948)
`id: cinderella-zakharov-1945` (with Ashton 1948, no register row) · threads: T06, T07, T20, T13 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Score: Sergei Prokofiev, Op. 87, begun 1940, finished in piano score 1944 [documented: Musikproduktion Höflich preface; Belcanto.ru]. Libretto Nikolai Volkov after Charles Perrault (1697). Premiere 21 November 1945, Bolshoi Theatre, Moscow; choreography Rostislav Zakharov; designs Pyotr Williams; conductor Yuri Fayer; Cinderella Olga Lepeshinskaya, with Galina Ulanova in the second cast [documented: Belcanto.ru; Russkaya mysl', 2017]. Kirov version: Konstantin Sergeyev, 8 April 1946, designs Boris Erdman (later Tatyana Bruni), using all 50 numbers against Zakharov's 43 [documented: Mariinsky exhibition; Russkaya mysl']. Ashton's version: Sadler's Wells Ballet, Royal Opera House, 23 December 1948, designs Jean-Denis Malclès, with Moira Shearer, Michael Somes, and Ashton and Robert Helpmann as the Stepsisters [documented: Ashton Foundation]. Three acts. Ashton's is the version most often seen in Britain; Bachtrack's 2023 count ranks Prokofiev's *Cinderella* fifth worldwide [documented: CENSUS §1.1].

### In one line
A wartime fairy tale about time: a girl who must leave at midnight, set to a score with a ticking clock at its centre and dedicated to Tchaikovsky.

### How it came to be
The Kirov discussed a *Cinderella* from early 1940 and commissioned Volkov's libretto, at first for music by Tchaikovsky [documented: Mariinsky exhibition]. After *Romeo*, Prokofiev signed a contract for *Cinderella* in December 1940; two acts were nearly done by June 1941 [documented: Mariinsky]. He thought of it "above all as a Russian fairy tale" and proposed "a Russian Cinderella of Elizabethan times", because Afanasyev's Russian version of the tale belonged to the 18th century [documented: Prokofiev quoted by Mariinsky]. Vakhtang Chabukiani was to choreograph [documented].

Then the war. The Kirov went to Molotov (Perm); Prokofiev wrote the opera *War and Peace* in Tbilisi; Chabukiani stayed in Georgia and in early 1943 refused to come back to the project [documented: Mariinsky]. Kasyan Goleizovsky was proposed, then the dancers Ulanova and Sergeyev [documented]. Prokofiev visited Perm in 1943, discussed details with Volkov and finished the piano score in Moscow [documented: Belcanto.ru]. Ulanova had moved to the Bolshoi; the Bolshoi took up the score and premiered it first, "against the will of the composer", while the Kirov's Sergeyev followed five months later [documented: Russkaya mysl'].

In London, Ashton had wanted a full-evening ballet since the Vic-Wells *Sleeping Princess* of 1939 [documented: Wikipedia, Ashton]. In 1946 Ninette de Valois said publicly she could not wait to see "the first full-length English classical ballet"; *Sylvia* was considered; in late spring 1948 Prokofiev's *Cinderella* was chosen [documented: Wikipedia]. Ashton was "inspired ... by Russian adaptations" of the tale [documented: Ashton Foundation].

### Roots and reference points
- **Perrault**, *Cendrillon* (1697), and Afanasyev's Russian tale [documented].
- **Tchaikovsky and Petipa.** Prokofiev wrote it "in the traditions of the old classical ballet", with pas de deux, adagio, gavotte, waltzes, pavane, passepied, bourrée, mazurka and galop, and "every character has its variation" [documented: Prokofiev via Belcanto.ru]. He dedicated it to Tchaikovsky [documented: Wikipedia].
- **His own past.** The Stepsisters' dance with oranges reuses music from *The Love for Three Oranges*, an opera not performed in the USSR in the 1940s and 1950s [documented: Russkaya mysl'].
- **Earlier Cinderella ballets.** Fernando Sor's *Cendrillon* opened Moscow's Bolshoi on 6 June 1825 [documented: Belcanto.ru]; Petipa, Cecchetti and Ivanov's *Cinderella* (St Petersburg, 1893, music Fitinghof-Schell); Fokine's *Cendrillon* (London, 1938) [documented: Rochester Cinderella Bibliography].

### The world it built
Act I: the stepmother's house; the stepsisters quarrel over a scarf; the beggar woman who turns out to be the Fairy Godmother; the four Fairies of the Seasons; the clock. Act II: the ball, the dancing master, the oranges, the waltz, midnight. Act III: the prince's search (a divertissement of journeys through foreign lands in the Soviet version) and the slipper [documented: Belcanto.ru; Wikipedia]. The music has a grotesque, sardonic side for the stepsisters and a long-lined lyric side for the lovers; the twelve strokes of midnight in Act II are among Prokofiev's most famous theatre effects [probable].

Ashton changed the world's balance. He cut the prince's journey ("I didn't like any of the places he went to, nor the music he wrote for them") and a dance of grasshoppers and dragonflies [documented: Wikipedia]. He cast the stepsisters as men, the English pantomime dame, playing them himself (the shy sister) with Helpmann (the bossy one) [documented: Ashton Foundation; RBO]. The roles were made for women, and Ashton and Helpmann took them "only after the first-choice dancers took other engagements" [documented: RBO]. Ashton added "ingenious, geometric sequences for the corps", notably the stars at the end of Act I [documented: Ashton Foundation].

### Design and art
Williams (Moscow, 1945) made a sumptuous court fairy tale [probable]. Malclès (1912–2002), a French painter and designer, gave Ashton a light, graphic, French 18th-century look in pastel and gold [probable]. The Royal Ballet replaced his designs in 2023 with new ones by Tom Pye (sets) and Alexandra Byrne (costumes), Byrne a film costume designer [documented: RBO]. Sergeyev's Kirov designs were by Boris Erdman (1946) and Tatyana Bruni (1964) [documented: Mariinsky].

### Premiere and reception
Moscow 1945: Lepeshinskaya, then Ulanova, for whom Prokofiev had written the part [documented: Russkaya mysl']. The director Alexander Tairov said *Cinderella* was among works "which become classical immediately upon their appearance" [documented: Russkaya mysl']. The critic Sergei Gorodetsky praised Ulanova's "humble dreaming of another life" [documented: same]. The Kirov's version ran from 8 April 1946 to 14 April 1996, 204 performances [documented: Mariinsky exhibition]. Prokofiev and Zakharov's team received Stalin Prizes in 1946 [probable].

London 1948: Shearer (who had just filmed *The Red Shoes*, released 1948) and Somes [documented for cast]. Fonteyn, the intended first Cinderella, was injured [probable]. The Sadler's Wells Ballet took it to the Metropolitan Opera, New York, on 18 October 1949, with Fonteyn [documented: Rochester]. NBC broadcast a 1957 television version with Fonteyn, Somes, Ashton and Kenneth MacMillan as the Stepsisters [documented: Ashton Foundation; Rochester].

### Versions and lineage
Zakharov 1945 (Bolshoi; film *The Crystal Slipper*, 1960, dir. Alexander Rou with Zakharov) [documented: Rochester; film title probable]; Sergeyev 1946 (Kirov; at the Met 1964) [documented]; Ashton 1948 (Royal Ballet ever since; revised 1965) [documented]; Alfred Rodrigues, La Scala 1955–56 [documented: Rochester]. Later versions widely staged include Nureyev (Paris, 1986, set in Hollywood), Matthew Bourne (1997, the London Blitz), Jean-Christophe Maillot (Monte Carlo, 1999), Alexei Ratmansky (Mariinsky, 2002), Christopher Wheeldon (2012) [probable; not re-checked this pass]. ABT took Ashton's in 2014 [documented: ABT].

### Readings and disputes
- **Earliest full-length British ballet?** Ashton Foundation says it was Ashton's "first full-length work"; Wikipedia relays de Valois's wish for "the first full-length English classical ballet". The register's claim (earliest known British full-length ballet) is probable; earlier British full-evening works existed in music hall (the Empire's *Cinderella*, 1906) [documented: Rochester], so the claim holds only for "classical" ballet companies [argued].
- **A Soviet fairy tale or an escape?** Prokofiev wanted Cinderella as "a real person, feeling, experiencing, and moving among us" [documented: Wikipedia]. A reading: in 1945 a story about a ragged girl rewarded by a just order fitted Socialist Realism's optimism [argued]. Another: the score's dark, broken waltzes and the menacing clock speak of the war years in which it was written [argued; often said by programme writers, source not pinned this pass].
- **Comedy against lyricism.** Ashton's pantomime sisters made the British version a family comedy; critics have long argued whether the drag sisters unbalance Prokofiev's darker score [probable].
- **Myth in an official text.** The Royal Ballet's own page says the "Russian original" was choreographed "for the Kirov Ballet by Marius Petipa, Enrico Cecchetti, and Lev Ivanov ... in 1940" [documented]. Petipa died in 1910; their *Cinderella* was 1893, to other music [documented: Rochester]. The sentence fuses two works.

### Facts worth telling
1. The Kirov first planned *Cinderella* to Tchaikovsky's music [documented: Mariinsky].
2. Prokofiev wanted a Russian Cinderella of Empress Elizabeth's time [documented].
3. The orange dance is a quotation from his banned opera [documented: Russkaya mysl'].
4. Ulanova, for whom it was written, danced the second night in Moscow [documented].
5. Ashton played a stepsister for years, opposite Helpmann, and later opposite MacMillan [documented].
6. Ashton cut the prince's travels because he "didn't like any of the places he went to" [documented].
7. Sergeyev's version stayed in the Kirov repertoire for exactly fifty years [documented: Mariinsky].

### Set it beside
- *sleeping-beauty-1890*: the model Prokofiev and Ashton both followed (fairies, variations, a court) (T06).
- *fille-mal-gardee-ashton-1960*: Ashton's next comic dame, Widow Simone, from the same pantomime root.
- *nutcracker-1892*: the other Christmas fairy tale; London premiered *Cinderella* on 23 December.
- *romeo-juliet-lavrovsky-1940*: the same composer, the same lead dancer (Ulanova), the opposite ending.

### What a maker can take from it
- A world can have two tones at once (grotesque and lyrical) if each has its own characters.
- One device can organise a whole world: the clock is plot, music and design at the same time.
- Re-cut what does not serve your version: Ashton removed a whole travel act and the work held.

### Beyond the stage
The NBC 1957 broadcast [documented]; the 1960 Soviet film [documented: Rochester]; the suites, Op. 107–109, and the piano transcriptions [documented: Rochester; Belcanto.ru].

## Шүрәле / Shurale (1950)
`id: shurale-1950` · threads: T03, T14, T01, T06 · confidence of the header: documented (Kazan choreography contested)

**Header.** Music Farid Yarullin (1913–1943). First staging: Tatar State Opera and Ballet Theatre, Kazan, 12 March 1945, orchestrated after the composer's death by Fabiy Vitachek; choreography Leonid Zhukov and Gai Tagirov; designer Pyotr Speransky; conductor Ilyas Aukhadeev [documented: ru.wikipedia; Belcanto.ru; KFU paper by V. R. Dulat-Aleev]. The Znanie.Wiki encyclopedia instead credits the 1945 choreography to Faizi Gaskarov [contested]. Second version, under the title *Ali-Batyr*: Kirov Theatre, Leningrad, 28 May 1950; choreography Leonid Yakobson; libretto Ahmed Faizi and Yakobson after Tatar tales and Ğabdulla Tuqay's poem *Şüräle* (1907); new orchestration Vladimir Vlasov and Vladimir Fere; designs Alexander Ptushko, Lev Milchin and Ivan Ivanov-Vano; conductor Pavel Feldt [documented: Mariinsky; Belcanto.ru]. Three acts, four scenes, 2 hours 45 minutes. The version seen today is Yakobson's, revived at the Mariinsky on 28 June 2009 [documented: Mariinsky].

### In one line
A Tatar hunter frees a bird-maiden from the long-fingered forest demon Shurale: the swan-maiden tale of *Swan Lake* told from a Turkic, Muslim-majority republic, with a happy ending.

### How it came to be
In 1934 the Soviet government decided to found an opera and ballet theatre in Kazan, and a Tatar Opera Studio was set up at the Moscow Conservatory to train composers for it [documented: Mariinsky]. In 1939 the student Farid Yarullin was set an exercise by his teacher Genrikh Litinsky: ballet scenes on Tuqay's Shurale tales. They were so good that Litinsky asked the writer Ahmed Faizi for a libretto [documented: Belcanto.ru]. The ballet was planned for the "Decade of Tatar Art" in Moscow, set for August 1941 [documented: Mariinsky; business-gazeta.ru].

Leonid Yakobson was sent to Kazan as production choreographer. He "rewove" Faizi's material, dropped side plots and sharpened the main conflict, which meant Yarullin had to rewrite fast; "to this day there are stories" that he was locked in a room in the Soviet Hotel and escaped down a drainpipe [documented: Mariinsky, as legend]. The piano score was done by June 1941. Then war. Yarullin was mobilised and died at the front in October 1943 [documented: Mariinsky]. Kazan staged the ballet in March 1945 with other choreography. In 1950 the Kirov invited Yakobson to finish what he had begun [documented: Mariinsky].

### Roots and reference points
- **Tuqay's *Şüräle* (1907)**, the classic poem by the founder of modern Tatar literature, in which a woodcutter traps the tickling forest spirit by the fingers in a split log [probable, working knowledge]. Shurale: "an evil wood spirit in the guise of a man with a horn on his forehead and long fingers" [documented: Mariinsky].
- **The swan-maiden tale**, known from Ireland to Japan: a man steals a bird-woman's wings so she must stay [probable]. Its ballet ancestor is *Swan Lake* (T03).
- **Petipa's structure.** A white act of bird-maidens in the forest; a village wedding with national dances; a final battle with the demon [argued, from the Mariinsky synopsis].
- **Music.** Yarullin "lightly combines the national melos with the standards of late-Romantic ballet scores" [documented: business-gazeta.ru]. The pentatonic cast of Tatar folk song is audible throughout [probable].

### The world it built
Act I: the forest at night; Shurale emerges from a tree trunk; his court of genies, witches and devils dances; at dawn birds land, become maidens, dance and fly away, all but Syuimbike, whose wings Shurale has stolen; the hunter Ali-Batyr saves her and takes her home. Act II: the village wedding; the matchmakers hide the bride and the groom must find her; Syuimbike, sad for her wings, is lured back into the forest when Shurale's minions return them. Act III: Shurale's lair; Ali-Batyr sets it on fire; he offers Syuimbike her wings to escape; she throws them into the flames, and the fire dies [documented: Mariinsky synopsis].

The ending inverts *Swan Lake*: here the bird-woman burns her wings to stay with a mortal, and lives [argued]. Yakobson's movement language mixed academic pointe for the birds with grotesque, angular, crawling movement for Shurale and his demons; Shurale's long-fingered hands and twisted body became a star part (Robert Gerbek, Igor Belsky) [probable; casts documented by Belcanto.ru]. The wedding uses Tatar customs: the hidden bride, the matchmakers [documented].

### Design and art
The Kirov did not hire stage designers. It hired three film-makers of Soviet children's fantasy: Alexander Ptushko (live-action fairy films such as *The Stone Flower*, 1946), Ivan Ivanov-Vano (animation, *The Humpbacked Horse*, 1947) and Lev Milchin [documented: business-gazeta.ru, which calls it a sign of "the extraordinary nature of the production"]. The result is a painted storybook forest with gnarled trunks, a firelit lair and a carpeted Tatar village [probable, from Mariinsky photographs]. The 2009 revival reconstructed these designs with Batozhan Dashitsyrenov and the costume technologist Tatiana Mashkova [documented: Mariinsky]. The Kazan 1945 designer was Pyotr Speransky [documented: ru.wikipedia].

### Premiere and reception
Leningrad 1950: Syuimbike was danced by Natalia Dudinskaya, Alla Shelest and Inna Zubkovskaya; Ali-Batyr by Konstantin Sergeyev, Boris Bregvadze, Askold Makarov; Shurale by Robert Gerbek and Igor Belsky [documented: Belcanto.ru]. It ran 176 performances [documented: Belcanto.ru]. It won a Stalin Prize [documented: Znanie.Wiki]. The title was changed to *Ali-Batyr* because "it was thought improper to name a Soviet ballet after an evil spirit" [documented: Mariinsky]. At the Bolshoi (1955), Maya Plisetskaya and Yuri Kondratov led; a 1960 revival had Marina Kondratyeva and Vladimir Vasiliev [documented: Belcanto.ru].

### Versions and lineage
Kazan 1945 (Zhukov and Tagirov, or Gaskarov); Ufa 1950; Kirov 1950 (Yakobson); Bolshoi 1955 (Yakobson); Kazan 1952, 1955 (Leo Järvi), 1957, 1970 (Yakobson) and 2005 (Vladimir Mikhailov) [documented: ru.wikipedia; Znanie.Wiki, which disagree on Kazan dates]. Staged in Alma-Ata, Vilnius, Kiev, Riga, Tashkent and elsewhere, and in Bulgaria, East Germany, Poland, Romania and Czechoslovakia; in Mexico in 2002 by Renat Ibatullin [documented: Dulat-Aleev]. Mariinsky revival 2009 under Valery Gergiev [documented]. A Kazan historian stresses that the Kazan and Leningrad ballets are "absolutely two different performances" in their dances [documented: business-gazeta.ru, interview].

### Readings and disputes
- **The earliest Tatar national ballet.** Kazan tradition treats it as the centre of Tatar ballet history [documented: Znanie.Wiki]. It belongs to the wave of national ballets made for the Moscow "decades" (T14).
- **Whose discovery?** A Kazan critic argues that Yakobson's hero was "not the fairy-tale forest king Shurale, not ... even the Tatar people, but the exotic eastern country itself", seen by "an enlightened European" as its discoverer [documented: business-gazeta.ru, 2018]. Janice Ross treats Yakobson more broadly as a choreographer whose inventions resisted Soviet norms (*Like a Bomb Going Off*, Yale, 2015) [probable on Shurale's place in that book].
- **Two different scores.** Vlasov and Fere's re-orchestration made "orchestral tutti" that, the same critic writes, could illustrate "the uprising led by Spartacus" rather than a fight with a wood-goblin [documented: business-gazeta.ru]. What audiences outside Kazan know as Yarullin's music is partly theirs [argued].
- **Names and credits.** Heroine names differ by version: Syuimbike (Leningrad), Bibisa in a "canonical" Kazan version [documented: Znanie.Wiki]. An article by Sapanzha in the Vaganova Academy *Vestnik* (2022) traces the history of the characters' names, and the Kirov archive keeps a file on "copyright of the libretto, 1941–1955" [documented: article reference list]. Whether Faizi, Tagirov, Gaskarov or Yakobson wrote the libretto shifts by source [contested].

### Facts worth telling
1. The composer began it as a student exercise [documented: Belcanto.ru].
2. Yarullin was killed in the war and never heard his ballet orchestrated [documented].
3. Leningrad renamed it after the hero because a Soviet ballet could not be named after a demon [documented: Mariinsky].
4. Three film animators and fantasy directors designed it [documented].
5. The heroine burns her own wings [documented].
6. It ran 176 performances in Leningrad [documented: Belcanto.ru].
7. It reached Mexico in 2002 [documented: Dulat-Aleev].

### Set it beside
- *swan-lake-1877-1895*: the bird-woman who cannot stay human, against one who chooses to (T03).
- *fish-beauty-1959*: Beijing's spirit-woman ballet a decade later, made with Soviet help (T01).
- *firebird-1910*: a Russian forest demon (Kashchei) and a magic bird, made in Paris by Russians for Europeans (T05, T06).
- *maiden-tower-1940*: the other Muslim-majority republic's national ballet, a tragedy rather than a fairy tale.

### What a maker can take from it
- Borrowing a tale-type is fine; turning its ending is where your world becomes yours.
- Hire designers from another medium when you want a new look: here, children's film.
- Record who did what at the time. Eighty years later the credits are still being argued.

### Beyond the stage
Tuqay's Shurale is a Tatar national figure on stamps, statues and children's books; Kazan's opera house runs a Shurale tradition at its annual Nureyev Festival [probable].

- Belcanto.ru: https://www.belcanto.ru/ballet_shuraleh.html

## Каменный цветок / The Stone Flower (1957)
`id: stone-flower-1957` · threads: T19, T01, T14, T06 · confidence of the header: documented (exact 1957 date contested)

**Header.** Score: Sergei Prokofiev, *Skaz o kamennom tsvetke* (The Tale of the Stone Flower), Op. 118, his last ballet, worked on from July 1948 until the day he died, 5 March 1953 [documented: Petukhova 2023; Belcanto.ru]. Libretto Mira Mendelson-Prokofieva and Leonid Lavrovsky after Pavel Bazhov's Ural tales *The Malachite Box* [documented: Mariinsky]. First staging: Bolshoi, 12 February 1954, choreography Lavrovsky, designer Tamara Starzhenetskaya, conductor Yuri Fayer, four acts [documented: *Russkii balet* encyclopedia via pro-ballet.ru]. The version that lasted: Kirov Theatre, Leningrad, Yuri Grigorovich's three-act revision, designs Simon Virsaladze, conductor and music editor Yuri Gamaley, premiere 22 April 1957 per the Mariinsky, 25 April 1957 per both Soviet encyclopedias [contested]. Revivals at the Mariinsky 6 December 2016 (two acts) and 23 April 2026 (from the 1957 version) [documented: Mariinsky].

### In one line
A Ural stone-carver wants to make a malachite bowl as alive as a flower and is drawn into the mountain by its spirit-mistress: a ballet about the maker and the made, which became the opening move of the post-Stalin "thaw" in Soviet ballet.

### How it came to be
Prokofiev wrote the score for Lavrovsky, the choreographer of his *Romeo* [documented: Mariinsky exhibition]. He started in 1948, the year of the Party decree that denounced him, Shostakovich and Khachaturian as "formalists"; the ballet had to answer "Party criticism" with a Russian folk subject and a social backbone [documented: Belcanto.ru; Petukhova]. His wife Mira Mendelson wrote the first scenario; Lavrovsky joined as co-author [documented: Belcanto.ru]. Revisions dragged on; nearly five years passed between score and production [documented: Mariinsky]. Prokofiev was orchestrating the scene of Katerina and Danila's meeting on the day he died, the same day as Stalin [documented: Belcanto.ru].

The Bolshoi production of 1954 failed. It ran 17 performances, from 12 February 1954 to 28 April 1955 [documented: Petukhova 2023]. "Even Ulanova and Yermolaev did not save it" [documented: Mariinsky]. Three years later Yuri Grigorovich (1927–2025), a 30-year-old Kirov soloist who had staged small works in the Gorky Palace of Culture's amateur studio, made a new version, largely in his spare time, with young dancers [documented: musicseasons.org 2017; Mariinsky]. It was his first major production [documented: Mariinsky].

### Roots and reference points
- **Pavel Bazhov, *The Malachite Box* (1939)**, *skazy* (tales told in a storyteller's voice) of the Ural mining and stone-cutting workshops, built on miners' lore: Danila the master, the Mistress of the Copper Mountain who appears as a lizard or a woman in a malachite gown, the fire-sprite Ognevushka [documented title; details probable].
- **Film.** Alexander Ptushko's colour film *The Stone Flower* (1946) had already made the tale a national image [probable].
- **Romantic ballet's structure.** The Mariinsky's own essay maps the four principals onto *Giselle* and *La Sylphide*: Danila as Albrecht or James, the steward Severyan as Hilarion or Gurn, Katerina as Act I Giselle or Effie, the Mistress as the Sylph or Myrtha [documented: Mariinsky exhibition]. The ballet's two worlds (village and mountain) are Romanticism's two worlds (T01).
- **Fyodor Lopukhov's "dance symphony".** Grigorovich's turn to continuous, large-scale dance rather than mime is usually traced to Lopukhov's 1920s experiments [probable].

### The world it built
Grigorovich's three acts: Danila wants to carve a bowl "to embody in stone the grace of a living flower"; his betrothal to Katerina; the steward Severyan demands the commissioned bowl and paws Katerina; the Mistress shows Danila the stone flower inside the mountain and keeps him; Katerina searches, through a fair with gypsies and merchants, guided by Ognevushka; Severyan is swallowed by the earth; the Mistress, touched by Katerina's love, lets Danila go [documented: Mariinsky synopsis]. In the Belcanto summary, a green flower sways in the background at the end, "the symbol of the tireless search for beauty" [documented].

The movement is the point. Instead of drambalet mime, character is built in dance phrases: the Mistress's sharp, angular, lizard-like movement, with flexed hands and darting jumps; the corps of "semi-precious stones" dancing in geometric ensemble; a whole fair as a choreographed crowd [probable; "lizard" quality widely described]. The Mariinsky calls it "a step forwards from the undanceable nature of the genre of the drama ballet" [documented]. Grigorovich re-edited the score with Gamaley, cutting Lavrovsky's four acts to three [documented: encyclopedia].

### Design and art
Virsaladze (1908–1989), the Kirov's chief designer, was a Georgian painter [documented: musicseasons.org]. This ballet began the Grigorovich–Virsaladze partnership that lasted three decades (*Legend of Love*, *Spartacus*, *Ivan the Terrible*) [documented: Mariinsky]. His idea is famous: the stage is enclosed by the walls of a giant malachite box, and the scenes open inside it; for the mountain, the green banded patterns of malachite fill the space; the Mistress wears a close-fitting green costume patterned like the stone, with no tutu [probable, from widely published photographs]. The design is unified, almost abstract, and leaves the floor empty for dance [argued]. The 1954 Bolshoi version, by contrast, had "almost natural peasant huts" and "heavy national clothes" for a "domestic drama" [documented: Belcanto.ru], with a designer the Mariinsky calls a "meticulous naturalist" [documented]. The original 1957 décor did not survive; Mikhail Sapozhnikov rebuilt it from Virsaladze's sketches in 2016 [documented: musicseasons.org].

### Premiere and reception
1957: Irina Kolpakova (Katerina), Alexander Gribov (Danila), Alla Osipenko (Mistress of the Copper Mountain), Anatoly Gridin (Severyan); Grigorovich himself and Igor Belsky also danced Severyan [documented: encyclopedia; Mariinsky captions; musicseasons]. The Mariinsky says the young dancers "became the creators of roles for the first time rather than mere interpreters" [documented]. Over thirty years it was among the Kirov's best-attended productions [documented: Mariinsky, citing theatre statistics]. The Bolshoi took it in 1959 per the Mariinsky, or on 7 March 1961 per the 1981 encyclopedia [contested]. Grigorovich moved to the Bolshoi as chief choreographer in 1964 [documented: W3 §2].

1954 cast: Ulanova (Katerina), Vladimir Preobrazhensky (Danila), Alexei Yermolaev (Severyan); the Mistress is Maya Plisetskaya in the 1981 encyclopedia and N. N. Chkalova in the 1997 one [contested: the two were probably alternates].

### Versions and lineage
Lavrovsky 1954 (lost); Grigorovich 1957 Kirov, 1959 or 1961 Bolshoi; Kirov to the early 1990s; Mariinsky 2016 (two acts) and 2026; also in the repertoire of the Stanislavsky and Nemirovich-Danchenko theatre and Krasnoyarsk [documented: musicseasons.org]. The Bolshoi took it on its foreign tours [probable]. Grigorovich said of the 2016 edit that "time has changed" and he wanted to bring out "the theme of love and creativity" more brightly [documented: musicseasons.org].

### Readings and disputes
- **The thaw ballet.** The Mariinsky's own text sets the two stagings three years apart as proof of "how cardinally the times changed" [documented]. On this reading Grigorovich's symphonic dance broke the drambalet [argued, widely held].
- **Continuity, not break.** Petukhova (2023) argues from new archival sources that the creators saw the 1954 project as a "promising and significant step forward" and that its reputation as a "creative failure" formed partly because the 1957 version eclipsed it before a literature could form [documented: abstract].
- **The artist's ballet.** Belcanto says the stone-carver was the figure "closest in spirit" to Prokofiev [documented]. A reading: an artist torn between his commission (the steward's bowl), his community (Katerina) and the pure demands of the material (the Mistress) [argued; T19].
- **Credit.** A woman, Mendelson-Prokofieva, co-wrote the libretto; she is credited on Mariinsky posters to this day [documented].

### Facts worth telling
1. Prokofiev died on the same day as Stalin, still orchestrating this ballet [documented].
2. The first version ran only 17 performances [documented: Petukhova].
3. Grigorovich made his version largely after hours [documented: musicseasons].
4. The Mariinsky's own essay compares its cast to *Giselle* and *La Sylphide* [documented].
5. The whole stage was a malachite box [probable].
6. A woman co-wrote the scenario [documented].
7. It was the start of the Grigorovich–Virsaladze partnership [documented].

### Set it beside
- *giselle-1841*: the same quartet of roles, as the Mariinsky itself says (T01).
- *coppelia-1870*: the maker and the made, as comedy (T19).
- *legend-of-love-1961*: the same team four years later; another artist (Ferhad) cutting stone for a cause (T19).
- *shurale-1950*: designed by Ptushko, who also filmed *The Stone Flower* in 1946: two Soviet fairy worlds from one visual workshop.

### What a maker can take from it
- The same material can fail and then triumph: the difference was one unifying design idea and a decision to let dance tell the story.
- A material can be the world: malachite gave colour, pattern, costume and set in one stroke.
- Make the obstacle the medium: the Mistress is the stone itself, and the artist must win her over.

### Beyond the stage
Ptushko's 1946 film; Bazhov's tales in Soviet schools and Ural tourism; Prokofiev's suites from the score [probable].

## Легенда о любви / The Legend of Love (1961)
`id: legend-of-love-1961` · threads: T05, T18, T19, T24 · confidence of the header: documented (libretto credit contested)

**Header.** Premiere 23 March 1961, Kirov Theatre, Leningrad [documented: Mariinsky]. Choreography Yuri Grigorovich; music Arif Melikov (1933–2023), from Baku; libretto Nâzım Hikmet after his own play [documented: Mariinsky]; sets, costumes and lighting Simon Virsaladze; conductor Niyazi (Niyazi Tagizade-Hajibeyov) [documented: Belcanto.ru]. Operabase says Grigorovich "crafted" the libretto; the Abai theatre in Almaty credits "Nazim Hikmet and Yuri Grigorovich" [contested]. Three acts, eight scenes; about 3 hours with two intervals. The version seen today is Grigorovich's, in both the Mariinsky and Bolshoi repertoires (Bolshoi premiere 15 April 1965; renewed October 2014) [documented: Operabase; newsru.com].

### In one line
A queen trades her beauty to save her sister's life; both love the painter Ferhad, who chooses to cut through a mountain for his people's water rather than run away with love: the victory of duty over feeling, told in dance from first to last.

### How it came to be
The story is centuries old. Nâzım Hikmet (1902–1963), the Turkish communist poet, wrote his play *Ferhad ile Şirin* (in Russian *Legenda o liubvi*) in a Turkish prison; newsru.com says "in the tenth year" of a 28-year sentence [documented as a claim]. He was freed in 1950 and fled to the USSR in 1951, where the play was staged widely; Grigorovich recalled that in the 1960s "it seemed to run in every theatre of the USSR" [documented: Grigorovich quoted by newsru.com, TASS 2014].

After *The Stone Flower* (1957), Grigorovich and Virsaladze chose the play as "a philosophical parable" for a libretto. The Azerbaijani composer Kara Karayev recommended his student Arif Melikov, "who already had several numbers of the future ballet" [documented: Grigorovich, same]. Melikov had in fact begun the score for Baku [probable]. Hikmet came to the Leningrad premiere and said: "My play ... has been performed in different languages in different countries, but what satisfied me most was what I saw in the ballet" [documented: quoted by the Abai theatre and newsru.com]. Grigorovich then staged it in Novosibirsk (1961), Baku (1962), Prague (1963) and Moscow (1965) [documented: Abai theatre].

### Roots and reference points
- **The legend of Farhad and Shirin**, Persian and Turkic: Ferdowsi's *Shahnameh* (c. 1010), Nizami Ganjavi's *Khosrow and Shirin* (c. 1180), Alisher Navoi's *Farhad and Shirin* (1484). In Nizami, Farhad is a stone-cutter who digs through Mount Bisotun for love of Shirin [probable, working knowledge]. Hikmet: "The plot of my play does not belong to me but to a folk legend" [documented].
- **Hikmet's turn.** The new element is the water: Ferhad cuts through the mountain to bring water to people "suffering without water", and gives up Shirin to do it [documented: Mariinsky synopsis]. The legend of love becomes a parable of the artist's social duty [argued].
- **Persian miniature painting** for the movement and the design: "characteristic poses of the arms, varying motifs of Persian miniatures, a capricious ligature of movement akin to the lace of Arabic letters" [documented: Mariinsky]. Hikmet's play has a Turkish source; the ballet's look is Safavid and Timurid Persia [argued].
- **Earlier works it answers.** *The Fountain of Bakhchisarai* (1934) and *Seven Beauties* (Karayev, Baku 1952): the "Eastern" drambalet with harems and divertissements, which this ballet refuses [argued].

### The world it built
Act I: the queen Mekhmene Banu's chambers; her sister Shirin is dying; a Stranger offers a cure, refusing gold and the crown, and asks for the queen's beauty; she agrees; Shirin, healed, does not recognise her disfigured sister. The palace garden: painters, among them Ferhad, paint an arch; both sisters are struck by him; he and Shirin fall in love. Act II: the lovers flee; the queen's pursuit. Act III: the queen condemns Ferhad to cut through the mountain to bring water; Shirin comes; the queen offers to let them go if Ferhad drops his pick; he cannot betray the people's hope; the lovers part [documented: Mariinsky synopsis].

The rules: no separate mime scenes; the action runs in dance, which "simultaneously conveys the movement of the plot and reveals its inner meaning" [documented: Afisha, Bolshoi programme text]. Signature passages: the queen's frenzied solo monologue; the tender Ferhad–Shirin duets; the chase, a mass number on a hard rhythm; the processions of courtiers as danced, not walked, ensembles; and three trios of the principals, one per act, in which "the action seems to stop and a beam lights only the main characters" [documented: Mariinsky]. The vocabulary is "neoclassical, saturated with acrobatic elements and Oriental features" [documented: Afisha].

### Design and art
Virsaladze made the set "in the form of an old open book, the pages covered with Arabic script"; the characters "seem to step down to us from the pages" [documented: Mariinsky]. The stage floor is left bare for dance; colour is concentrated in costumes: deep reds and gold for the queen's court, pale tones for Shirin, earth colours for Ferhad and the people at the mountain [probable, from Mariinsky photographs]. The queen's disfigurement is shown not with make-up horror but by a veil or mask and by dance [probable]. The two men "freed the stage from everyday interiors and the dance action from everyday motivations and divertissement pauses" [documented: Abai theatre text]. The book is a design argument: the story is a manuscript illumination, a legend told, not a reconstructed history [argued].

### Premiere and reception
Leningrad 1961: Irina Kolpakova (Shirin), Alexander Gribov (Ferhad), Anatoly Gridin (the Vizier), Anatoly Sapogov (the Stranger), the same young team as *The Stone Flower*; Olga Moiseyeva (Mekhmene Banu), later Alla Osipenko [documented: Afisha]. The Abai theatre calls it at once "an artistic sensation" and "a manifesto of a new ballet aesthetic" [documented]. Bolshoi 1965: Maya Plisetskaya (Mekhmene Banu), Natalia Bessmertnova (Shirin), Maris Liepa (Ferhad) [documented: Afisha; Operabase, which misprints Liepa as "Maria Liepa-Schultz"]. Plisetskaya: "here ... I like every moment of Mekhmene's stage life" [documented: quoted by newsru.com]. Ulanova said she regretted she no longer danced [documented: same].

### Versions and lineage
Kirov 1961; Novosibirsk 1961; Baku 1962; Prague 1963; Bolshoi 1965 and continuously since, renewed 23 October 2014 with Melikov at the dress rehearsal; Yekaterinburg 1998; Almaty 15 March 2007 [documented: Abai theatre; newsru.com]. Grigorovich kept editing it: "individual details were corrected, the meaning of the whole refined" [documented: Afisha]. The Abai theatre says a Turkish staging was planned; whether it has happened was not found in this pass.

### Readings and disputes
- **The manifesto of the "symphonic" ballet.** For Russian writers it confirmed the break begun in *The Stone Flower*: no pantomime, dance as drama [documented: Afisha; Abai theatre]. Ezrahi treats the Grigorovich turn as the thaw's main ballet event [probable].
- **Duty over love.** Newsru calls it "the victory of duty over feeling" [documented]. One reading: a Soviet message (the individual serves the collective's water); another: a prisoner-poet's parable about what the artist owes his people, which the ballet universalises [argued, both]. The queen, who sacrifices most, is the role dancers want, as Plisetskaya's quote shows [argued].
- **Orientalism from inside?** The music is by an Azerbaijani composer and the story Turkic and Persian, yet the makers' framing (arabesques "akin to the lace of Arabic letters") repeats a Western picture of "the East" [argued]. Set against Scholl's critique of *Fountain*'s Zarema (see the Maiden Tower dossier).
- **Who wrote the libretto?** Hikmet (Mariinsky), Grigorovich (Operabase), both (Abai theatre) [contested].

### Facts worth telling
1. The play was written in a Turkish prison [documented as a claim: newsru.com].
2. Its composer was 27 at the premiere, a student of Kara Karayev [documented: dates; Grigorovich].
3. The whole set is an open book of calligraphy [documented].
4. There are no mime scenes [documented: Afisha].
5. The heroine trades her face for her sister's life [documented].
6. The hero ends the ballet alone, digging [documented].
7. Hikmet saw the premiere and said it satisfied him more than any staging of his play [documented].

### Set it beside
- *stone-flower-1957*: the same team; another artist pulled between love and the stone (T19).
- *fountain-bakhchisarai-1934*: the drambalet "East" of harem and mime against the danced "East" of illumination (T05).
- *spartacus-1968* (W3 §2): Grigorovich's next big work, where the trio-monologue device becomes the closed-curtain soliloquy.
- *bayadere-1877*: two women, one man, and a palace; Petipa's divertissement East against Grigorovich's parable East.

### What a maker can take from it
- One framing device (the book) can tell an audience how to read a whole world: as legend, not history.
- Freeze-frames work: three trios that stop time give the inner life a place in a plot-driven world.
- Give the hardest choice to the strongest character, even if she is not the "heroine".

### Beyond the stage
Melikov's suites; the ballet as Azerbaijani cultural pride, with Melikov honoured in Baku [probable]. Hikmet's play and poems remain widely read in Turkey and beyond [probable].

## Кармен-сюита / Carmen Suite (1967)
`id: carmen-suite-1967` · threads: T13, T18, T05, T24 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 20 April 1967, Bolshoi Theatre, Moscow [documented: Mariinsky; Wikipedia]. Choreography and scenario Alberto Alonso (1917–2007), Cuban; music Georges Bizet arranged by Rodion Shchedrin for strings and percussion; designer Boris Messerer [documented: Mariinsky credits, which settle the register's "designer probable"]. One act, thirteen numbers, about 45 minutes [documented: Wikipedia; Mariinsky]. Made for Maya Plisetskaya, Shchedrin's wife. The version seen today is Alonso's as revived by the Bolshoi "twenty years" after it lapsed, for a Plisetskaya jubilee (year not confirmed in this pass), and staged by the Mariinsky since 19 April 2010 (production choreographer Viktor Barykin, lighting Vladimir Lukasevich) [documented: Mariinsky].

### In one line
Carmen's freedom and death played out in a bullring watched by masked spectators, with Fate as a dancer who becomes the bull: a 45-minute Thaw-era jolt to the Bolshoi's three-act habits.

### How it came to be
The idea was Plisetskaya's. In 1964 she asked Shostakovich for a Carmen ballet; he "gently but firmly refused" [documented: Plisetskaya, via Wikipedia]. Khachaturian was also approached [probable, widely reported]. In late 1966 the Ballet Nacional de Cuba toured the USSR; Plisetskaya's mother saw it and urged her to go; Plisetskaya approached its choreographer, Alberto Alonso, brother of Fernando Alonso and brother-in-law of Alicia [documented: Wikipedia; family probable]. Alonso wrote the libretto, worked out the choreography with Cuban dancers, then flew to Moscow to teach it [documented: Wikipedia].

Why could a foreigner choreograph at the Bolshoi? The Mariinsky's programme essay answers: "a politically-motivated exception was made for the Cuban" after the Cuban Missile Crisis [documented]. Shchedrin watched the first rehearsals and tried to write an original score, but could not separate the story from Bizet's music, "one of the best in the whole history of music"; he made instead "a creative meeting of the minds", with no brass or woodwind so as to be as far as possible from Bizet's scoring, and because the Bolshoi's string and percussion players were superb [documented: Shchedrin, BBC Music Magazine 2009, via Wikipedia]. That Shchedrin wrote it in about twenty days is widely repeated [probable; not seen at source].

### Roots and reference points
- **Prosper Mérimée's novella *Carmen* (1845)** and **Bizet's opera (1875)** [documented].
- **The corrida.** Alonso made the bullfight the frame of the whole work: the arena "symbolises life", uniting the bullfight and Carmen's fate in a sinister figure [documented: Wikipedia].
- **Drama theatre and the Thaw.** Alonso said he was inspired by drama theatre: "the movements have to speak" [documented: Mariinsky]. The Mariinsky links Messerer's semicircle of chairs to Yuri Lyubimov's Taganka Theatre production of Brecht's *The Good Person of Szechwan* (1964), where actors sat on chairs in a semicircle [documented: Mariinsky essay].
- **Cuban and Spanish dance**: Alonso combined classical ballet "with elements of Spanish and Latin American dances" and an "unheard-of expressionism" for the USSR [documented: Mariinsky].
- **Earlier Carmens.** Roland Petit's *Carmen* (1949, `carmen-petit-1949`), Paris and London, with Zizi Jeanmaire's cropped hair: the Western precedent for an erotic one-act Carmen [probable as influence].

### The world it built
One continuous act, 13 numbers: Introduction; Dance; First Intermezzo; Changing of the Guard; Carmen's Entrance and Habanera; Scene; Second Intermezzo; Bolero; Torero; Torero and Carmen; Adagio; Fortune-Telling; Finale [documented: Wikipedia]. Fate, a dancer in black and Carmen's alter ego, reads her cards and in the finale plays the bull; Carmen dances in turn with Fate, the Torero and José until José stabs her [documented: Wikipedia]. Alastair Macaulay noticed that in the final quartet "Carmen lies dead at José's feet and Fate at the Torero's" [documented: NYT 2011, via Wikipedia].

The movement: turned-in hips, flexed feet, shoulder shrugs and a heel-first walk; Carmen on pointe but not "balletic"; José in a white uniform, stiff with military code; the Torero all flourish [probable, from the film and photographs]. The Mariinsky's Russian text says Plisetskaya walked "not on tiptoe but heel-first" and wore "not a tutu but a risky short dress" [documented]. Shchedrin's music: Habanera introduced by vibraphone and timpani; percussion that "unexpectedly rattle[s]" the Changing of the Guard; a finale ending in distant bells and pizzicato [documented: Andrew Lindemann Malone, AllMusic, via Wikipedia].

### Design and art
Messerer (b. 1933), a Moscow painter and stage designer and Plisetskaya's cousin [probable], built a half-arena: a curved wooden barrier with high-backed chairs on which the dancers not dancing sit and watch, a uniformed judge, and above it an enormous bull's-head mask that hangs over the stage [documented for bullring, masked spectators and judge: Wikipedia; mask probable]. Lighting is hard and white, the palette black, red and ochre [probable]. Plisetskaya's costume, a short black skirt with a red flower in her hair, became the image of the role [probable; 1974 photograph on Wikimedia Commons]. The design's "shocking conditionality and asceticism of stage space" put it at "the cutting edge" of Moscow theatre [documented: Mariinsky].

### Premiere and reception
Plisetskaya (Carmen); Nikolai Fadeyechev (José) [documented: Mariinsky mentions Fadeyechev]; Sergei Radchenko (Torero) and Natalia Kasatkina (Fate) [probable]. Alonso's first version was more erotic; Plisetskaya and Fadeyechev asked him to tone it down "so that the ballet was not totally banned" [documented: Mariinsky]. The Minister of Culture, Yekaterina Furtseva, banned it after the premiere [documented: Wikipedia]. Quotes attributed to her: "We cannot allow them to make a whore out of Carmen, the heroine of the Spanish people" [documented as a quotation: Wikipedia]; at the Bolshoi, "Nothing but eroticism. The opera's music has been mutilated" [documented: Wikipedia]. Shostakovich, then head of the RSFSR composers' union, phoned the ministry to call it "a masterly transcription"; the ban was lifted [documented: Wikipedia, citing Plisetskaya's autobiography]. It was then dropped from the Bolshoi's 1967 Expo tour to Canada, reportedly over Plisetskaya's "very abbreviated black tights", after a seven-hour meeting in Furtseva's office [documented: Wikipedia, from the impresario's account to the diplomat Robert Ford]. Premier Alexei Kosygin saw and praised it in 1968; it went to Britain that year [documented: Wikipedia].

### Versions and lineage
Moscow 1967 (Plisetskaya danced it to the end of her Bolshoi career); Havana the same year for Alicia Alonso, with the cut passion restored; Alonso then staged "not just the same production but variations" internationally [documented: Mariinsky]. Bolshoi revival for Plisetskaya's jubilee, Alonso staging "another new version" for Svetlana Zakharova [documented: Mariinsky]. Mariinsky 2010 [documented]. Mats Ek's *Carmen* (Cullberg, 1992) uses Shchedrin's score with new choreography [probable; W3 §5 lists Ek's *Carmen* 1992]. The Shchedrin score is now Shchedrin's best-known work [documented: Wikipedia]. A 1978 film-ballet with Plisetskaya exists [probable].

### Readings and disputes
- **Freedom against the state.** The Mariinsky essay reads the "impermissible ... idea of freedom" so clearly that the ballet "could never be shown in any Soviet theatre", and Carmen as the "best expression" of Plisetskaya's individuality [documented]. Plisetskaya herself, in her autobiography *I, Maya Plisetskaya* (1994), presents the fight as hers [probable].
- **Iconoclasm or vulgarity?** Sanderson: "iconoclastic but highly entertaining"; Raymond Tuttle: "half lurid and half disturbing", with "incipient vulgarity" [documented: both via Wikipedia]. Edward Greenfield (*Gramophone*, April 1969): "a skilful hotchpotch on popular themes" [documented].
- **The famous exchange.** The Mariinsky prints Furtseva's "Your Carmen will die!" and Plisetskaya's reply "As long as I live Carmen will not die" [documented]. In Plisetskaya's own version (via Wikipedia) Furtseva "screeched" that she was "a traitor to classical ballet" and she "did not reply" [documented]. Two versions of the line that defines the work's legend.
- **Translation myth.** The Mariinsky's English text says Plisetskaya danced "not en pointes but in plain shoes"; the Russian original says she walked "heel-first", a gait, not a shoe [documented]. Photographs show pointe shoes [probable].
- **Cuba in Moscow.** A Cold War alliance opened the Bolshoi to a foreign choreographer [documented: Mariinsky]; Greenfield joked it would not "endear itself to the US State Department" [documented].

### Facts worth telling
1. Shostakovich refused to write it, then saved it [documented].
2. Shchedrin dropped all brass and woodwind [documented].
3. The minister said they were making "a whore" of Carmen [documented as a quote].
4. A Cuban choreographer was allowed in because of the Missile Crisis alliance [documented: Mariinsky].
5. Fate plays the bull [documented].
6. Its sets went to Canada without it [documented: Wikipedia].
7. The whole ballet lasts about 45 minutes [documented].

### Set it beside
- *carmen-petit-1949*: the Paris erotic Carmen; this is the Moscow-Havana one (T13).
- *giselle-alonso-1945-1963* (W3 §3): the same Cuban family's national classic; one school, two directions.
- *spartacus-1968* (W3 §2): a year later at the same theatre: Grigorovich's heroic epic against Alonso's chamber tragedy.
- *bolero-bejart-1961*: a single circular space, a watching ring of dancers, one music, one erotic charge.

### What a maker can take from it
- A single space can hold a whole story if it is also a metaphor: the bullring is street, prison and fate.
- Arranging a classic can be authorship: Shchedrin kept every tune and changed every colour.
- Put the audience inside the world: the watching chairs make spectators complicit.

### Beyond the stage
Shchedrin's score is a concert and recording staple (Kremlin Chamber Orchestra, Russian National Orchestra under Pletnev) [documented: Wikipedia]; figure skaters use it [probable].

## Across these works

**The state is the client, but the idea rarely comes from it.** Only *Gayane* has a politician at its origin (Mikoyan's suggestion). Everywhere else the idea came from inside the arts: a newspaper competition (*Golden Age*), a librettist (*Fountain*), a film-studio director (*Romeo*), a composition teacher's exercise (*Shurale*), a husband-and-wife team at home (*Maiden Tower*), a ballerina (*Carmen Suite*). The state's role was the frame (the anniversary, the Moscow "decade" of a republic's art, the ban) and the veto. The "decades" are the hidden engine of the national ballets in this batch: Baku 1940, Yerevan 1939 (*Happiness*, the root of *Gayane*), Kazan 1941 (*Shurale*, delayed by war). All three were built from local folk song on Petipa's frame, with Russian or Leningrad-trained choreographers beside local artists, and all three produced credit disputes that are still open.

**One method, two generations.** The batch traces the drambalet from its trial (*Golden Age*, where an opera director first supervised a ballet) to its model (*Fountain*) and peak (*Romeo*), its strain (the 1954 *Stone Flower*, 17 performances) and its overthrow by Grigorovich and Virsaladze (*Stone Flower* 1957, *Legend of Love* 1961). Then *Carmen Suite* (1967) shows a third path: not Grigorovich's "symphonic" spectacle but a 45-minute chamber piece from abroad. The same theatres and some of the same dancers (Sergeyev, Dudinskaya, Ulanova, Plisetskaya) carried all three styles.

**Design as the unifying idea.** The works that last are the ones with a single design idea: Khodasevich's props-in-action revue (lost) and her ninety-year *Fountain*; Virsaladze's malachite box and calligraphy book; Messerer's bullring of chairs; three film animators' fairy forest for *Shurale*. Where design was naturalist "huts and heavy clothes" (*Stone Flower* 1954), the work died.

**Scores outlive stories.** Almost every ballet here had its plot rewritten while its music held: *Golden Age* (three librettos), *Gayane* (at least five), *Romeo* (happy then tragic ending), *Maiden Tower* (1999), *Shurale* (renamed, re-orchestrated). The pieces that reached the world were musical fragments: *Tahiti Trot*, the Sabre Dance, the Dance of the Knights, Shchedrin's Habanera.

**Contradictions in the record are the rule.** Two premiere dates for *Gayane*, *Stone Flower* and the 1982 *Golden Age*; two performance counts from the same author for *Golden Age*; two Mistresses of the Copper Mountain in two editions of one encyclopedia; two versions of Plisetskaya's famous retort; official English pages with a wrong year (*Romeo*, "1938") and a mistranslation (*Carmen*'s "plain shoes"); the Royal Ballet fusing Petipa's 1893 *Cinderella* with a 1940 Kirov plan. A course that teaches from company websites must check them against encyclopedias and the theatres' own Russian texts.

**Surprises.** The 1999 *Maiden Tower* "rid Soviet propaganda" by removing an incest plot, not a political message (Scholl). Ulanova's pulse was taken by a labour inspector in 1930. *Romeo* reached the stage first in Brno and *Cinderella* in Moscow against the composer's wish. A Kirov orchestra voted in writing not to play Prokofiev. Two women are central makers in a period usually told through men: Nina Anisimova (*Gayane*) and Gamar Almaszade (*Maiden Tower*), with Mira Mendelson as co-librettist of *Stone Flower*.

**For the course.** This batch is the best place to teach "who is the author?" (T13, T18): composer, librettist, director, choreographer, designer, ballerina and minister all leave marks, and the credit line hides most of them.
