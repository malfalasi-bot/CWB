## Золотой век / The Golden Age (1930)
`id: golden-age-1930` · threads: T08, T11, T09 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 26 October 1930, Leningrad State Academic Theatre of Opera and Ballet (GATOB, the former and future Mariinsky, named the Kirov from 1935) [documented: Mariinsky exhibition page "Shostakovich at the Mariinsky", text by Olga Makarova]. Choreographers Vasily Vainonen (Act I), Leonid Yakobson (Act II) and Vladimir Chesnakov (Act III); overall staging by the opera director Emmanuil Kaplan with Vainonen [documented: Mariinsky; act division documented in Wikipedia]. Music Dmitri Shostakovich, Op. 22, his first ballet, written at 23 and 24. Libretto Alexander Ivanovsky, a film director. Sets and costumes Valentina Khodasevich. Conductor Alexander Gauk. Three acts (six or eight scenes; sources differ). The version most often seen now is not the 1930 ballet but Yuri Grigorovich's 1982 Bolshoi remake, with a new story, and the concert suite Op. 22a.

### In one line
A Soviet football team visits a capitalist city's industrial fair, resists its jazz, its fascists and its seductress, and is freed by the local workers: a political cartoon made into a dance revue.

### How it came to be
On 6 January 1929 the Leningrad paper *Zhizn' iskusstva* (Life of Art) printed the rules of a competition for "a Soviet ballet" that would give "material for a pantomime-choreographic experiment" [documented: conditions cited by Makarova from *Zhizn' iskusstva*, 6 Jan 1929, p. 2]. The theatre wanted to break "the domination of the decrepit classical repertoire" [documented: 1931 audience booklet quoted by Makarova]. Ivanovsky's entry, first called *Dinamiada* (after the Dynamo sports club), won [documented: Belcanto.ru]. The libretto was handed to Shostakovich, who wrote 37 numbers in the rest of 1929 [documented: Belcanto.ru]. A historian of Leningrad ballet notes that Shostakovich and the ballet chief Fyodor Lopukhov were unenthusiastic about the libretto but, after the Shakhty show trial of 1928, "in no position to refuse" [documented as a claim: Kate Lebere, UVic honours thesis 2019, citing Carolyn Pouncy 2005].

The theatre then did something new. It gave a full-length ballet to three young dancers who had never staged one, and put an opera director over them. Makarova calls this the earliest case at the theatre of a director supervising a ballet, a practice that became standard in the drambalet [documented: Makarova]. Rehearsals ran over a year. The premiere was planned for 28 June 1930 and moved to the autumn because the school children in the cast had a right to summer holidays [documented: Mariinsky].

### Roots and reference points
- **Sport and physical culture.** Shostakovich wrote that Soviet dances must be "saturated with elements of healthy physical culture and sport" [documented: 1931 booklet, quoted by Makarova]. He was a keen football fan [probable: MusicWeb review, 2018].
- **The West as entertainment.** Foxtrot, tango, cancan, tap ("chechotka") and music-hall stood for capitalism. The famous entr'acte *Tahiti Trot* is Shostakovich's orchestration of Vincent Youmans's "Tea for Two" (from *No, No, Nanette*, 1925), written for a bet with the conductor Nikolai Malko that he could not do it in under an hour; he took about 40 minutes [documented: LA Phil programme note, Dennis Bade]. The date of the bet is usually given as 1927; LA Phil says the piece was first heard in 1928 [contested in detail, probable 1927–28].
- **Agitprop and the revue.** The ballet borrows from the "living newspaper" and the workers' club show: quick scenes, types instead of characters (the Diva, the Fascist, the Negro boxer, the Komsomol girl) [argued]. Earlier Soviet works it follows: *The Red Poppy* (1927) for the decadent-West Charleston (see W2 §10a) [argued].

### The world it built
Three acts. Act I: the "Golden Age" industrial exhibition in a Western city, a boxing match "for advertising" in which a fascist referee rules against a Black boxer and a Komsomol girl slaps him; the fascist Diva tries to seduce the Soviet team captain and is refused [documented: Lebere 2019; Mariinsky photo captions]. Act II: provocations, planted leaflets, arrest. Act III: the workers free the team; a football match; a final dance of solidarity [documented: MusicWeb].

Two vocabularies carry the politics [argued]. The Soviets dance athletic, acrobatic, high-lifting movement; the bourgeoisie dances ballroom and cabaret with what Shostakovich called "the character of unhealthy eroticism" [documented: booklet via Makarova]. Gauk heard the score as "two warring planes", ironic West against energetic sport [documented: Gauk, quoted by Belcanto.ru]. Signature passages: Yakobson's "magnifying glass of time", in which a whole crowd of sportsmen suddenly froze and moved on in slow motion, "like a rapid" film effect, which audiences cheered [documented: Makarova]; the Pioneer dance, where children strip a boy dressed as a top-hatted capitalist to reveal a Pioneer underneath [documented: Shostakovich's memoir of Yakobson, TsGALI, via Makarova]; the "Pendulum", the dance of the Western Komsomol girl and four Soviet footballers. The score: saxophones, banjo, a symphonic adagio with solo saxophone, a fugue in Act III, polka, foxtrot, tango [documented: Wikipedia instrumentation; Belcanto.ru].

### Design and art
Khodasevich (1894–1970), a painter trained in Paris and Munich and an experienced drama designer, set out her rules in the 1931 booklet: break with "pictorial representational decorations"; maximum use of décor in the action; "entertainment; quick change of impressions"; place and situation told "by props and costumes, played with by the actor" [documented: quoted by Makarova]. Her sketches for the Diva and a Secretary survive in the Mariinsky collection [documented: captions]. The look was constructivist revue rather than painted fairyland [argued]. Grigorovich's 1982 remake was designed by Simon Virsaladze [documented: BelAir Blu-ray credits via Classical Net].

### Premiere and reception
The Diva was Olga Jordan (Iordan), with Elena Lyukom in another cast; the fascists included Leonid Lavrovsky, later the choreographer of *Romeo and Juliet*; Galina Ulanova, Olga Mungalova, Konstantin Sergeyev and Vakhtang Chabukiani danced in Yakobson's act [documented: Mariinsky photo captions]. Audiences loved it. At the dress-rehearsal debate it was called "un-Soviet", some dances "pitiful and wretched"; one speaker said the old ballet had been "laid flat on both shoulder blades" by it [documented: council minutes, TsGALI f. 337, via Makarova]. The *Krasnaya gazeta* critic blamed "formalism and aestheticism" and an "unjustified passion for music hall" [documented: SPb Vedomosti, 22 Nov 2024]. The labour-safety inspector wanted the "Pendulum" removed: "Once Ulanova's pulse was 170" [documented: minutes via Makarova]. The run was 18 performances by Makarova's journal article and 19 by her Mariinsky exhibition text, 26 Oct 1930 to 15 May 1931 [contested: same author, two counts].

### Versions and lineage
- 1930 Leningrad: lost as choreography; photographs, sketches, minutes and the répétiteur score survive [probable].
- 1982 Bolshoi, Grigorovich, new libretto with the musicologist Isaak Glikman, Shostakovich's friend: the action moves to a Soviet seaside town in the 1920s and a restaurant called "The Golden Age"; Boris the fisherman, Rita the cabaret dancer and Yashka the gangster; music padded with other Shostakovich works, including piano concerto movements [documented: Wikipedia; Mariinsky 2006 page; Classical Net]. Premiere 4 November 1982 [documented: Wikipedia; SPb Vedomosti] or 14 October 1982 [documented: Belcanto.ru] [contested]. The role of Boris was made on Irek Mukhamedov [documented: Wikipedia]. Revived 2016 and filmed for cinema [documented: MusicWeb].
- 2006 Mariinsky, Noah D. Gelber with the drama director Andrei Prikotenko, new libretto by Konstantin Uchitel, full original score [documented: Mariinsky 2006 page].
Grigorovich had once asked Shostakovich's permission to revive his ballets and was told "Don't reanimate these ballets, I wouldn't want that" [documented: Mariinsky 2006 page, quoting the anecdote].

### Readings and disputes
- **A failed experiment killed by politics.** The ballet was withdrawn in the turn against "formalism"; its music survived only in the suite [documented: Wikipedia; MusicWeb]. Makarova leaves the cause open: the failure of *The Bolt* (1931) or the campaign against "class enemies" [documented: Makarova 2017].
- **A school for choreographers.** Makarova's own thesis (Vaganova Academy *Vestnik* 2017, no. 2) is that the real "golden age" it opened was for young choreographers: Vainonen and Yakobson went on to *The Flames of Paris* and *Spartacus* [argued, Makarova].
- **Satire with two edges.** Laura E. Kennedy's book *Shostakovich's Ballets and the Search for Soviet Dance* argues that the three ballets offered three distinct answers to the demand for a Soviet ballet (modern, then romantic) [documented: catalogue abstract; publisher and year not confirmed in this pass]. Critics in 1930 complained that the "decadent" young people came out attractive rather than repellent [documented: SPb Vedomosti]. A reading: the satire let a Soviet audience enjoy the jazz it was told to despise [argued].
- **The 1982 version as a different work.** Arlene Croce wrote that Grigorovich "doesn't tell stories, he untells them" [documented: *New Yorker*, 27 July 1987].

### Facts worth telling
1. *Tahiti Trot* is "Tea for Two", orchestrated in about 40 minutes for a bet [documented: LA Phil].
2. The premiere was postponed because the child performers had a right to summer holidays [documented: Mariinsky].
3. A safety inspector took Ulanova's pulse in rehearsal: 170 [documented: Makarova].
4. Lavrovsky, later Bolshoi chief choreographer, danced a Fascist [documented: Mariinsky caption].
5. The ballet was performed 18 or 19 times and then not staged for 52 years [contested count; documented gap].
6. Shostakovich refused permission for revivals in his lifetime [documented: Mariinsky 2006, anecdote].
7. The orchestra includes a banjo and two saxophones [documented: Wikipedia].

### Set it beside
- *pas-dacier-1927* (Massine and Prokofiev for Diaghilev, Paris): the Soviet factory as seen from Paris, against the Paris café as seen from Leningrad.
- *train-bleu-1924*: sport and leisure as chic in the West; sport as political virtue here (T11).
- *flames-of-paris-1932* (W2 §10b): two years later the same theatre, with Vainonen, found the formula (history, folk, research) that *The Golden Age* lacked.
- *fancy-free-1944*: American popular dance used with affection, not satire.

### What a maker can take from it
- When the "villain" style is the most fun to make, it may become the audience's favourite. Decide which side the pleasure lies on.
- A suite can save a world when the world is cancelled: package a portable piece of your concept.
- A single device (the slow-motion freeze) can outlive a whole production in memory.

### Beyond the stage
The suite, the Polka (reused in Two Pieces for String Quartet, 1931, and arranged for piano) and *Tahiti Trot* are concert and encore standards [documented: Wikipedia]. Grigorovich's 2016 staging was released on Blu-ray by BelAir Classiques (BAC443) [documented].
