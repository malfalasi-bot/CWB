## Fröken Julie / Miss Julie (1950)
`id: miss-julie-1950` · threads: T18, T07, T24, T15 · confidence of the header: documented (with two contested credits)

**Header.** Premiere 1 March 1950, Västerås Theatre, Sweden, on a tour by Riksteatern, the Swedish state touring theatre [documented: SKBL, Cullberg archive]. Second, "official" premiere 7 September 1950, Royal Swedish Opera, Stockholm [documented: Klassika; sv.Wikipedia citing *Kungliga teatern: repertoar 1773–1973*]. Choreography Birgit Cullberg. Music Ture Rangström (1884–1947), existing pieces played on piano on tour, orchestrated by Hans Grossman for Stockholm [documented: Näslund 1993]. Scenario Cullberg with Allan Fridericia, the tour's director [contested: SKBL says both wrote it; Klassika credits Fridericia alone]. Costumes after Fridericia's designs; Stockholm scenery by the painter Sven Erixson, "X" [documented: Näslund]. One act, four scenes, about 46 minutes [documented: Cullberg archive; Klassika]. The version seen today descends from the Stockholm staging of September 1950 [probable].

### In one line
Strindberg's play of sex and class told without words, with the count's daughter on pointe and the servants on flat, heavy feet.

### How it came to be
In the summer of 1949 three Swedes went to Paris: Birgit Cullberg, a 41-year-old choreographer trained in modern dance with Kurt Jooss in England; Elsa Marianne von Rosen, a dancer then in operetta; and von Rosen's partner, the Danish writer Allan Fridericia [documented: SKBL, Näslund]. They saw Roland Petit's *Carmen* (1949), and saw that a ballet could show a mind under pressure. Riksteatern had booked them for a 1950 tour with von Rosen as its star, so they needed a vehicle. Fridericia proposed Strindberg's *Miss Julie* (1888); it gave von Rosen a starring role and Cullberg "the erotic and social conflict" she wanted [documented: Näslund; SKBL]. The original company had eight dancers [documented: Näslund]. The ballet opened in Västerås on 1 March 1950 and played 30 times in 21 towns; it was one of the few Riksteatern programmes to make a profit [documented: Näslund]. Joel Berglund, director of the Royal Opera, saw it in Eskilstuna on 21 or 22 March; by 26 March the Opera had taken it [documented: Näslund]. It was the Opera's earliest known Swedish ballet taken in from outside its own stage, and von Rosen became the earliest known prima ballerina not trained in its school [documented: sv.Wikipedia; SKBL]. Cullberg wrote that she had to show she could "make use of the classical technique" to get a permanent post [documented: letter quoted by Näslund].

### Roots and reference points
- **Strindberg's play** (1888), with its Midsummer Eve, its fiddle music and a dance scene that Strindberg himself labelled "Ballet" [documented: Näslund].
- **Petit's *Carmen*** (Paris and London, 1949; register `carmen-petit-1949`), the trigger, named by every source [documented].
- **Kurt Jooss**, Cullberg's teacher; critics judged *Julie* the first time she reached "the level of the master Jooss" [documented: review quoted by Näslund]. See `green-table-1932`.
- **Ture Rangström**, a composer with a "Swedish tone" who had set Strindberg; one critic found the pairing apt [documented: "Lill", *Svenska Dagbladet*, 8 September 1950, via Näslund]. Which Rangström works were used: not found in this pass.
- **Alf Sjöberg's stage and film versions** of the play ran in the same season (the film with Anita Björk) [documented: Näslund]. The ballet competed with them.

### The world it built
Cullberg had to break Strindberg's unity of time and place, so she put on stage what the play only reports: Julie's broken engagement, the Midsummer dance in the barn, the weight of the noble family [documented: Näslund]. The roles are Julie, Jean the valet, Kristin the cook, Julie's fiancé, a farmer, the peasants and the **ancestors** [documented: sv.Wikipedia]. The ancestors come out of their portraits in the final scene to demand their price, and their dance was made larger in Stockholm [documented: Näslund].

The movement rule is a class rule. Julie dances on pointe in the classical style; Jean, Kristin and the farmers dance a grounded, freer style [documented: Cullberg archive]. The farmers' steps are classical jumps "distorted", with flexed feet and bent knees [documented: Näslund, from a 1991 interview with Cullberg]. The seduction duet was the scandal: Bengt Häger called it "a sexual game of fearlessness and intensity, never seen before in ballet" [documented: quoted by Näslund]. Strindberg sends Julie offstage to kill herself; Cullberg has her die on stage, the blade guided by Jean, so that she is a victim of "the power of tradition" [documented: Näslund].

### Design and art
The tour had little scenery and Fridericia's costumes [documented: Näslund]. For Stockholm the painter Sven Erixson (1899–1970), known as "X", designed the set. He was a modernist painter with a reputation for "the Swedish", and gave the barn scene strong local colour; he kept Fridericia's costume designs almost exactly [documented: Näslund]. It was his debut as an Opera designer [documented]. The look was a painted Swedish manor and barn, birch-green Midsummer, peasants in regional dress against Julie's riding habit and pale gown [probable]. The famous image came from the press: the weekly *Se* ran a spread of Mengarelli tearing von Rosen's skirt, under the line "A new love passion shook the venerable Royal Opera" [documented: Näslund]. A 1950 photograph of von Rosen as Julie by Sallstedts bildbyrå is in the Nordiska museet (NMA.0080450) [documented: SKBL caption; licence not checked, link-only].

### Premiere and reception
Julie: Elsa Marianne von Rosen. Jean: Julius Mengarelli, on loan from the Opera [documented: SKBL; Cullberg archive]. Kristin: Cullberg herself [documented: SKBL; *The Independent* obituary, 1999]. The Cullberg company archive names Anne-Marie Lagerborg as the "originator" of Kristin [contested: probably the Stockholm cast; see disputes]. At Västerås there was "complete silence" at the curtain, then cheers [documented: Näslund]. Anna Greta Ståhle (*Dagens Nyheter*) wrote that Cullberg had expressed "the essential psychological developments of the drama through choreography" [documented: via Näslund]. In Stockholm it played 37 times in the first season, often to more than 1,000 of 1,177 seats [documented: Näslund]. In five years about 200 Swedish performances reached some 100,000 people [documented: Näslund]. The Gothenburg staging (1952) met the old charge of "plundering of the dead" until the young Marianne Fröijdh took over the title role [documented: Näslund].

### Versions and lineage
- Riksteatern tour, 1950; Royal Swedish Opera, September 1950; Gothenburg 1952, 1966, 1992; Malmö 1955 (perhaps through Ingmar Bergman, adviser to the theatre) and 1977 [documented: Näslund].
- American Ballet Theatre, Metropolitan Opera House, September 1958, with Violette Verdy and Erik Bruhn [documented: *New York Times* headline, 19 September 1958; *The Independent*]. Bruhn's Jean turned a *danseur noble* into a character dancer [documented: Näslund].
- Cullberg Ballet from 1968, performed to 1987 [documented: Cullberg archive]. Rudolf Nureyev, Flemming Flindt and Niklas Ek danced Jean [documented: Cullberg archive].
- 35 productions worldwide by the end of 1992, including Chile, Iran and Japan [documented: Näslund].
- Named in Sweden's cultural canon in 2025 [documented: sv.Wikipedia citing *Svenska Dagbladet*, 2 September 2025].

### Readings and disputes
- **A ballet of class written in technique.** Erik Näslund ("*Miss Julie*: The Ballet, 1950–1", *Theatre Research International* 18, 1993) reads the pointe-against-flat-foot split as the work's invention, anticipating the classical–modern blends of Glen Tetley and Hans van Manen [documented]. On this reading, the ballet's form is its argument.
- **A ballet of one lonely woman.** The Cullberg company's archive text reads it as "the vulnerability" of a person whom "birthright and wealth help little" [documented]. On this reading, the class device serves a portrait.
- **Faithful or plundered.** Swedish critics in 1950 said the ballet gave a "more primitive direct experience" of the play than the stage or film versions [documented: Näslund's summary]. Gothenburg critics in 1952 said Strindberg had been "stripped right down to the bones" [documented: via Näslund]. Both judged the ballet against the play; neither judged it as dance first.
- **Contradictions in the record.** Näslund's essay as reprinted gives the premiere both as "7 March" and "1 March" [documented]; SKBL and the Cullberg archive give 1 March, so 7 March is probably a slip. The same reprint dates Bruhn's ABT Jean to "1938", plainly 1958. Kristin: Cullberg (SKBL) or Lagerborg (Cullberg archive). The libretto: Cullberg and Fridericia, or Fridericia alone. The register's "razor" is Strindberg's; the ballet's sources speak of a dagger [documented: Näslund].

### Facts worth telling
- Cullberg began classical ballet training only after 40 [documented: Cullberg archive].
- The music was by a composer who had died three years before; it was never written for dance [documented: dates; Näslund on the piano tour].
- Von Rosen was a real countess's daughter, which the press made much of [documented: SvD 2008, "äkta adelsflickan"; SKBL on her father, Count Reinhold von Rosen].
- Fridericia recalled her costume marked with blood when the dagger pressed too hard [documented: quoted by Näslund].
- The tour made a profit, rare for Riksteatern [documented: Näslund].
- Cullberg's son Mats Ek later reworked *Giselle* for her company [documented: W3 §5].

### Set it beside
- **`giselle-1841`**: a peasant and a noble across class, but in *Julie* it is the noble woman who falls, and no spirits forgive her.
- **`carmen-petit-1949`**: the model; Petit's realism is Parisian chic, Cullberg's is Swedish rural.
- **`manon-1974`** (this batch): MacMillan's later "destroyed woman" ballets; MacMillan also made a *Miss Julie* for Stuttgart in 1970 [probable].
- **`giselle-ek-1982`**: the son's answer to the mother's dance-drama.

### What a maker can take from it
- Give each social group its own physical rule (here pointe against flat foot), and the audience reads class without a word.
- A touring constraint (eight dancers, a piano) can force the strongest version of an idea.
- When you adapt, show what the source only tells; that is where a new medium earns its place.

### Beyond the stage
Sjöberg's film of the play (1951) ran alongside the ballet's early life [documented: Näslund for the season; film date probable]. Cullberg's television ballets won the Prix Italia twice [documented: *The Independent*]. The *Se* magazine spread made ballet a tabloid story in Sweden [documented: Näslund].

## The Nutcracker / George Balanchine's The Nutcracker (1954)
`id: nutcracker-balanchine-1954` (new row) · threads: T04, T06, T13, T20, T01 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 2 February 1954, City Center of Music and Drama, New York, New York City Ballet (NYCB) [documented: NYCB repertory page]. Choreography George Balanchine after Lev Ivanov's 1892 dances (`nutcracker-1892`); battle scene staged, uncredited, by Jerome Robbins [documented: Playbill 2021; PNB]. Music Tchaikovsky, the full 1892 score, with an entr'acte from *The Sleeping Beauty* added [documented: PNB credits]. Scenario after Hoffmann (1816) and Dumas (1844) [contested: see disputes]. Costumes Barbara Karinska, masks Lawrence Vlady; sets Horace Armistead (1954), then Rouben Ter-Arutunian (1964); lighting Jean Rosenthal [documented: NYCB]. Two acts, about 49 and 41 minutes [documented: NYCB]. The version seen today is the 1964 restaging at the New York State Theater (now David H. Koch Theater) [documented: NYPL exhibition].

### In one line
An émigré choreographer rebuilt the Christmas ballet of his St Petersburg childhood for New York, and America made it a yearly ritual and a balance sheet.

### How it came to be
Morton Baum, who chaired City Center's finance committee, wanted a full-evening ballet that would sell, and counted on the fame of Tchaikovsky's *Nutcracker Suite* [documented: PNB; Playbill]. Balanchine answered, "If I do anything, it will be full-length and expensive" [documented: quoted by PNB]. He had danced in the Mariinsky production as a boy: a soldier, the Mouse King, the Little Prince and the lead in the hoop dance [documented: PNB]. In his *Complete Stories of the Great Ballets* he wrote that he went back to the original score, restored its cuts and used Hoffmann's story, "keeping to the outlines of the dances as given at the Mariinsky" [documented: quoted by NYCB, 2024]. Baum gave him $40,000; the growing tree took $25,000 of it. Baum asked, "George, can't you do it without the tree?" Balanchine's reply is quoted as "The ballet is the tree" or "*The Nutcracker* is the tree" [documented: Balanchine to Nancy Reynolds, quoted in *Vanity Fair*, 2014; PNB; NYPL]. On opening night the costumes were unfinished, and Balanchine and Robbins sat down with Karinska's seamstresses to sew [documented: PNB]. The ballet "quickly became NYCB's biggest box office success" [documented: company's words, NYCB].

### Roots and reference points
- **Hoffmann, *Nussknacker und Mausekönig*** (1816), and **Dumas's** French retelling (1844), the source of the 1892 libretto [documented: PNB].
- **The 1892 Petipa–Ivanov ballet**, which Balanchine knew from inside; he kept Ivanov's mime for the Little Prince and the Candy Cane dance, both of which he had performed [documented: Playbill].
- **Earlier American Nutcrackers**: Willam Christensen's full-length version for San Francisco Ballet, 1944, made on a budget of $1,000 [documented in `scope2/G2` from NEA]; Ballet West calls Christensen's the oldest full production in the country [documented: yellowface.org, quoting Ballet West's director]. A one-act Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo version toured from 1940 [probable].
- **Disney's *Fantasia*** (1940) had made the Suite a household sound [probable; see `scope2/G3`].
- **Memory**: Balanchine linked the tree to the Christmases of his own childhood, its tinsel and smell [documented: NYCB 2024; *Vanity Fair*].

### The world it built
Act I: the Stahlbaum party, with games, gifts and Drosselmeier's nephew; the night, the battle of mice and soldiers; the tree's growth; the snow forest and the Waltz of the Snowflakes [documented: NYCB synopsis]. Act II: the Kingdom of the Sugarplum Fairy; the Little Prince tells of the battle in mime; divertissements (Hot Chocolate, Coffee, Tea, Candy Canes, Marzipan, Mother Ginger and her Polichinelles, Waltz of the Flowers); the grand pas de deux; departure in a sleigh [probable for the full list; NYCB for the frame].

Its rules: children are real children, more than 120 from the School of American Ballet in two casts [documented: NYCB]; the ballerina is a fairy rather than a girl grown up, so Marie stays a child to the end [probable]. Balanchine kept adding effects from St Petersburg memory: in 1968 a hidden track so that the Sugarplum Fairy glides across the stage, and wands with snowballs for the Snowflakes [documented: PNB]. Coffee was first made for Francisco Moncion with four child "parrots", then remade in 1964 as a solo for the tall Gloria Govrin [documented: NYCB 2024].

### Design and art
The 1954 sets by Horace Armistead were simple and relied on painted clouds; the City Center stage limited the tree [documented: NYCB 2024]. The 1964 staging was built for a bigger house. Rouben Ter-Arutunian, an Armenian designer trained in Berlin, Vienna and Paris, made a Germanic interior in an Eastern European palette, yellows and blues, with Slavic food on the tree and fruit in the land of sweets [documented: NYPL exhibition]. He made set models to test coloured gels [documented: NYPL]. The tree grows from 12 to 41 feet, spreads 23 feet and weighs a ton [documented: NYCB]. The blizzard uses about 50 pounds of paper snow, swept up and reused [documented: NYCB]. Karinska, whose shop worked only for NYCB from 1964 under a Ford Foundation grant, revised her own costumes and gave the Sugarplum Fairy two: rose-pink for the welcome, pale green for the pas de deux [documented: NYPL; NYCB 2024]. Mother Ginger's skirt is nine feet wide, weighs 85 pounds and needs three handlers [documented: NYCB]. Karinska sewed the word "Balanchine" into costumes as a hidden signature [documented: Phil Chan, Oregon Ballet Theatre, 2025].

### Premiere and reception
Original cast: Maria Tallchief (Sugarplum Fairy), Nicholas Magallanes, Tanaquil Le Clercq, Michael Arshansky (Drosselmeier), Alberta Grant, Susan Kaufman, Paul Nickel [documented: NYCB]. Walter Terry wrote that Tallchief was "herself a creature of magic" [documented: quoted by NYPL]. Playbill (2021) calls 1954 a turning point "in the history of dance in America" [documented as the magazine's claim]. Money: the budget was $40,000 [documented]. For the later economy, see `scope2/G2` §2.3 and `scope/05` §2.1, which already give the Dance/USA and NPR figures (holiday shows about 48% of surveyed US companies' season revenue in 2017; about 45% of NYCB's ticket revenue in 2025) and the trap of confusing ticket and total revenue [documented there].

### Versions and lineage
- 1954, City Center (Armistead); 11 December 1964, State Theater (Ter-Arutunian) [documented: Playbill].
- CBS *Playhouse 90*, 25 December 1958, with Balanchine as Drosselmeier [documented in `scope2/G3`].
- Film, 1993, directed by Emile Ardolino, narrated by Kevin Kline, with Darci Kistler, Damian Woetzel and Macaulay Culkin; Stephen Holden found Culkin an "uncomfortably heavy presence" [documented: AFI Catalog; *NYT* review, 24 November 1993].
- Licensed stagings through the George Balanchine Trust, often with new designs: Pacific Northwest Ballet, 2015, designs by the picture-book artist Ian Falconer [documented: PNB]. Miami City Ballet and others also dance it [probable].
- 2017: NYCB changed Tea after letters to Peter Martins; the man's hat and make-up were modified and the pointing fingers and little runs removed [documented: *NYT*, 26 December 2017, via yellowface.org]. In 2018 the Trust told licensees the changes were "an approved option, though not required" [documented: *NYT*, 13 November 2018]. PNB turned the Tea man into a cricket in 2021 [documented: PNB blog].

### Readings and disputes
- **A ritual made by communities.** Jennifer Fisher (*"Nutcracker" Nation*, Yale, 2003) reads the American Nutcracker as a folk ritual remade in each town, with local children and local jokes [probable: summary; book documented in `scope/05`]. On this reading, Balanchine's is one parent among many.
- **An émigré's lost Russia.** PNB's notes say Balanchine "remembered the luxurious days before the Russian revolution and held them as an ideal"; the NYPL exhibition names nostalgia, faith and childhood as its themes [documented]. On this reading, the production is a memory palace.
- **Who invented the American Nutcracker.** NYCB says its popularity "prompted other ballet companies" to make their own [documented: NYCB]. San Francisco's 1944 staging came ten years earlier [documented: NEA via G2]. Both can be true: San Francisco made the earliest known full American one; Balanchine's made the national habit [argued].
- **Hoffmann or Dumas.** Balanchine wrote that he used Hoffmann's story; NYCB's own repertory page says it is "based on the Alexandre Dumas père version" [documented, both]. The heroine's name Marie Stahlbaum is Hoffmann's [documented: NYCB 2024].
- **The 1892 date.** NYCB's page gives 17 December 1892; PNB gives 6 December; the register gives 18 December. 6/18 December (Old Style/New Style) is the usual pairing [probable].
- **Caricature.** Phil Chan and Georgina Pazcoguin (Final Bow for Yellowface, 2017) argue the Tea dance was caricature to be replaced with character, not deleted [documented]. Holders of the original text argue for changing as little as possible: the Trust made the changes optional [documented].

### Facts worth telling
- More than half the 1954 budget went on one tree [documented: PNB].
- The 1964 tree is a one-ton machine that grows to 41 feet [documented: NYCB].
- Balanchine sewed costumes on opening night [documented: PNB].
- Robbins staged the battle and got no credit [documented: Playbill; PNB].
- The Sugarplum Fairy glides on a hidden track, added in 1968 [documented: PNB].
- The title carries a registered trademark sign, *George Balanchine's The Nutcracker®* [documented: NYCB].
- Only five of Balanchine's ballets are full-length [documented: NYPL].

### Set it beside
- **`nutcracker-1892`**: the failure of St Petersburg against the triumph of New York, same score.
- **`hard-nut-1991`** (Mark Morris): the same party in the 1960s suburbs, with men in the snow.
- **`jewels-1967`** (this batch): Balanchine's other full-evening "collection", plotless where this is storybook.
- **`agon-1957`** (W2 §11): made three years later by the same company in practice clothes; the two poles of one house.

### What a maker can take from it
- Spend on the one image that carries the world (the tree), even if everything else is thin.
- Build in a place for the audience's own children; it turns a show into a family tradition.
- A world that returns every year must be maintained like a building: patched, relit and, when its pictures of others offend, redesigned.

### Beyond the stage
CBS 1958 telecast; the 1993 Warner film; Joel Meyerowitz's photo book of the production [documented: AFI Catalog]. The Nutcracker market in US shopping malls, toys and school trips is treated in `scope2/G3` [see there].

## Revelations / Revelations (1960)
`id: revelations-1960` · threads: T14, T16 · confidence of the header: documented (design credit contested)

**Header.** Premiere 31 January 1960, Kaufmann Concert Hall, 92nd Street YM-YWHA, New York, by Alvin Ailey's young company, which counts itself as the Alvin Ailey American Dance Theater [documented: Ailey programme note quoted in DeFrantz 2004; Ailey pressroom]. Choreography Alvin Ailey, aged 29. Music: traditional spirituals, song-sermons and gospel, in arrangements by Hall Johnson, James Miller, Howard A. Roberts, Ella Jenkins and Brother John Sellers [documented: Ailey credits]. No scenario beyond Ailey's programme note. Décor and costumes Ves Harper; "Rocka My Soul" costumes redesigned by Barbara Forbes; lighting Nicola Cernovich [documented: Ailey credits; contested: Wikipedia names Laurence Maldonado for the original sets and costumes]. Three sections, 36 minutes in the fixed version [documented: Ailey]. The version seen today is the three-part form fixed in 1961–62 [documented: DeFrantz].

### In one line
A suite of Black church music, danced from sorrow through baptism to a Sunday celebration, which became the most seen modern-dance work in the world.

### How it came to be
Ailey grew up in rural Texas and went to services at the small Mount Olive Baptist Church [documented: Ailey pressroom]. He called what he brought from there "blood memories" [documented]. "*Revelations* began with the music," he said; he remembered hearing "Wade in the Water" sung at a baptism and "I Been 'Buked" sung by the pastor's wife [documented: Ailey info sheet]. He planned it as the second part of an evening-long survey of African American music, begun in 1958 with *Blues Suite*; the survey was never finished [documented: DeFrantz]. At the premiere *Revelations* had sixteen numbers, a live chorus with two onstage singers, and ran over an hour [documented: DeFrantz]. Ailey cut it to about half an hour to take it to Jacob's Pillow in summer 1961 [documented: DeFrantz]. A State Department tour in 1962, which took the ten-dancer company to Australia and Asia, made him fix the music on tape, and the three-part form set hard [documented: Wikipedia; *Encyclopedia of American Studies* for Australia]. The dancers were surprised by a standing ovation at the premiere [documented: *Encyclopedia of American Studies*].

### Roots and reference points
- **The Black Baptist church**: its choirs, deacons, ushers, testifying and river baptism [documented: Ailey].
- **Writers**: James Baldwin and Langston Hughes [documented: Ailey pressroom].
- **Technique**: Lester Horton, Ailey's teacher in Los Angeles, whose flat backs, lateral "T" tilts and deep lunges are the base; also Martha Graham and Doris Humphrey; and the Black concert-dance line of Asadata Dafora, Katherine Dunham and Pearl Primus [documented: Wikipedia citing Julia Foulkes, *Modern Bodies*, 2003].
- **Music**: Hall Johnson's concert arrangements of spirituals; the gospel of Brother John Sellers, who sang in the early performances [documented: credits; probable for Sellers on stage].
- **Its own forebear**: *Blues Suite* (1958), the secular half of the plan [documented: DeFrantz].

### The world it built
Three sections, each with a colour [documented: Ailey, quoted by the company]:
1. **"Pilgrim of Sorrow"** (brown, "for coming out of the earth, for going into the earth"): "I Been 'Buked", the group as one rooted body with arms spread like branches; "Didn't My Lord Deliver Daniel"; "Fix Me, Jesus", a duet of a woman and a man who supports her like a minister.
2. **"Take Me to the Water"** (white and pale blue): a processional to the river with a white parasol; "Wade in the Water", the baptism; "I Wanna Be Ready", a male solo of floor work and slow balance.
3. **"Move, Members, Move"** (yellow and black, earth tones): "Sinner Man" for three men; then the church women with hats, fans and stools, gossiping and fanning; the finale "Rocka My Soul in the Bosom of Abraham", almost always repeated as an encore [documented: Google Arts & Culture / Ailey].
The music leads: each song is a scene, and the words of the spirituals are danced as images (water, the chariot, the road) [argued]. The order follows Ailey's own memory, "I did it chronologically" [documented].

### Design and art
Ailey designed by colour before cloth: brown earth, white and pale blue water, yellow Sunday best [documented]. The best-known images are the opening wedge of dancers in brown with arms raised; the white parasol and long white skirts of the baptism; and the yellow dresses and wide hats of the finale [documented: company photographs, rights reserved]. In "Wade in the Water" lengths of blue and white cloth are stretched across the stage as the river [probable]. Judith Jamison remembered being given the parasol as a later idea: "I thought I was being totally demoted" [documented: *Los Angeles Times*, 19 February 2006]. Props are church objects: fans, hats, stools, a parasol, a bowl of water [probable for the bowl].

### Premiere and reception
Dancers at the premiere included Nancy Redi, Joan Derby, Minnie Marshall, Dorene Richardson, Merle Derby, Herman Howell, Jay Fletcher, Nat Horne and Gene Hobgood, with Ailey [documented: Wikipedia list; Ailey's own role probable]. The 1962 *New York Times* review by Allen Hughes found "pleading, struggle, protest, pathos, humor and much more" [documented: quoted in *LA Times* 2006]. James Truitte called it "a dance that contains the history of blacks in America" [documented: *Encyclopedia of American Studies*]. By the mid-1960s some critics charged Ailey's work with being too commercial and not "legitimate" [documented: *Encyclopedia of American Studies*].

### Versions and lineage
- 1960: sixteen numbers, live chorus, over an hour [documented: DeFrantz]; Wikipedia says ten sections [contested].
- 1961: cut to about 30 minutes for Jacob's Pillow; filmed for the CBS religious programme *Lamp Unto My Feet* [documented: DeFrantz; the company dates the broadcast 1962].
- 1962 onward: recorded score, three sections, State Department tours [documented].
- Danced by Ailey II since 1975 [documented: Ailey]. Opening ceremonies, Mexico City Olympics, 1968; White House, including the inaugurations of Jimmy Carter and Bill Clinton [documented: Ailey pressroom].
- Ranked eighth among the most performed dance works worldwide in Bachtrack's 2023 count [documented in `CENSUS.md` §1.1].
- Kept by the company itself; it is not licensed widely to other troupes [probable].

### Readings and disputes
- **A specific history.** Thomas F. DeFrantz (*Dancing Revelations: Alvin Ailey's Embodiment of African American Culture*, Oxford, 2004) argues that Ailey helped define the African American cultural canon, and ties the company's rise to the civil rights struggle [documented: OUP/Duke summary]. On this reading, the dance is a Black American archive.
- **A universal rite.** The company describes it as "transcending barriers of faith and nationality" and "appealing to universal emotions" [documented: Ailey]. On this reading, the church is a frame any audience can enter.
- **Contradictions.** Sixteen numbers (DeFrantz) or ten (Wikipedia); 1961 (DeFrantz) or 1962 (company) for the first telecast; Ves Harper (company) or Laurence Maldonado (Wikipedia) for the original design. The company's 1960 programme note and DeFrantz are the stronger witnesses [argued].
- **Ballet or not.** The company and press call it "the ballet"; the register files it as modern dance [documented, both]. In the US the word "ballet" often means any theatre dance [argued].

### Facts worth telling
- The first version ran over an hour with live singers on stage [documented: DeFrantz].
- Ailey made it at 29 [documented: Ailey].
- It closes most Ailey programmes and almost always gets an encore of "Rocka My Soul" [documented: Google Arts & Culture / Ailey].
- It was danced at the 1968 Olympics in Mexico City [documented].
- A school programme, *Revelations: An Interdisciplinary Approach*, uses it to teach language arts and social studies [documented: Ailey pressroom].
- Its colour plan was set before the costumes: brown, white and blue, yellow [documented].

### Set it beside
- **`rite-of-spring-1913`** (W2 §5): a community rite of sacrifice imagined from outside; Ailey's rite is remembered from inside.
- **`agon-1957`** (W2 §11): the same years in New York; Arthur Mitchell's partnering as the ballet side of Black American presence.
- **`creole-giselle-1984`** (W3 §4): Dance Theatre of Harlem placing Black American life inside a ballet classic, against Ailey's making of a new classic.
- **`common-grounds-acogny-2021`**: a later sacred ensemble from Africa itself.

### What a maker can take from it
- Start from a sound world you know in the body; it gives the work a structure you do not have to invent.
- Plan colour by meaning (earth, water, Sunday) before choosing materials.
- Cut hard. The work became itself only when it lost half its length.

### Beyond the stage
CBS *Lamp Unto My Feet* (1961/62), many later broadcasts [documented]. Images of "I Been 'Buked" and the yellow finale are widely used in American advertising and education [probable].

## Marguerite and Armand / Marguerite and Armand (1963)
`id: marguerite-armand-1963` (new row) · threads: T18, T15, T24 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 12 March 1963, Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, London, the Royal Ballet, at a Royal Gala in the presence of Queen Elizabeth II [documented: Ashton Foundation; EBSCO Research Starters]. Choreography Frederick Ashton. Music Franz Liszt, Piano Sonata in B minor (1854) preceded by *La lugubre gondola* No. 1 (1883), orchestrated by Humphrey Searle; re-orchestrated by Gordon Jacob (1968) and Dudley Simpson (1977, *Gondola* dropped) [documented: Ashton Foundation, after David Vaughan]. Scenario Ashton, after Alexandre Dumas fils, *La Dame aux camélias* (novel 1848, play 1852). Sets and costumes Cecil Beaton; lighting William Bundy, revival lighting John B. Read [documented: ROH Collections]. One act: prologue and four scenes, about 30 minutes [documented: Ashton Foundation; Mariinsky]. The version seen today is the Royal Ballet's 2000 revival with Simpson's orchestration [documented].

### In one line
A dying courtesan relives her one love in four fevered flashbacks, made as a portrait of the most famous partnership in ballet.

### How it came to be
In February 1962 Margot Fonteyn, the Royal Ballet's prima ballerina, first danced *Giselle* with Rudolf Nureyev, who had defected from the Kirov in Paris in June 1961 [probable]. She was 43 when *Marguerite* opened and he was 24 [documented for her age: Mariinsky; his age from his birth date, probable]. Frederick Ashton, the company's chief choreographer, had made ballets on Fonteyn for nearly thirty years [documented: Mariinsky]. The story goes that Ashton heard Liszt's B minor Sonata on the radio and thought it fitted *La Dame aux camélias*; only then did he learn that Liszt had been the lover of Marie Duplessis, the real woman behind Dumas's Marguerite [documented as the story told by the Mariinsky's programme; the affair is probable]. Ashton made the ballet in fifteen rehearsals; he, the conductor John Lanchbery and others wrote sketch scenarios and he took the best ideas [documented: Theatre Heritage Australia, citing David Vaughan]. When Armand first rushes into Marguerite's salon, Nureyev "started stripping off his coat," Ashton recalled, and "flew out from behind me into Margot's arms" [documented: quoted by Theatre Heritage Australia]. Ashton later refused to let anyone else dance it [documented: Ashton Foundation; Mariinsky].

### Roots and reference points
- **Dumas fils**, novel (1848) and play (1852), based on Marie Duplessis (died 1847) [documented: Bonhams note; probable for dates].
- **Verdi's *La traviata*** (1853), the same story in opera [documented: Sarasota Ballet].
- **Liszt's music and Liszt's life**: the Sonata as one long, many-moods movement [documented: Theatre Heritage Australia].
- **Garbo's film *Camille*** (1937, George Cukor), named as Beaton's main costume source [documented as a dealer's catalogue claim: Sarah Colegrave Fine Art; probable].
- **Beaton's own earlier *Camille***, a ballet he designed in 1946 for an American company [documented: Bonhams lot note; the choreographer, John Taras, is probable].
- **Ashton's earlier flashback ballet**: *Apparitions* (1936), also Liszt, also Beaton, also Fonteyn, a poet's opium visions of a woman in a ballgown [documented for Liszt, Beaton and Fonteyn: Costume Society 2025; plot probable].

### The world it built
The structure is a deathbed dream: Prologue (Marguerite dying), The Meeting, The Country, The Insult, The Death of the Lady of the Camellias [documented: NYPL film record timings, about 2, 5, 10, 6 and 5 minutes]. The roles are few: Marguerite, Armand, his Father, a Duke, a Maid, and a male corps of admirers [documented: Ashton Foundation]. The admirers are all men, so the stage is a ring of men round one woman [argued]. Signature passages: Armand's run into her arms; the country pas de deux; the Father's scene, in which Marguerite gives Armand up without being allowed to explain; the Insult, where Armand tears off her necklace and throws money in her face; the final reunion as she dies in his arms [documented: Sarasota Ballet synopsis]. The choreography is built from lifts and rushes rather than set solos [probable]. Ashton said the role freed Fonteyn from her "innate dignity" [documented as paraphrase: Theatre Heritage Australia].

### Design and art
Cecil Beaton (1904–1980) was a society photographer, diarist and Oscar-winning designer (*My Fair Lady*, 1964) [probable for the film]. His Marguerite is a Second Empire courtesan in crinolines, with the camellia as the sign: she throws Armand a white camellia at their meeting [documented: Sarasota Ballet]. The publicity stills of 1963 show Fonteyn in a white tutu with white flowers in her hair, but the costume kept from the stage is black net with a dark velvet bodice and silk roses on the shoulders [documented: *The Observer*, 10 May 2025, on the Garden Museum exhibition]. So the famous picture and the danced dress differ. Beaton's set is a light, open frame that turns the stage into a feverish memory [probable]. Beaton's costume designs for both principals survive and sell at auction (gouache, watercolour and pencil) [documented: Bonhams 2025; Sarah Colegrave]. In 1975 William Chappell made new costumes for a touring "Evening with Fonteyn and Nureyev" [documented: Ashton Foundation].

### Premiere and reception
Fonteyn (Marguerite; Ann Jenner as the young Marguerite in the prologue), Nureyev (Armand), Michael Somes (his Father), Leslie Edwards (a Duke), Barbara Remington (Maid) [documented: Ashton Foundation]. The theatre was decorated with thousands of white carnations [documented: EBSCO]. Eyewitnesses counted 21 curtain calls [documented: Mariinsky programme]. Critics were mixed [documented: EBSCO summary]; quotes not found in this pass. Fonteyn called the rehearsals "a passion more real than life itself" [documented: Ashton Foundation]. The pair danced it about 40 times at Covent Garden and in Milan, Paris (1966), Vienna (1967), the Americas (1975) and London for the last time in 1977 [documented: Colegrave note; Ashton Foundation].

### Versions and lineage
- 1963 Royal Ballet; 1967 Vienna State Opera (staged by Leslie Edwards); 1968 Royal Ballet touring section (Jacob's orchestration); 1975 US tour (Chappell costumes); 4 July 1977 Nureyev Festival, London Coliseum (Simpson's orchestration) [documented: Ashton Foundation].
- 29 February 2000: revived for Sylvie Guillem and Nicolas Le Riche, with David Drew as the Father, by Anthony Dowell's Royal Ballet [documented: Ashton Foundation]. Guillem had refused twice and built the role from Dumas, not from Fonteyn [documented: Mariinsky programme].
- Since then: Tamara Rojo and Sergei Polunin, Zenaida Yanowsky and Roberto Bolle, Alessandra Ferri and Federico Bonelli, Natalia Osipova and Vladimir Shklyarov [documented: Ashton Foundation]; Mariinsky from 8 July 2014; Sarasota Ballet; Australian Ballet 2023 [documented: Mariinsky; Sarasota; Theatre Heritage Australia].
- Film: a 33-minute rehearsal film in costume by Edmée Wood, 1963 or 1964 (NYPL, rights status undetermined) [documented].

### Readings and disputes
- **A ballet about its dancers.** The Mariinsky programme (Olga Makarova) argues Ashton "created their story"; the only content was the pair's magnetism, so the roles could not be passed on [documented]. On this reading, *Marguerite* is a portrait and should have died with its sitters.
- **A ballet about Dumas.** The same essay says Guillem looked for Marguerite "in the literary source", and that today's dancers "dance the story of Dumas' characters" [documented]. On this reading, the ballet outlived its portrait and became repertory.
- **Ashton's own doubt.** Ashton was "not entirely happy" with it [documented as paraphrase: Theatre Heritage Australia, after Vaughan]. Critics in 1963 were mixed (EBSCO). Audiences gave 21 curtain calls (Mariinsky). Both are true of the same night.
- **Contradictions.** The Ashton Foundation says Fonteyn was "in her late forties"; born in 1919, she was 43 [documented: Mariinsky gives 43]. The Mariinsky says Ashton "categorically forbade" others yet notes there were understudies in rehearsal [documented]. The radio-and-Liszt story is told by companies; no first-hand source was found in this pass.

### Facts worth telling
- No one else danced it for 37 years (1963–2000) [documented].
- Liszt really was Marie Duplessis's lover, briefly [probable].
- Ashton made it in fifteen rehearsals [documented: Theatre Heritage Australia].
- The music was orchestrated three times by three arrangers [documented].
- The white-tutu photographs are not the stage dress [documented: *The Observer* 2025].
- The 1963 corps of admirers included the young Anthony Dowell, who later revived the ballet as director [documented: NYPL film record; Ashton Foundation].

### Set it beside
- **`lady-of-camellias-1978`** (this batch): Neumeier's three-act, Chopin version of the same novel; thirty minutes against three hours.
- **`giselle-1841`**: another heroine who dies and forgives; here the heroine is a paid woman and society, not spirits, condemns her.
- **`manon-1974`** (this batch): MacMillan's courtesan, eleven years later, with no forgiveness at the end.
- **`apollon-musagete-1928`** (W2 §9): a ballet made on one dancer's body; compare a work made on two.

### What a maker can take from it
- A frame device (a deathbed dream) lets you cut a long novel to its four strongest scenes.
- Making a work on particular people gives it heat, and also makes it hard to hand on; decide which you want.
- The famous image need not be the object itself; publicity can design a second, parallel work.

### Beyond the stage
The Fonteyn–Nureyev partnership became a press phenomenon of the 1960s, and *Marguerite* was its emblem [probable]. Beaton's designs are shown as art: Barbican, 1986; Garden Museum, 2025 [documented: Bonhams literature; Costume Society].

## Onegin / Onegin (1965)
`id: onegin-1965` · threads: T18, T24 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 13 April 1965, Württembergisches Staatstheater, Stuttgart, Stuttgart Ballet; revised version 27 October 1967 [documented: Stuttgart Ballet]. Choreography and scenario John Cranko, after Alexander Pushkin's verse novel *Eugene Onegin* (1825–1832). Music Tchaikovsky, not from the opera, arranged and orchestrated by Kurt-Heinz Stolze, the company's ballet conductor [documented: Stuttgart Ballet; ABT]. Sets and costumes Jürgen Rose; backdrops painted by Alfons Ostermeier [documented: Rose and Haydée, Stuttgart talk, 2017, reported by Ilona Landgraf]. Three acts, six scenes, about 2 hours 35 minutes with intervals [documented: Bolshoi listing]. The version seen today is the 1967 revision [documented: Stuttgart Ballet; RBO].

### In one line
A bookish country girl offers her heart to a bored dandy, is refused, and years later, married, refuses him: Pushkin's novel told as four great duets.

### How it came to be
Cranko, a South African trained at Sadler's Wells, met Pushkin's poem in 1952 when he made the dances for Tchaikovsky's opera *Eugene Onegin* in London [documented: Bolshoi; Wikipedia]. He proposed a ballet to the Royal Opera House board; it was turned down [documented: Bolshoi; Wikipedia]. Marcia Haydée, his Brazilian muse, later said Cranko had planned it for Covent Garden with Nureyev, Fonteyn, Antoinette Sibley and Anthony Dowell, and that the London board objected to adapting the opera's music [documented: Haydée, 2017, via Landgraf]. Cranko became director in Stuttgart in 1961 [documented: RBO]. When the Stuttgart Opera staged *Eugene Onegin* in his first year, the idea came back; the general manager Walter Erich Schäfer backed it on condition that the opera score was not used [documented: Bolshoi]. Stolze assembled a new score from lesser-known Tchaikovsky [documented]. Because Haydée and Ray Barra were different dancers from Fonteyn and Nureyev (Fonteyn "hated lifts", Haydée was "always up in the air"), "the whole concept had to be changed" [documented: Haydée via Landgraf]. The press did not like the 1965 version [documented: Landgraf]; Cranko revised it until October 1967, cutting a prologue at Onegin's uncle's deathbed and an ending in which Tatiana kisses her children goodnight [documented: Bolshoi; Wikipedia].

### Roots and reference points
- **Pushkin's verse novel** (1825–1832), with its famous letter scene and its ironic narrator [documented for the letter; probable for the narrator].
- **Tchaikovsky's opera** (1879), the work the ballet had to avoid [documented].
- **The Soviet *drambalet*** that Cranko and MacMillan saw on the Bolshoi's London tour of 1956, a model of ballet as acted drama (see `romeo-juliet-lavrovsky-1940`) [probable].
- **Cranko's own *Romeo and Juliet*** (Stuttgart, 1962), his first full-length success, also designed by Rose [documented: Landgraf; opera.lv].
- **Music sources**: *The Seasons* (Op. 37a), piano pieces Opp. 19 and 72, the opera *Cherevichki* (a theme for Tatiana and Onegin), *Francesca da Rimini*, *The Voyevoda* and a duet from an unfinished *Romeo and Juliet* opera [documented: Wikipedia; RBO; ABT running list].

### The world it built
Act I: Madame Larina's garden, where girls play a folk game of looking into a mirror to see their beloved; Tatiana sees Onegin. Her bedroom: she writes the letter, falls asleep, and in a dream Onegin steps out of her mirror for a passionate pas de deux [documented: Stuttgart synopsis; RBO]. Act II: her name-day party, where Onegin tears up her letter and flirts with her sister Olga to spite his friend Lensky; Lensky's solo before the duel; the duel [documented for the party and duel; the torn letter probable]. Act III: years later, the ball at Prince Gremin's palace, where Onegin sees Tatiana as Gremin's wife; her room, where she reads his letter, dances with him, tears it and sends him away [documented: RBO; Stuttgart].

The movement rules are those of Cranko's dance drama: ensemble dances in folk and ballroom styles, and the story carried by pas de deux with acrobatic, high lifts [documented: Wikipedia; RBO]. Hartmut Regitz describes "gigantic Pas de deux" as the goal [documented: quoted by Wiener Staatsoper]. The music is cut to scene: "June" from *The Seasons* for Lensky and Olga, the Overture in F for the mirror duet, "Autumn" for Lensky's solo, the *Cherevichki* polonaise for the ball, *Francesca da Rimini* for the final duet [documented: ABT list].

### Design and art
Jürgen Rose (born 1937), a German designer from Bernburg trained in Berlin, met Cranko in the Stuttgart canteen in 1961 and designed nearly all his big ballets [documented: Landgraf; Staatsoper Munich]. His Onegin is naturalistic Russia of the 1820s: a birch-edged country garden, a girl's bedroom with a tall mirror, a St Petersburg palace ballroom [probable for the birches]. In 1967 he changed the ballroom from blue and gold to red and gold, and Tatiana's ball dress from blue to red [documented: Landgraf]. The skirt has five layers of organza and must match the silk bodice exactly; Rose said that if the reds differ "the dress looks bourgeois, like Giselle's" [documented: Rose via Landgraf]. Ostermeier's painted backdrops were so fine that, on the 1969 New York tour, the director of the Metropolitan Museum wanted to buy one [documented: Landgraf]. Gremin's uniform jacket, stiff and epauletted, makes the lifts harder [documented: Reid Anderson via Landgraf]. The mirror is the key device: a game in Act I, a dream portal in Scene 2 [argued].

### Premiere and reception
Haydée (Tatiana), Ray Barra (Onegin), Egon Madsen (Lensky), Ana Cardus (Olga), Kenneth Barlow (Gremin), Ruth Papendieck (Madame Larina) [documented: ABT]. Audiences liked it; the press did not [contested: the Bolshoi note says "hailed a success with audiences and performers"; Haydée says it "wasn't well received by the press"]. Opera purists objected to the discarding of the opera score, and the Bolshoi note names Balanchine among them [documented as the note's claim]. Box-office figures: not found in this pass.

### Versions and lineage
- 1965 first version; 1967 revision, the standard [documented].
- Stuttgart Ballet's New York tour of 1969 made the "Stuttgart ballet miracle" known abroad [documented for the tour: Landgraf; the phrase: Wiener Staatsoper].
- Australian Ballet 1976; National Ballet of Canada 1984 (staged by Reid Anderson); Royal Ballet 2001 (Adam Cooper, Tamara Rojo, Ethan Stiefel, Alina Cojocaru); American Ballet Theatre (sets by Santo Loquasto); Bavarian State Ballet; Bolshoi; Vienna [documented: Wikipedia; RBO; ABT; Staatsoper Munich; Bolshoi listing].
- Over 20 companies by about 2010 [documented: Bolshoi note].
- Rights are controlled by Cranko's heir Dieter Gräfe, credited on every staging [documented: ABT and Bolshoi credits name "Dieter Graefe"]. Stagings are licensed with coaches from Stuttgart [probable].

### Readings and disputes
- **A new kind of full-length ballet.** Hartmut Regitz (quoted by the Wiener Staatsoper) argues Cranko invented a full-evening form marked by "clarity of its story" and psychology, culminating in huge duets [documented]. On this reading, *Onegin* is the founding text of the modern European story ballet.
- **A ballet made on its dancers.** Haydée's account says the work was reshaped for her and Barra's bodies and their love of lifts [documented]. On this reading, the style of *Onegin* is a portrait of the Stuttgart company in 1965.
- **Tatiana's strength.** Stuttgart Ballet calls her "one of the strongest heroines in the ballet repertoire" because she refuses Onegin [documented]. A reading of the last scene as her sacrifice to marriage and duty is also common (see T24) [argued].
- **Who banned the opera music?** The RBO says the Stuttgart board opposed it; the Bolshoi note says Schäfer set the condition; Haydée says the London board objected [documented, all three]. The likeliest reconciliation is that both houses refused [argued].

### Facts worth telling
- None of the opera's music is used [documented].
- The mirror duet is a dream: Onegin steps out of the glass [documented: RBO].
- Rose changed the Act III colour from blue to red two years after the premiere [documented].
- A museum director wanted to buy a backdrop [documented].
- Cranko died in 1973, aged 45, on a flight home from an American tour [probable].
- The Royal Ballet, which turned the idea down, first danced it in 2001 [documented].

### Set it beside
- **`romeo-juliet-macmillan-1965`**: the same year, another Sadler's Wells–trained choreographer making a three-act drama.
- **`lady-of-camellias-1978`** (this batch): Neumeier, Cranko's pupil, with the same designer, takes the duet-drama to Chopin.
- **`fountain-bakhchisarai-1934`**: Pushkin in the Soviet drambalet, thirty years earlier.
- **`anna-karenina-plisetskaya-1972`**: a Russian novel staged by Russians, with Tchaikovsky's heir Shchedrin's music.

### What a maker can take from it
- When the obvious material is barred (the opera), assembling your own from the same maker's lesser works can give you a fresher voice.
- Reuse a single object (the mirror) in new roles across the story to bind it together.
- Colour is a decision that can be revised: Rose changed his after the work had premiered.

### Beyond the stage
Films and broadcasts of the Stuttgart, Royal and Bolshoi productions circulate on DVD and cinema relays [probable]. Royal Ballet and Opera sells an *Onegin* merchandise line [documented: RBO].

## Das Lied von der Erde / Song of the Earth (1965)
`id: song-of-the-earth-1965` · threads: T10, T05 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 7 November 1965, Württembergisches Staatstheater, Stuttgart, Stuttgart Ballet, on a triple bill with MacMillan's *Danses concertantes* and Cranko's *Opus 1* [documented: MacMillan estate; RBO]. Choreography Kenneth MacMillan. Music Gustav Mahler, *Das Lied von der Erde* (1908–09), a song cycle for tenor, mezzo-soprano (or baritone) and orchestra, on Hans Bethge's free German versions of Tang Chinese poems in *Die chinesische Flöte* [documented: RBO; MacMillan estate]. Singers at the premiere Margarethe Bence and James Harper [documented: Wikipedia]. No scenario beyond MacMillan's one-line summary. Designs Nicholas Georgiadis [documented]. One act, six songs, about an hour [probable for length]. Royal Ballet premiere 19 May 1966, lighting William Bundy [documented: ROH Collections]. Two looks survive: Stuttgart keeps the 1965 colours; the Royal Ballet keeps the 1966 monochrome [documented: MacMillan estate].

### In one line
"A man and a woman; death takes the man; they both return to her," and death turns out to hold "the promise of renewal" [documented: MacMillan's own summary].

### How it came to be
MacMillan, the Royal Ballet's young resident choreographer, asked the Royal Opera House board in 1959 to let him use Mahler's song cycle; he was refused [documented: MacMillan estate; Wikipedia]. He asked again after the success of his *Romeo and Juliet* in February 1965 and was told that Mahler's music was unsuitable for ballet [documented: MacMillan estate]. One board member's reported phrase was "a masterpiece that should not be touched" [documented as reported: *British Theatre Guide*, 2012]. MacMillan took it to his friend John Cranko, who had made Stuttgart an open door for him; they had met as teenagers at the Sadler's Wells school, and Cranko had first pushed MacMillan to choreograph [documented: MacMillan estate; Landgraf 2015]. Cranko gave him first choice of dancers [documented]. Because the songs would be sung in German, the Stuttgart audience would understand the words and see their images in the dance [documented: MacMillan estate]. After glowing reviews by London critics who travelled to Stuttgart, among them John Percival and Andrew Porter, the board gave way, and the Royal Ballet took it six months later [documented: MacMillan estate; Landgraf 2015].

### Roots and reference points
- **Tang poetry** (eighth century) through **Bethge** (1907), a German poet working from earlier European translations; Mahler added lines of his own to the last song, ending on the repeated word *ewig*, "forever" [documented for Mahler's added lines: MacMillan estate; Bethge's intermediaries probable].
- **Mahler's crisis of 1907**: forced from the Vienna Court Opera amid antisemitism, the death of his daughter Maria, a heart diagnosis [documented: ENB note via videotanz].
- **Martha Graham**: her company visited London in 1954 and 1963; MacMillan's floor-bound, contracted shapes owe her a debt [documented: MacMillan estate].
- **Nijinska's *Les Noces*** and Nijinsky's friezes, in the flat-footed, angled group shapes [argued: *British Theatre Guide* 2012]. See `les-noces-1923`.
- **An earlier Mahler ballet**: Antony Tudor's version of the same score for Ballet Theatre (1948) [documented that Tudor made a version: Wikipedia; date and title *Shadow of the Wind* probable]. So "unsuitable for ballet" was already untrue [argued].
- **Postwar theatre**: MacMillan admired John Osborne and wanted to "make people sometimes feel uncomfortable" [documented: MacMillan quoted by ENB].

### The world it built
Six songs, alternating tenor and mezzo: "The Drinking Song of Earth's Sorrow", "The Lonely One in Autumn", "Of Youth", "Of Beauty", "The Drunkard in Spring", and "The Farewell", which is about as long as the other five together [probable for titles and proportion]. Three named figures: the Woman (Haydée), the Man (Ray Barra) and *Der Ewige*, the Eternal One, called in London the Messenger of Death (Egon Madsen) [documented: Wikipedia; RBO]. The Messenger is not a villain but a gentle companion present in every song [documented: Wikipedia]. The Woman is isolated from her friends; the Man and his group do not know they are mortal [documented: Wikipedia]. The movement mixes classical line with flexed feet, angled knees, contractions and "earthbound, non-classical movements" [documented: Wikipedia quoting a review; *BTG*]. German critics compared it to Viennese *Jugendstil*, the art nouveau of Mahler's own time [documented: MacMillan estate]. In "The Farewell" the three walk forward together, slowly, as the music repeats *ewig* and fades [probable].

### Design and art
Georgiadis (1923–2001), a Greek-born painter who designed most of MacMillan's big ballets, first made floating chiffon costumes with long sleeves [documented: MacMillan estate; Staatsoper Munich list]. After the dress rehearsal he and MacMillan threw them out and had the Stuttgart wardrobe dye simple tunics, T-shirts and tights in bluey-green and purple [documented]. *Der Ewige* wore dark grey with a flesh-coloured half-mask [documented]. The backdrop was a plain cyclorama whose colour moved through blues to a pale green-yellow [documented]. For London in 1966 Georgiadis turned it to monochrome: grey to black sky, a cast in greys, the Woman in white, the Messenger in black [documented]. The half-mask is the one theatrical object in an otherwise bare world [argued]. The ROH Collections hold five costumes and one piece of technical equipment from the production [documented: record].

### Premiere and reception
Haydée, Barra and Madsen in Stuttgart [documented]. It was "an immediate success" with German audiences and critics [documented: RBO, the company's words]. In London, 19 May 1966: Haydée as guest, Donald MacLeary and Anthony Dowell [documented]. Percival and Porter called it a masterstroke [documented as paraphrase: Landgraf 2015]. Money and audience figures: not found in this pass.

### Versions and lineage
- Stuttgart 1965 (colour); Royal Ballet 1966 (monochrome) and many revivals, staged by Monica Mason and Grant Coyle [documented: ROH Collections].
- MacMillan refused a Vienna staging in 1977; his letter giving reasons is in the Royal Academy of Dance archive [documented: RAD/2/MAC/7/36/1].
- Darcey Bussell's farewell performance, broadcast live on BBC Two in 2007, with Carlos Acosta as the Messenger and Gary Avis as the Man [documented: Wikipedia; RAD archive file of reviews].
- Scottish Ballet 2011; English National Ballet first danced it in 2017 [documented: *BTG*; videotanz/ENB].
- Other choreographers on the score: Heinz Spoerli; John Neumeier (Paris Opéra, 2015); Anne Teresa De Keersmaeker and Jérôme Bel's *3Abschied* on "The Farewell" [documented: Wikipedia; Landgraf 2015; *BTG*].
- MacMillan's works are kept in Benesh notation as well as on film (see `scope2/G4`).

### Readings and disputes
- **Death as renewal.** MacMillan's own summary, quoted by the RBO and his estate, reads the ending as hope [documented].
- **Death as discomfort.** English National Ballet's notes set it among MacMillan's "darker territory", the theatre of "angry young men" [documented]. On this reading, it belongs with his later psychological ballets, not with consolation.
- **East, or a European idea of East.** The *British Theatre Guide* (2012) says the songs are "true to the oriental sensibility", which MacMillan "makes visible" [documented]. Against this, the Chinese source reached Mahler through Bethge's German paraphrases, not from Chinese [probable], so the ballet's "China" is a twice-translated European image (thread T05) [argued].
- **Unsuitable music.** The ROH board judged Mahler unsuited to dance; Tudor had set the same score in 1948 and MacMillan's success settled the question [argued from the record].

### Facts worth telling
- The Royal Opera House refused it twice, then bought it [documented].
- The original chiffon costumes were thrown away after the dress rehearsal [documented].
- Death wears a half-mask [documented].
- Two colour schemes exist, one per company [documented].
- Mahler avoided calling it his Ninth Symphony, a superstition told of him [documented as a story: Landgraf 2015].
- Bussell chose it for her farewell [documented].

### Set it beside
- **`jewels-1967`** (this batch): another plotless ballet on great music, but celebratory and costumed in luxury.
- **`dances-at-a-gathering-1969`** (this batch): a plotless ensemble that is warm and social, where MacMillan's is elegiac.
- **`les-noces-1923`** (W2 §7): Nijinska's earthbound friezes, which MacMillan's groups echo.
- **`sacre-bausch-1975`** (this batch): a later German stage where the earth is literal.

### What a maker can take from it
- When the dress rehearsal shows the costumes are wrong, throw them out; simpler is often truer.
- One emblematic object (a half-mask) can carry an abstract world.
- If your home institution says no, take the idea somewhere that will say yes; success can bring it home.

### Beyond the stage
BBC Two live broadcast, 2007 [documented]. *Das Lied* itself is widely recorded; the ballet has made "The Farewell" familiar to dance audiences [probable].

## Jewels / Jewels (1967)
`id: jewels-1967` · threads: T10, T13 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 13 April 1967, New York State Theater, New York City Ballet; conductor Robert Irving, pianist Gordon Boelzner [documented: Balanchine Catalogue no. 358]. Choreography George Balanchine. Music: *Emeralds*, Gabriel Fauré (from incidental music for *Pelléas et Mélisande*, 1898, and *Shylock*, 1889); *Rubies*, Igor Stravinsky (*Capriccio* for piano and orchestra, 1929); *Diamonds*, Tchaikovsky (Symphony No. 3 in D, 1875, first movement omitted) [documented]. No scenario. Scenery Peter Harvey, built by Feller Scenery Studios; costumes Barbara Karinska; lighting Ronald Bates [documented]. Three parts with two intervals, about 2 hours including intervals; 81 minutes of dancing; 66 dancers [documented: NYCB; George Balanchine Trust]. NYCB's current sets are Harvey's 2004 redesign (Robin Wagner's of 1983 came between) [documented: NYCB].

### In one line
Three gem colours, three composers and three national schools of ballet (France, America, Russia), held together by costume rather than story.

### How it came to be
The programme note by Lincoln Kirstein, NYCB's co-founder, says Balanchine was introduced to the jeweller Claude Arpels by the violinist Nathan Milstein "over fifteen years ago" in Europe, and that the idea grew "after a visit by Mr. Balanchine to Van Cleef & Arpels' Fifth Avenue Salons" in early 1966 [documented: quoted by PNB]. NYCB's own later story dates the first meeting to 1939 [documented: NYCB 2019]. Balanchine said, "I have always liked jewels; after all, I am an Oriental, from Georgia in the Caucasus" [documented: Balanchine Trust]. He considered pearls and sapphires too, with Schoenberg for sapphire, but "the color of sapphire is so hard to get across on stage" [documented: NYCB; PNB]. The ballet opened without an overall title: the three parts were billed separately on a programme that ended with *Prodigal Son* [documented: PNB, Doug Fullington]. Pittsburgh Ballet Theatre calls it "a box office sensation" [documented as that company's claim]. Balanchine kept insisting, "The ballet has nothing to do with jewels, the dancers are just dressed like jewels" [documented: NYCB 2019].

### Roots and reference points
- **Petipa's jewel dances** in Balanchine's home theatre: the gold, silver, sapphire and diamond fairies in *The Sleeping Beauty* (1890); emerald and ruby pages in *The Nutcracker* (1892); pink, black, white and yellow pearls in *The Pearl* (1896) [documented: PNB].
- **His own *Le Palais de cristal*** (Paris Opéra, 1947, later *Symphony in C*), each movement a jewel colour, costumes by the surrealist Leonor Fini [documented: NYCB; PNB, which lists ruby, black diamond, emerald and pearl].
- **Van Cleef & Arpels**, Fifth Avenue, and Claude Arpels personally [documented].
- **Three schools**: French Romantic ballet; Balanchine's American neoclassicism with Stravinsky; the Imperial Russian Mariinsky [documented: Balanchine Trust; PBT].
- **Mary Clarke and Clement Crisp**: "If the entire imperial Russian inheritance of ballet were lost, *Diamonds* would still tell us of its essence" [documented: quoted by Balanchine Trust].

### The world it built
*Emeralds* (about 31 minutes): two leading couples, three soloists, ten women; the "walking" pas de deux and the Sicilienne made on Mimi Paul; long tulle, slow arms, Balanchine's "evocation of France... elegance, comfort, dress, perfume" [documented: Wikipedia; Balanchine Trust; PBT]. In 1976 Balanchine added a pas de deux and a closing pas de sept [documented: Wikipedia].

*Rubies* (about 19 minutes): McBride and Villella, a tall solo woman (Patricia Neary), eight women and four men; jazzy, hip-thrusting, with a chase in which the corps men run after the lead "like a pack of boys on the street" [documented: PNB]. Many read it as America; Balanchine did not say so [documented: PBT]. It is the part most often danced alone [documented: PNB].

*Diamonds* (about 31 minutes): Farrell and d'Amboise, four demi-solo couples, twelve women and twelve men; a white adagio and a polonaise finale for the whole company [documented: Balanchine Catalogue; PBT].

The rule is a hierarchy of scale: from intimate French reverie, to a small, quick American cast, to a Russian imperial army of 34 [argued from the casts].

### Design and art
Barbara Karinska (born Varvara Zhmoudsky, Kharkiv, 1886) ran the shop that made almost all NYCB costumes (see `scope/03`). She gave each part its own silhouette: calf-length green tulle "Romantic" skirts for *Emeralds*; short red skirts flared at the hips, for men and women alike, for *Rubies*; the flat, white classical tutu of the Imperial ballet for *Diamonds* [documented: Wikipedia; Catalogue: "green... red... white"]. The trims imitate stones that would have been "too heavy for the dancers to wear" [documented: Balanchine]. She said: "I sew for girls and boys who make my costumes dance; their bodies deserve my clothes" [documented: quoted by Wikipedia]. Peter Harvey's set is a series of hanging, faceted shapes like gem settings [probable]. Some costumes have been shown in museums and theatre lobbies [documented: Wikipedia].

### Premiere and reception
Original casts: *Emeralds*, Violette Verdy and Conrad Ludlow, Mimi Paul and Francisco Moncion, with Sara Leland, Suki Schorer and John Prinz; *Rubies*, Patricia McBride, Edward Villella, Patricia Neary; *Diamonds*, Suzanne Farrell and Jacques d'Amboise [documented: Catalogue]. Critics and audiences read the three parts as France, America and Russia; Balanchine accepted the reading but refused to describe the dances: "they have no literary content at all" [documented: NYCB 2019]. Money figures: not found in this pass.

### Versions and lineage
- Licensed widely: Paris Opéra 1974, Dutch National Ballet 1977, Zurich 1980, East Berlin 1982, Royal Danish Ballet 1985, San Francisco 1987 and many more [documented: Catalogue staging list]. Mariinsky from 30 October 1999 [documented: Mariinsky]; Royal Ballet from 23 November 2007 [documented: Wikipedia photo caption].
- In 2017, at Lincoln Center, the Bolshoi, Paris Opéra and NYCB shared the evening, each company taking sections in turn [documented in outline: *New Yorker*, "'Jewels' Three Ways", 31 July 2017; details probable].
- Many companies make new designs under licence: PNB's of 2017 by Jérôme Kaplan [documented: PNB].
- The Balanchine Foundation's Interpreters Archive filmed Mimi Paul, Verdy, McBride, Villella and others coaching the roles (2003–2019) [documented: Catalogue].

### Readings and disputes
- **The earliest full-length plotless ballet.** NYCB says it "has been considered the first three-act story-less ballet" [documented]. The Balanchine Catalogue says "each section is a separate work" [documented]. On the first reading it is a single evening-long abstract work; on the second it is three ballets sharing a theme. The register already flags the claim; "earliest known" is the safe form.
- **About jewels, or about dancing.** NYCB argues "the jewel motif... is actually a device to unify sections that would otherwise be disparate" [documented]. The Mariinsky's programme argues the opposite surprise: that Balanchine, after thirty years of "pure dance", made "a ballet about precious stones in the spirit of the most archaic possible allegories" [documented]. Both see the jewels; they disagree about whether they are packaging or content.
- **A brand ballet?** The work's origin in a visit to a luxury shop makes it an early case of a ballet tied to a commercial name [argued]. No evidence of payment by Van Cleef & Arpels in 1967 was found in this pass.
- **Contradictions.** The first Arpels meeting: "over fifteen years" before 1966 (Kirstein, 1967) or 1939 (NYCB, 2019). The *Palais de cristal* colours: ruby, black diamond, emerald, pearl (PNB) or red, green, blue, white (NYCB). Running times: 81 minutes of dance (Trust) against about two hours with intervals (NYCB).

### Facts worth telling
- A fourth part, *Sapphires* to Schoenberg, was considered and dropped [documented].
- The ballet first had no title [documented: PNB].
- *Rubies* gives the male lead a famous burst of steps; one company says 100 in 40 seconds [documented as PBT's claim].
- Karinska's costumes are imitation stones, because real ones are too heavy [documented].
- Each section uses a composer from a different country and century [documented].

### Set it beside
- **`agon-1957`** (W2 §11): the same company stripped to practice clothes; *Jewels* is the dressed opposite.
- **`les-sylphides-1909`**: the earlier plotless "white" ballet; *Emeralds* is often read as Balanchine's homage to that Romantic world [argued].
- **`song-of-the-earth-1965`** (this batch): plotless, but about death; *Jewels* is about pleasure.
- **`sleeping-beauty-1890`**: the Petipa source of the jewel fairies and the *Diamonds* polonaise style.

### What a maker can take from it
- A single sensory theme (gem colour) can unify parts that share nothing else.
- Use the theme as a frame, not a script: three styles stay three, and the theme makes them a set.
- A shop window is a legitimate source; say so in the credits, as Kirstein did.

### Beyond the stage
Van Cleef & Arpels has long made ballerina brooches and clips and has since sponsored dance [probable]. The *Jewels* name and look are used in fashion shoots and jewellery advertising [probable].

- George Balanchine Foundation, *Balanchine Catalogue* no. 358, https://balanchine.org/balanchine-catalogue/catalogue-of-works/358-jewels-1967/ [documented].
- George Balanchine Trust, https://www.balanchine.com/Ballet/jewels [documented].
- NYCB repertory page, http://www.nycballet.com/discover/ballet-repertory/jewels ; "Jewels: Bringing Gems to Life", 2019, http://nycballet.com/discover/stories/bringing-gems-to-life [documented].
- Doug Fullington, "A Historical Look at George Balanchine's Jewels", PNB, 2021, https://www.pnb.org/blog/a-historical-look-at-george-balanchines-jewels/ ; PNB repertory, https://www.pnb.org/repertory/jewels [documented].

## Dances at a Gathering / Dances at a Gathering (1969)
`id: dances-at-a-gathering-1969` · threads: T10, T14 · confidence of the header: documented (lighting credit contested)

**Header.** Gala preview 8 May 1969; premiere 22 May 1969, New York State Theater, New York City Ballet [documented: Wikipedia; NYCB]. Choreography Jerome Robbins. Music Frédéric Chopin, 18 solo piano pieces (mazurkas, waltzes, études, a scherzo and a nocturne), played on stage-side piano [documented: NYCB credits]. No scenario. Costumes Joe Eula. Lighting Thomas Skelton at the premiere [documented: *NYT* review, 23 May 1969; Wikipedia]; NYCB and the Royal Ballet now credit Jennifer Tipton [documented: NYCB; RBO]. One act, about an hour, ten dancers [documented]. The version seen today is Robbins's, kept by the Robbins Rights Trust [probable for the trust].

### In one line
Ten young people meet in an open space, dance with and for each other to Chopin, and at the end look up at the sky.

### How it came to be
Robbins had left NYCB in the 1950s for Broadway, where he made *West Side Story* (1957) and *Fiddler on the Roof* (1964) [documented: PNB]. The length of the gap is given as 13 years (NYCB, RBO), 12 (PNB programme) or about 10 (PNB blog) [documented, all three]. In 1969 Kirstein invited him back, and he planned a pas de deux for Patricia McBride and Edward Villella [documented: NYCB 2019; RBO]. He kept adding Chopin and dancers. Worried at 25 minutes that it was too long, he showed it to Balanchine, who said, "Make more, make it like peanuts" [documented: NYCB 2019; PNB blog]. Robbins later told reporters "popcorn", miming a moviegoer [documented: PNB blog]. NYC Ballet dancers marked steps in daytime rehearsals to save energy for evening shows; Robbins watched Villella mark his solo and said, "Now that's what I want" [documented: PNB blog]. He dedicated the ballet to the lighting designer Jean Rosenthal, who died on 1 May 1969 [documented: Wikipedia; PNB]. After the premiere Balanchine came backstage and kissed him on both cheeks [documented: PNB blog].

### Roots and reference points
- **Chopin's Polish dances** (mazurkas) heard through Paris salon elegance; Robbins wrote that Chopin was "rough & painful & loving... full of stabbing notes and kissing notes" [documented: Robbins's journal, quoted by NYCB 2019].
- **Folk dance**: heel-toe steps, stamps, linked arms, a sense of village celebration [documented: NYCB 2019].
- **Fokine's *Les Sylphides*** (1909), the earlier one-act Chopin ballet, here turned from moonlit spirits to daylight people [argued]. See `les-sylphides-1909`.
- **Robbins's own *Afternoon of a Faun*** (1953) and Broadway ensemble work, where ordinary behaviour becomes dance [probable].
- **1969 itself**: Robbins called it "a revolt from the faddism today... what's the matter with love, what's the matter with celebrating positive things?" [documented: quoted by PNB].

### The world it built
The structure is a string of eighteen numbers for changing groupings: solos, duets, trios, a sextet, a finale [documented: NYCB credits; Wikipedia]. It opens with a man alone (the Man in Brown, Villella), walking, remembering a dance [documented: Wikipedia]. Midway, a woman who has not yet appeared dances a solo [documented]. In a sextet, three women are tossed from man to man [documented]. The best-known part is the pas de deux of the Pink Girl (McBride) and the Mauve Boy [documented]. Verdy's Green Girl solo, coquettish and posed, keeps her stamp [documented: NYCB 2019]. No more than six dancers are on stage at once until the finale [documented: Wikipedia]. At the end all ten stand still, watching something cross the sky, touch the ground, and walk off in pairs [probable]. The dancers are named only by costume colour [documented]. Robbins wrote that the ballet "stays and exists in the time of the music" [documented: NYCB].

### Design and art
Joe Eula (1925–2004), a fashion illustrator who a year later became creative director at Halston, chose the colours that became the dancers' names: for the women apricot, pink, mauve, blue and green; for the men mustard, olive, blue-green, plum and brown [documented: NYCB 2019]. The men wear loose blouses, tights and calf-high boots; the women "flimsy, misty dresses" [documented: *NYT* review 1969]. The stage is bare with a sky backdrop; the 1969 review praised Skelton's lighting for its "exultation" [documented: *NYT*]. The look is a Polish summer field seen through New York fashion [argued]. Images: Martha Swope's rehearsal photographs with McBride, Villella and Gelsey Kirkland (NYPL, rights reserved) [documented: PNB blog caption].

### Premiere and reception
Allegra Kent, Sara Leland, Kay Mazzo, Patricia McBride, Violette Verdy; Anthony Blum, John Clifford, Robert Maiorano, John Prinz, Edward Villella [documented: NYCB]. Clive Barnes (*New York Times*): "as honest as breathing, as graceful as lark song" [documented: quoted by PNB blog]. *The New Yorker* (31 May 1969): "my idea of what choreography is all about" [documented; critic not named in the excerpt]. So many stories were read into it that Robbins wrote to *Ballet Review* in capitals: "THERE ARE NO STORIES... THE DANCERS ARE THEMSELVES DANCING WITH EACH OTHER TO THAT MUSIC IN THAT SPACE" [documented: *NYT* 2016; NYCB 2019].

### Versions and lineage
- Royal Ballet 1970, staged by Robbins and brought in by Kenneth MacMillan; cast included Nureyev in Villella's role, whom Robbins tried to make "unrecognizable" [documented: Wikipedia; RBO]. Danced to 1976, revived 2008 [documented].
- Paris Opéra Ballet 1991, staged by Robbins [documented: Wikipedia]; Verdy coached Isabelle Guérin there in 1977 (date as given) [documented: NYCB 2019].
- San Francisco Ballet 2002; Pacific Northwest Ballet 2009 [documented].
- Followed by Robbins's own Chopin works *In the Night* (1970) and *Other Dances* (1976) [documented: PNB blog].

### Readings and disputes
- **No story.** Robbins's letter is the strongest statement: no plots, no roles [documented]. Many critics take him at his word and read it as pure dance, near to Balanchine [documented: *The Nation*, 2008, notes the letter "sounds very much like a Balanchinean idea"].
- **Hidden stories.** *The Nation* (2008) says Robbins did not coach that way: "Robbins was not one to leave interpretations to chance" [documented]. Dancers report that the steps "told a tale" (Guérin) [documented: NYCB 2019]. *The New York Times* (2016) calls it "a multilayered masterpiece of theater" in which "nobody speaks, is betrayed, dies or goes mad" [documented]. On this reading, it is full of small dramas he refused to name.
- **The sky at the end.** Some read the final upward gaze as a passing cloud or bird; others as a shadow over the period (Vietnam, the end of the 1960s) [probable as reported readings; no named source found this pass].
- **Contradictions.** Peanuts or popcorn; 10, 12 or 13 years away; Skelton or Tipton for the lighting. The 1969 review settles Skelton for the premiere [documented].

### Facts worth telling
- It began as a duet [documented].
- Balanchine's advice was about snacks [documented].
- The dancers are named after their costume colours [documented].
- It is dedicated to a lighting designer [documented].
- Robbins used dancers' marking (half-performed steps) as a style [documented].
- Its costume designer went straight to Halston [documented].

### Set it beside
- **`les-sylphides-1909`**: Chopin in moonlight and white; Robbins's Chopin in daylight and colour.
- **`jewels-1967`** (this batch): two years earlier at the same house, plotless but grand and costumed by rank.
- **`push-comes-to-shove-1976`** (this batch): another American's use of casual behaviour inside classical steps.
- **`fancy-free-1944`**: Robbins's earliest ballet, everyday people with a story; here everyday people without one.

### What a maker can take from it
- Let a small idea grow by adding only what the material asks for; the hour came from a duet.
- Ordinary behaviour (walking, marking, looking) can be the most moving part of a formal work.
- Naming roles by colour keeps a world open: the audience supplies the people.

### Beyond the stage
Joe Eula's link from ballet to Halston's fashion [documented]. A televised excerpt by Tiler Peck and Jared Angle honoured McBride at the 2014 Kennedy Center Honors [documented: Wikipedia].

## Manon / Manon (1974)
`id: manon-1974` · threads: T18, T15, T24 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 7 March 1974, Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, London, the Royal Ballet [documented: RBO; ABT]. Choreography and scenario Kenneth MacMillan, after the Abbé Prévost's novel *L'Histoire du Chevalier des Grieux et de Manon Lescaut* (1731). Music Jules Massenet, none of it from his opera *Manon* (1884), compiled and orchestrated by Leighton Lucas with the company pianist Hilda Gaunt; re-orchestrated by Martin Yates (credited by ABT from 2008, by the Royal Ballet now) [documented: MacMillan estate; ABT]. Sets and costumes Nicholas Georgiadis [documented]. Three acts, seven scenes, about 2 hours 10 minutes of film running time [documented: Opus Arte DVD length 131 min]. The version seen today is the Royal Ballet's, with cuts made at the start of Act III after the first season [documented: MacMillan estate].

### In one line
A girl on her way to a convent chooses love with a poor student, then money with a rich man, then love again, and dies of it in a Louisiana swamp.

### How it came to be
MacMillan became director of the Royal Ballet in 1970. His first three-act work as director, *Anastasia* (1971), was attacked for its structure, so for the next he chose a familiar "operatic" story [documented: MacMillan estate; Jann Parry in Playbill]. The designer Nicholas Georgiadis proposed Prévost [documented: Parry; Voices of British Ballet]. MacMillan had enjoyed film versions, and Jean Aurel's *Manon 70* (1968), with Catherine Deneuve, fired him [documented: Voices of British Ballet]. He was told to avoid Puccini's *Manon Lescaut*, then in the Royal Opera's repertoire [documented: MacMillan estate]. On the last night of the 1973 summer season Antoinette Sibley found a book in her dressing room with a note: "Some holiday reading for you... which will come in handy for March 7, '74" [documented: Parry]. The volume held both Prévost's *Manon* and Mérimée's *Carmen*, and she had to send Anthony Dowell to ask which [documented]. As always, MacMillan began with the pas de deux [documented]. Then Sibley's knee gave way, and he made much of the role on Jennifer Penney: Manon's Act I entrance, the Act II scene in the brothel, all of Act III, and the "death-defying lifts" of the swamp duet [documented: Parry]. Sibley came back in time for the first night [documented].

### Roots and reference points
- **Prévost's novel** (1731), told by Des Grieux as a confession [documented for the novel; narrator probable].
- **Operas**: Massenet's *Manon* (1884) and Puccini's *Manon Lescaut* (1893), both avoided [documented].
- **Film**: *Manon 70* (1968) [documented]; Henri-Georges Clouzot's *Manon* (1949), the story moved to postwar France [probable].
- **Eighteenth-century pictures**: the ratcatcher and the girl dressed as a pretty boy come from period paintings and etchings [documented: MacMillan estate]. Hogarth's prints of London low life are a likely model [probable].
- **MacMillan's own *Romeo and Juliet*** (1965), with Georgiadis, the model of a crowded Royal Ballet drama [documented: Opus Arte review on the shared palette].
- **MacMillan's theory of the heroine**: "Manon is not so much afraid of being poor as ashamed of being poor. Poverty in that period was the equivalent of long, slow death" [documented: quoted by the MacMillan estate].

### The world it built
Act I: an inn yard near Paris, full of actresses, gentlemen, beggars and the ratcatcher; Manon arrives on her way to a convent; her brother Lescaut plans to sell her to the rich Monsieur G.M.; she runs away with the student Des Grieux; their bedroom in Paris, where Lescaut and G.M. find them and she leaves with G.M. [documented: estate; ABT synopsis]. Act II: a party at the *hôtel particulier* of Madame, a high-class brothel; Manon wears G.M.'s jewels and is passed among the men above the floor; Des Grieux cheats at cards; Lescaut is shot [documented: Parry; *Spectator*, 23 March 1974]. Act III: New Orleans, where Manon arrives as a deported prostitute; the gaoler "forces himself on Manon"; Des Grieux kills him; the swamp, where she hallucinates her past and dies in his arms [documented: estate].

The movement grammar is MacMillan's: duets as the arias of the story, with lifts that throw the woman round the man's body [documented: Parry, "pas de deux taking the place of lyrical duets"]. Signature passages: the Act I "bedroom" pas de deux; Lescaut's drunken solo and duet with his mistress; the Act II pas d'action of Manon carried overhead; the swamp pas de deux, in which she is flung into the air [documented: RBO; *Spectator*; Voices of British Ballet]. The music is assembled from about a dozen Massenet operas and orchestral suites; music from his oratorio *La Vierge* (The Virgin) frames her and plays at her death [documented: ABT list; *Spectator*].

### Design and art
Georgiadis moved the action later in the eighteenth century so that the gap between rich and poor before the Revolution would be sharper [documented: estate; Opus Arte review]. His key device is a towering back wall of hanging rags in the first two acts, half-hidden behind architectural sets [documented: estate]. Jann Parry notes that by lighting alone the rags can "look as velvety as fur or as rank as unwashed clothing" [documented: quoted in Opus Arte review]. The palette is "bloody reds, sickly oranges and faecal browns" [documented: Opus Arte review]. The rich wear satin, lace, bows and jewels; the poor wear tatters [documented]. Arlene Croce (probable author), in *The New Yorker*: "The rags are beautiful, as stage rags always are" [documented: *New Yorker*, 27 May 1974; author probable]. Other companies' designers, for example Peter Farmer for ABT in 2014, "have been less specific about the ballet's social context" [documented: estate; ABT].

### Premiere and reception
Sibley (Manon), Dowell (Des Grieux), David Wall (Lescaut), Derek Rencher (G.M.), Monica Mason (Lescaut's Mistress), David Drew (Gaoler), Gerd Larsen (Madame) [documented: ABT]. The audience was "ecstatic" [documented: Opus Arte review]. Critics were mixed. Mary Clarke (*The Guardian*): "Basically, Manon is a slut and Des Grieux is a fool" [documented: estate]. Jane King (*Morning Star*): "a nasty little diamond digger" [documented]. The *Spectator* critic mocked the score as proof that Massenet "wrote the same music whether his heroine was... Manon or the Virgin Mary" [documented]. Andrew Porter (*Financial Times*) predicted it would "reward repeated observation and generations of performers" [documented: estate]. Most found it too long [documented].

### Versions and lineage
- Royal Ballet 1974; cuts after the first season [documented].
- Royal Ballet video 1982 with Penney, Dowell and Wall [documented: estate].
- American Ballet Theatre from 28 May 1993 (Alessandra Ferri, Julio Bocca) [documented: ABT]. Paris Opéra Ballet also dances it, with Georgiadis's designs [documented: Staatsoper Munich Georgiadis list].
- Royal Ballet relays and DVDs to 2024 (Natalia Osipova, Reece Clarke) [documented: Opus Arte].
- Kept in Benesh notation; the estate insists on staging from the score [documented in `scope2/G4`].

### Readings and disputes
- **A moral tale of a bad girl.** Clarke (1974) and King (1974) read Manon as greedy and empty [documented]. The Opus Arte reviewer (2024) calls this a "Mary Whitehouse-style morality" [documented]. On this reading, the ballet's heroine lacks a motive.
- **A tale of power and poverty.** MacMillan placed her motive in shame of poverty [documented]. Voices of British Ballet argues the ballet "is surely about power: sexual, financial, and political" [documented]. On this reading, every character is trading.
- **Woman as object.** Manon is passed from man to man over their heads, and assaulted by the gaoler [documented]. This can be read as MacMillan's exposure of a world that uses women, or as a ballet that makes their use its spectacle [argued; see scope files on gender and MacMillan].
- **A myth.** MacMillan "liked to deny" that critics were positive, but most praised the choreography [documented: Opus Arte review; estate].

### Facts worth telling
- None of Massenet's *Manon* is in it [documented].
- Much of the title role was made on a second ballerina while the first was injured [documented].
- The swamp lifts were invented with Penney and then learned by Sibley [documented].
- The back wall is made of rags [documented].
- The ballet moves Prévost's story about half a century later [documented].
- A book left in a dressing room started it [documented].

### Set it beside
- **`miss-julie-1950`** (this batch): another woman destroyed by class and desire, without the swamp.
- **`lady-of-camellias-1978`** (this batch): Neumeier puts Manon on stage as a ballet-within-the-ballet, read by Marguerite.
- **`giselle-1841`**: both heroines go mad or delirious; Giselle forgives in death, Manon only remembers.
- **`mayerling-1978`** (this batch): MacMillan's next three-act drama, with the same designer.

### What a maker can take from it
- Give your world a single material that states its theme (a wall of rags) and let lighting change its meaning.
- Moving the period can sharpen the idea: Georgiadis put Prévost nearer the Revolution.
- Work with what happens: an injury forced a second dancer into the role, and the role became richer.

### Beyond the stage
Royal Ballet cinema relays and DVDs (Opus Arte) [documented]. The story's long film life (Clouzot 1949, *Manon 70*) fed the ballet [documented for *Manon 70*].

- Jann Parry, "The Creation of a Classic", Playbill, 30 July 2015, https://playbill.com/article/the-creation-of-a-classic-the-history-of-kenneth-macmillans-manon ; Parry, *Different Drummer: The Life of Kenneth MacMillan* (Faber, 2009) [documented].
- RBO, https://www.rbo.org.uk/production/manon-kenneth-macmillan?mode=cinema [documented].

- Opus Arte (MusicWeb International review, 2024), https://www.opusarte.com/news-and-reviews/article/Kenneth_MacMillans_Manon_Review_by_MusicWeb_International [documented].

## Das Frühlingsopfer / The Rite of Spring (1975)
`id: sacre-bausch-1975` · threads: T13, T16, T24 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 3 December 1975, Opernhaus Wuppertal, Tanztheater Wuppertal, as the last part of a three-part Stravinsky evening titled *Frühlingsopfer*, after *Wind von West* (to the *Cantata*) and *Der zweite Frühling* [documented: Pina Bausch Foundation evening leaflet; Bausch company site]. Choreography and direction Pina Bausch. Music Igor Stravinsky, *Le Sacre du printemps* (1913), on tape at first, live in later revivals [probable for tape]. Set and costumes Rolf Borzik; collaboration Hans Pop [documented]. One act, 35 minutes [documented]. Since 1978 usually danced in a double bill with *Café Müller* [documented: ENB programme 2017]. The version seen today is the Pina Bausch Foundation's, staged by former Wuppertal dancers [documented].

### In one line
On a stage deep in brown peat, a group of frightened women and men pass a red dress from hand to hand until one woman must wear it and dance until she dies.

### How it came to be
Pina Bausch (1940–2009), trained by Kurt Jooss at the Folkwang school in Essen and in New York, became director of the former Wuppertal Ballet in 1973 and renamed it Tanztheater Wuppertal [documented: ENB programme; Bausch Foundation]. Her first seasons provoked the audience: "Audiences were booing, walking out, slamming doors. An occasional tomato was even thrown on stage," recalled the dancer Josephine Ann Endicott [documented: ENB programme 2017]. Bausch made the *Sacre* while also making dances for operas in the same house, and two dance-operas of Gluck preceded it, *Iphigenie auf Tauris* (1974) and *Orpheus und Eurydike* (1975) [documented: Bausch Foundation; *ArtsJournal*]. Her partner since 1970, the designer Rolf Borzik, designed every piece until his death in 1980 [documented: Bausch Foundation; *ArtsJournal*]. In rehearsal Bausch searched for movement that was "earthy, ideally"; Endicott remembers that "very often it was the simpler version which proved better" [documented: ENB programme]. The evening's first two parts are now rarely danced; the *Sacre* was "quickly recognised as a masterwork" and has been in repertory since [documented: ENB programme].

### Roots and reference points
- **Nijinsky's *Sacre*** (Paris, 1913), and its scenario of a pagan Russian sacrifice by Nicholas Roerich and Stravinsky (`rite-of-spring-1913`, W2 §5) [documented].
- **Earlier remakes**: Mary Wigman (1957) and Maurice Béjart (1959), the German and the Brussels versions that had already made the *Sacre* a choreographer's test piece [documented in W2 §5; register `sacre-bejart-1959`].
- **German *Ausdruckstanz*** (expressionist dance), passed to Bausch through Jooss; Borzik and Bausch held that pieces must stay "close to reality", which the Bausch Foundation calls "an heritage of Ausdruckstanz" redefined for the present [documented: Bausch Foundation].
- **Gluck dance-operas** (1974–75), where Bausch and Borzik learned to strip the stage to what is needed [documented].

### The world it built
The structure follows Stravinsky's two parts: the Adoration of the Earth and the Sacrifice [probable for the mapping]. The women enter first, one lying on the red dress; fear spreads through the group as each woman holds the dress and passes it on [documented in outline: Operabase description; details probable]. The men are a block apart; one man (the first was Jan Minařík) takes the dress to the chosen woman [probable for the role]. Movement rules: deep pliés into the earth, contractions of the torso, arms beating the chest and thrown upward, unison running in a great circle; "the dance is the music. The dancers are the music," says Endicott [documented]. Movements are "masculine and not pretty" at one moment and "extremely soft, sensual and feminine" at another [documented: Endicott]. The ensemble of 26 in 1975 (32 to 36 in later stagings) closes a ring round the ritual space [documented: 1975 leaflet lists 27 dancers; *ArtsJournal* counts 36 in 2017]. The Chosen One, Marlis Alt at the premiere, dances her solo to exhaustion and falls [documented: Operabase for Alt].

### Design and art
Rolf Borzik "tears the stage open to the fire walls" and covers it with a thick layer of peat (*Torf*), "which makes the dancers' steps heavy" [documented: Bausch Foundation]. The men wear plain black trousers and bare chests; the women short, sheer slip dresses [documented]. One costume is coloured: the red dress [documented: Operabase]. The peat sticks to sweating skin, so by the end the bodies are marked by the floor [probable]. The preparation is itself a performance: in the interval, "a small army of black-clad stage technicians" staples a groundcloth, then tips six or eight containers of red-brown peat and rakes it smooth [documented: Tobi Tobias, *ArtsJournal* "Dancebeat", 2017]. Borzik's floors became a Bausch signature: carnations, water, leaves in later pieces [probable]. The design idea is one material, natural and real, chosen so that the dancers cannot pretend [argued, after the Foundation's "close to reality"].

### Premiere and reception
1975 dancers included Marlis Alt (the Chosen One), Josephine Ann Endicott, Jan Minařík, Lutz Förster, Vivienne Newport, Ed Kortlandt and others [documented: leaflet; Operabase]. Critics of the early Bausch years were "often negative" [documented: ENB programme], but the *Sacre* was soon seen as a masterwork [documented]. It toured from 1977: Berlin, Edinburgh 1978, and an Asian tour in 1979 from Hong Kong and Seoul to Jakarta, Manila, Mumbai and Kolkata [documented: Bausch company site].

### Versions and lineage
- Tanztheater Wuppertal, 1975 to now; double bill with *Café Müller* from 1978 [documented].
- Paris Opéra Ballet: taught in 1996 with Endicott assisting [documented: ENB programme]; the register gives 1997 for the company premiere [probable].
- English National Ballet, 23 March 2017, Sadler's Wells, "only the third company to perform it" [documented: ENB programme].
- École des Sables (Senegal) with the Pina Bausch Foundation and Sadler's Wells: 38 dancers from 14 African countries, cancelled on the eve of its Dakar premiere in March 2020; filmed on the beach at Toubab Dialaw as *Dancing at Dusk*; toured from Madrid in 2021 to New York, Tokyo, Shanghai and London, ending at the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées, Paris, in September 2025, fifty years on [documented: *Dance Magazine* 2020; Sadler's Wells]. See `common-grounds-acogny-2021`.
- Performance rights are held for the Foundation by Verlag der Autoren, Frankfurt [documented: ENB programme].

### Readings and disputes
- **A rite of gender.** The Operabase description, following the Foundation's material, reads it as "the intense dynamics between women and men within a community" [documented]. Much feminist writing reads the Chosen One as the woman the group makes a victim [probable; for the Wuppertal method and its power relations see Gabriele Klein, *Pina Bausch's Dance Theater*, 2020, open access, in `scope2/G1`].
- **A rite of collective fear and love.** Original dancers remember "the profound sense of support and love embedded" in the movement [documented: Operabase summary]. Germaine Acogny read it as a ritual shared by Africa and Europe: "it was quite usual to sacrifice young, female virgins to obtain something from the gods" [documented: *Dance Magazine*]. On this reading it is about community, not only about men and women.
- **Ballet or not.** Bausch's company is Tanztheater, not a ballet company, but its *Sacre* is in ballet repertory (Paris, ENB) [documented]. ENB's coaches say classical dancers must "forget all you have learnt before" [documented: Endicott].
- **Contradictions.** Dates for Paris (1996 or 1997); peat containers ("six (or was it eight?)") [documented: *ArtsJournal*]. The register's premise ("chooses a woman by a red dress") is correct in outline [documented].

### Facts worth telling
- The set is a floor of peat, laid live in the interval [documented].
- It began as the last third of an evening whose other parts are now almost unknown [documented].
- Wuppertal audiences threw tomatoes in Bausch's first years [documented: Endicott].
- Its first Chosen One was Marlis Alt [documented].
- An African cast filmed it on a Senegalese beach when the pandemic shut theatres [documented].
- It runs 35 minutes [documented].

### Set it beside
- **`rite-of-spring-1913`** (W2 §5): Roerich's painted Russia against Borzik's real earth.
- **`sacre-bejart-1959`**: Béjart's sacrifice as a mating rite for a couple; Bausch's as a sacrifice of one woman.
- **`revelations-1960`** (this batch): a community rite with water and church, ending in joy, not death.
- **`common-grounds-acogny-2021`**: the African staging and its companion duet.

### What a maker can take from it
- Choose one real material and let it change the bodies on stage; the world is made by its physics.
- One colour against none (a red dress among pale slips) is enough to carry a whole plot.
- Show the making: the peat-laying in the interval turns stagehands into part of the ritual.

### Beyond the stage
Wim Wenders's film *Pina* (2011) uses the *Sacre* [probable]. *Dancing at Dusk* (2020) streamed on Sadler's Wells Digital Stage [documented]. Sadler's Wells' film *The Evolution of an Epic* (2025) [documented].

## Push Comes to Shove / Push Comes to Shove (1976)
`id: push-comes-to-shove-1976` · threads: T10, T11, T13 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 9 January 1976, Uris Theater (now the Gershwin), New York, American Ballet Theatre (ABT); conductor Akira Endo [documented: Twyla Tharp site; ABT]. Choreography Twyla Tharp. Music Joseph Lamb, *Bohemia Rag* (1919), and Joseph Haydn, Symphony No. 82 in C, "The Bear" (1786), arranged by David E. Bourne [documented]. No scenario. Costumes Santo Loquasto; lighting Jennifer Tipton [documented]. One act: a prelude and four movements, 22 minutes 30 seconds; 29 dancers [documented: Tharp site]. Paid for by a gift from Mr and Mrs Robert E. Rubin [documented: original programme, quoted by Tharp site]. The version seen today is ABT's, revived at intervals [probable].

### In one line
The new Soviet star of an American ballet company shrugs, slouches and loses his bowler hat to a ragtime tune, then leads the whole troupe through a Haydn symphony as a cheeky master of ceremonies.

### How it came to be
Mikhail Baryshnikov had defected from the Kirov in Toronto in 1974 and joined ABT [probable]. He wanted to go beyond the classical princes; he had seen Tharp's *Deuce Coupe* and set out to master her "off-kilter, non-balletic style" [documented: *LA Dance Chronicle*, 2025]. ABT invited Tharp, then running her own modern-dance company, to make a work round him [documented]. Tharp's brief to herself was: "Make movement that he'd never done and perhaps nobody had ever done, and let's see if it's doable" [documented: *NYT*, 13 October 2025]. Some ABT dancers were sceptical and asked not to be in it; the steps, such as rotating one way, shifting weight and going the other, were "not a ballet dancer's tendency" [documented: Elaine Kudo, quoted in *LA Dance Chronicle*]. Tharp paired a rag and a symphony to show that both use syncopation [documented: *LA Dance Chronicle*]. The headlines that followed included "Twyla Tharp's Descent Into Slapstick" and "Baryshnikov Is Tharpist" [documented: *LA Dance Chronicle*; *NYT*, 4 July 1976].

### Roots and reference points
- **Tharp's *Deuce Coupe*** (Joffrey Ballet, 1 March 1973), to the Beach Boys, with live graffiti artists painting the backdrop; Gia Kourlas calls it "the first crossover ballet" [documented: *NYT*, 22 May 2019; Playbill 2019]. Arlene Croce called it a "masterpiece" [documented: Playbill].
- **Tharp's *As Time Goes By*** (Joffrey, October 1973), to Haydn, a crossover without pop music [documented: EBSCO].
- **Vaudeville and the soft-shoe**: the prelude is a front-cloth turn by three "entertainers", and the derby hat is a music-hall prop [documented: Croce, *New Yorker*, 26 January 1976].
- **Black American vernacular dance**: Croce describes "Tharpian shrugs, slouches, and pelvis bumps", calling them a "defunct style of black American dancers in blues performance" [documented]. See Brenda Dixon Gottschild on the Africanist presence (`scope/01` §2.4).
- **Haydn's classical symphony**, a court form, set against the rag [documented].

### The world it built
Prelude (Lamb): Baryshnikov, Martine van Hamel and Marianna Tcherkassky in front of a curtain; the women keep stealing his hat [documented: Croce; Tharp site]. Movement I (Vivace assai): the three leads. Movement II (Allegretto): Tcherkassky with a large female corps. Movement III (Menuet e Trio): van Hamel and Clark Tippet with eight dancers. Movement IV (Vivace): the entire ensemble with Christopher Aponte [documented: Tharp site]. The movement language joins ballet's legwork to a loose, rippling torso and casual timing: "hips softly swiveling below an ever-fluid spine" [documented: *NYT* 2025]. Groups break formation, collide and re-form; a 1976 critic saw "brutality" in the way Tippet throws van Hamel away, "a passing comment on stereotyped ballet partnering" [documented: *NYT*, 4 July 1976]. EBSCO reads it as a "droll commentary on Baryshnikov, backstage politics at American Ballet Theatre, ballet conventions, and rehearsal habits" [documented].

### Design and art
Santo Loquasto, who began his long work with Tharp the year after *Deuce Coupe*, designed the costumes; Jennifer Tipton, Tharp's regular lighting designer, lit it [documented: Playbill 2019; Tharp site]. The key prop is the black derby, or bowler, hat, tossed, passed and stolen [documented]. The prelude is played before a front curtain like a vaudeville act [documented: Croce]. Costumes for the Haydn movements dress the corps in versions of classical practice wear and tutus, with Baryshnikov in a loose, casual outfit set apart [probable]. Martha Swope's photographs of the premiere are on the Tharp site (rights reserved) [documented].

### Premiere and reception
Principals Baryshnikov, van Hamel, Tcherkassky; soloists Kristine Elliott, Clark Tippet, Christopher Aponte; an ensemble including Cynthia Harvey and Victor Barbee [documented: Tharp site]. Croce's review was headed "More or Less Terrific" [documented: *New Yorker*, 26 January 1976]. The *Times* (July 1976) found Baryshnikov "certainly much better in his role than his partners", who were "more classical" than he [documented]. Wendy Perron later wrote that it was "when Baryshnikov became an American" [documented: quoted in *LA Dance Chronicle*]. EBSCO: "The popularity of the piece was astounding" [documented]. Box-office figures: not found in this pass.

### Versions and lineage
- ABT 1976; Tharp went on to make more than a dozen works for ABT, an all-Tharp evening in May 1984, and shared the artistic direction with Baryshnikov briefly in the late 1980s [documented: EBSCO; Playbill 2019].
- Televised in *Baryshnikov by Tharp* (PBS, 1984) [probable].
- Danced by other companies under licence from Tharp [probable; list not found in this pass].
- Tharp later used the title for her autobiography, *Push Comes to Shove* (Bantam, 1992) [documented: EBSCO bibliography].

### Readings and disputes
- **The earliest crossover ballet.** Tharp's own site says *Push* "marks the first such work ever presented by a major ballet company" [documented]. Gia Kourlas (*NYT*, 2019) gives the honour to *Deuce Coupe* (Joffrey, 1973) [documented]. The difference turns on whether the Joffrey counts as "major"; *LA Dance Chronicle* calls it "an upstart" next to ABT, "a bastion of classical ballet" [documented]. The register's note needs this correction.
- **Comedy as critique.** EBSCO reads the work as a commentary on ballet's habits and politics [documented]. The 1976 *Times* headline read it as a "Descent Into Slapstick" [documented]. On the first reading the jokes are an argument; on the second they are a lapse.
- **Whose style?** Croce traced Tharp's shrugs to Black American blues dancers [documented]. Gottschild's later work argues that such borrowings into white concert dance are often unnamed [probable; see `scope/01`]. The work itself credits no such source [argued].

### Facts worth telling
- The music jumps from a 1919 rag to a 1786 symphony [documented].
- The hat is the plot [documented].
- Some ABT dancers asked not to be in it [documented: Kudo].
- It lasts 22 and a half minutes [documented].
- A private gift paid for it [documented].
- Its lighting designer, Jennifer Tipton, is one of very few women credited in this batch outside choreography [documented for her credit].

### Set it beside
- **`dances-at-a-gathering-1969`** (this batch): Robbins's casual behaviour inside classical dance, sincere where Tharp is ironic.
- **`parade-1917`** (W2 §6): Cocteau's music-hall entertainers in a ballet, sixty years earlier.
- **`in-the-upper-room-1986`**: Tharp's later ABT work, sneakers and pointe shoes together.
- **`agon-1957`** (W2 §11): another New York work that put Black American movement inside ballet without naming it so.

### What a maker can take from it
- Put the star in a role that shows them doing what they have never done; the strain is the show.
- Two musics with one shared device (syncopation) can be made to argue with each other.
- One small object passed among the cast can structure a plotless world.

### Beyond the stage
Baryshnikov's crossover continued into film (*The Turning Point*, 1977) and television [probable]. Tharp became a Broadway and film choreographer (*Hair*, 1979; *Amadeus*, 1984) [probable].

## Mayerling / Mayerling (1978)
`id: mayerling-1978` · threads: T17, T15 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 14 February 1978, Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, the Royal Ballet, at a Royal Ballet gala; conductor John Lanchbery [documented: ROH Collections performance record]. Choreography Kenneth MacMillan. Music Franz Liszt, some 30 pieces arranged and orchestrated by Lanchbery [documented: MacMillan estate]. Scenario Gillian Freeman, novelist and screenwriter [documented]. Sets and costumes Nicholas Georgiadis; lighting David Hersey, revival lighting John B. Read [documented: ROH Collections]. Three acts with prologue and epilogue [documented: *Oxford Dictionary of Dance*]. Dedicated to Frederick Ashton [documented: Wikipedia]. Benesh master score by Jacquie Hollander and Lyn Vella-Gatt, 1978 [documented: estate]. The version seen today is the Royal Ballet's; Stuttgart dances it in Jürgen Rose's designs [documented: Voices of British Ballet].

### In one line
The last months of an heir to the Habsburg throne, told through his duets with five women, ending with his murder of his teenage mistress and his own suicide in a hunting lodge.

### How it came to be
MacMillan's interest was sparked by George Marek's book on the fall of the Habsburgs, *The Eagles Die* (1974) [documented: estate]. Films had romanticised the 1889 deaths of Crown Prince Rudolf and Baroness Mary Vetsera; MacMillan wanted "the social, political and personal pressures" behind them [documented: estate]. He asked Gillian Freeman for a scenario; she researched "pen portraits" of the court and wrote an outline "very like a film treatment", which he accepted without changes so he could concentrate on the dance [documented: Parry, *Different Drummer*, excerpt]. Her typed "First Draft Outline" and character notes are in the Royal Academy of Dance archive [documented: RAD/2/MAC/3/1/22/2]. Lanchbery, the company's former chief conductor, proposed Liszt, a composer of the same time and empire who had written piano music for Empress Elisabeth [documented: estate]. Anthony Dowell, the first Rudolf, was injured early; David Wall took over in April 1977 and created the role [documented: Parry]. The planned autumn 1977 premiere was postponed [documented]. MacMillan had resigned as the Royal Ballet's director in 1977 [documented: estate]. Parry argues he saw parts of himself in Rudolf: "in effect, the Crown Prince of the Royal Ballet" [documented: Parry excerpt].

### Roots and reference points
- **History**: the Mayerling incident of 30 January 1889; Mary Vetsera was 17 [documented: Wikipedia; *Oxford Dictionary of Dance*].
- **Marek, *The Eagles Die*** (1974) [documented].
- **Films**: Anatole Litvak's *Mayerling* (1936) and Terence Young's (1968) romanticised the story [probable for titles; documented that films existed].
- **Liszt's music**: the *Faust Symphony* as a motif for Rudolf's obsession with guns and death; the *Mephisto Waltz* No. 1; the *Transcendental Études* [documented: estate; ROH].
- **MacMillan's own *Anastasia*** (1971) and *Manon* (1974), the earlier psychological three-act dramas (this batch) [documented].

### The world it built
Prologue and epilogue: the cemetery at Heiligenkreuz before dawn, a coffin lowered in the rain; only at the end does the audience learn it is Mary's, smuggled out by her uncles, her corpse propped between them in hat and coat [documented: estate]. Act I: the Hofburg ball for Rudolf's marriage to Princess Stephanie of Belgium; Rudolf flirts with her sister; then his bedroom, where he terrifies his bride with a revolver and a skull [documented for the ball: Wikipedia; skull and revolver probable]. Act II: a tavern with prostitutes and Hungarian officers, among them his mistress Mitzi Caspar; the Emperor's birthday; Countess Larisch introduces Mary; Mary's bedroom, where Rudolf asks her to die with him [documented in outline: Wikipedia]. Act III: a hunt where Rudolf's gun goes off; the hunting lodge at Mayerling, the cab-driver Bratfisch's song and hat-juggling, morphine, the last duet, the two shots [documented: estate; Wikipedia].

The rule is the duet: Rudolf "is scarcely off the stage" and dances seven major pas de deux with five women (Stephanie, Larisch, Mitzi, his mother Elisabeth and Mary) [documented: estate; VBB]. It was "the first British ballet to make such demands of a male dancer in a leading role" [documented as the estate's claim]. The duets "veer between extremes of desire and hate" [documented: *Oxford Dictionary of Dance*]. The final pas de deux is "sexually frenzied" [documented: estate].

### Design and art
Georgiadis dressed a dark, crowded imperial Vienna: black and gold uniforms, heavy gowns, mourning crêpe [probable]. The cemetery of the prologue, in rain and black umbrellas, frames the whole as a funeral [documented in outline: estate]. Five costumes from the production are in the ROH Collections [documented]. The palette continues *Manon*'s deep reds and browns [probable]. Jürgen Rose redesigned it for Stuttgart [documented: VBB]. The guns, the skull and the morphine syringe are the props that carry the plot [argued].

### Premiere and reception
David Wall (Rudolf), Lynn Seymour (Mary Vetsera), Wendy Ellis (Stephanie), Michael Somes (Franz Josef), Georgina Parkinson (Elisabeth), Merle Park (Larisch), Laura Connor (Mitzi Caspar), Graham Fletcher (Bratfisch), David Drew (Bay Middleton) and a large cast [documented: ROH Collections]. The audience gave "a prolonged ovation" [documented: estate]. Many critics found it too long and the history hard to follow [documented: Wikipedia]. Mary Clarke (*The Guardian*), who had disliked *Manon*, defended it: "patience and understanding bring rewards; every scene tells something" [documented: quoted by Wikipedia]. Voices of British Ballet says many American critics doubted ballet could handle such harsh material [documented]. A television documentary, *MacMillan's Mayerling*, filmed the original cast in 1978 [documented: VBB].

### Versions and lineage
- Royal Ballet 1978 and many revivals (131 performances listed in the ROH database) [documented: ROH Collections, "1 of 131"].
- US premiere at the Shrine Auditorium, Los Angeles, 1978 [documented: RAD archive, Freeman's *LA Times* article, 21 May 1978].
- Video: Irek Mukhamedov, Viviana Durante, Darcey Bussell, Lesley Collier (Opus Arte) [documented: estate].
- MacMillan refused requests to stage it in Vienna in 1988 and 1991; his handwritten reasons survive [documented: RAD/2/MAC/7/36/1].
- MacMillan died of a heart attack backstage at Covent Garden on 29 October 1992, during a revival of *Mayerling* [documented: Wikipedia].

### Readings and disputes
- **A political tragedy.** The estate frames it as the pressures of court, state and family on a man who "could not meet the expectations of those around him" [documented: Parry, via estate]. On this reading, it is about the dying empire.
- **A psychological case study.** The *Oxford Dictionary of Dance* reads Rudolf as "a depraved drug addict, riddled with venereal disease", a typical MacMillan protagonist "outside the bounds of society" [documented]. On this reading, it is about one diseased mind.
- **History against the ballet.** The ballet shows murder then suicide; the estate notes that "rumours of suicide and even assassination" have never stopped [documented]. The ballet takes one side of a live historical dispute.
- **Valentine's Day.** The estate itself calls the date of a murder-suicide premiere "somewhat inappropriate" [documented].

### Facts worth telling
- The hero dances seven pas de deux with five women [documented].
- The first Rudolf was injured and never danced it [documented: Parry].
- The coffin in the prologue holds the heroine, hidden from history [documented].
- The composer once wrote for the empress who appears on stage [documented: estate].
- MacMillan died in the theatre during a performance of this ballet [documented].
- The scenario writer was a novelist who wrote it like a film treatment [documented].

### Set it beside
- **`manon-1974`** (this batch): the same team, a heroine destroyed; here the man is the destroyed centre.
- **`sleeping-beauty-1890`**: the court as celebration; *Mayerling* is the court as poison (T17).
- **`swan-lake-bourne-1995`** (W3 §6): another doomed prince crushed by a royal mother and the press.
- **`onegin-1965`** (this batch): the duet-drama form Cranko built, which MacMillan pushes to its limit.

### What a maker can take from it
- Build the story on one character's relationships, one duet per relationship, and the structure writes itself.
- Frame a complex history with a single image (a coffin in the rain) that the audience understands only at the end.
- Separate the jobs: a writer for the scenario, an arranger for the music, so the choreographer can invent the language.

### Beyond the stage
The 1978 television documentary [documented]. Mayerling remains a tourist site in Austria and a recurring film subject [probable].

- ROH Collections, premiere performance record, http://www.rohcollections.org.uk/performance.aspx?performance=11760&row=0&searchtype=workprodperf&title=Mayerling ; production record, http://rohcollections.org.uk/production.aspx?production=4585&row=0 [documented].

## Die Kameliendame / The Lady of the Camellias (1978)
`id: lady-of-camellias-1978` · threads: T18, T15, T24 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 4 November 1978, Württembergisches Staatstheater, Stuttgart, Stuttgart Ballet [documented: Neumeier Foundation; Stuttgart Ballet; ABT]. Choreography, direction and scenario John Neumeier, after Alexandre Dumas fils, *La Dame aux camélias* (novel, 1848). Music Frédéric Chopin: Piano Concerto No. 2; the Romance from Piano Concerto No. 1; the *Grande Fantaisie* Op. 13; the *Grande Polonaise brillante* Op. 22; solo piano works including the Ballade in G minor Op. 23, Préludes from Op. 28, waltzes, the *Trois Écossaises* Op. 72; and the Largo of the B minor Sonata Op. 58 as a recurring "bracket" [documented: Bavarian State Opera]. Sets and costumes Jürgen Rose; lighting concept Neumeier [documented: ABT]. Three acts with prologue, about three hours with two intervals (acts of 40, 40 and 50 minutes) [documented: Neumeier Foundation]. Revised for the Hamburg Ballet, 1 February 1981 [documented]. Both the Stuttgart and Hamburg lines are danced today [documented].

### In one line
At the auction of a dead courtesan's belongings, her young lover remembers their affair, while the ghosts of Manon Lescaut and Des Grieux dance the same fate beside them.

### How it came to be
John Cranko died suddenly in 1973, and Marcia Haydée, his Brazilian prima ballerina, became director of the Stuttgart Ballet [documented: Stuttgart Ballet blog; probable for the year]. John Neumeier, an American from Milwaukee who had danced in Cranko's company and in 1973 became director of the Hamburg Ballet, "had promised her that he would support the company" [documented: Stuttgart Ballet; probable for his biography]. He had already worked with Haydée on *Antony and Cleopatra* in Stuttgart [documented: taniecPOLSKA, Neumeier at a 2018 press conference]. The new ballet was a vehicle for her as dancer and director: she was both at the premiere [documented: Stuttgart Ballet]. Neumeier chose Chopin, "the musical quintessence of Romanticism", for the story's Romantic love [documented: taniecPOLSKA]. The premiere "elicited a storm of enthusiasm" [documented: the company's words]. Neumeier re-staged it in Hamburg in 1981, with Haydée as guest, and in 1986 directed a film of it, which won a gold medal at the International TV Festival in New York [documented: ABT; Bavarian State Opera; Wiener Staatsoper].

### Roots and reference points
- **Dumas fils, the novel** (1848), with its opening auction and the copy of *Manon Lescaut* that Armand gives Marguerite [documented for the novel's use of Manon: Bavarian State Opera, "a motif from the original novel"; the auction opening probable].
- **Prévost's *Manon Lescaut*** (1731), here a ballet-within-the-ballet [documented].
- **Verdi's *La traviata*** (1853) and the films [documented: Bavarian State Opera].
- **Ashton's *Marguerite and Armand*** (1963, this batch), the 30-minute version, which the register asks us to compare [documented as register note].
- **Cranko's dance drama**: the large duets of *Onegin*, made by the same company and designer [argued].
- **Cinema**: the Bavarian State Opera describes the dramaturgy as "oriented on cinematic structures", with flashbacks from several points of view [documented].

### The world it built
Prologue: March 1847, 9 rue d'Antin; the auction of Marguerite's furniture; her maid Nanina carries the diary; Armand rushes in and collapses into his father's arms, and begins to tell the story [documented: Bavarian State Opera; Neumeier Foundation]. Act I: the Théâtre des Variétés, where a ballet of *Manon Lescaut* is playing; Marguerite, in the audience, recognises herself in Manon and refuses to; Armand fears he is Des Grieux [documented]. The affair; Act II in the country, where Armand's father asks Marguerite to give him up; Act III, the parting, his public insult, her illness, and Manon and Des Grieux appearing as phantoms until "her identification with Manon seems complete"; "Marguerite dies alone. Armand silently closes her diary" [documented: Bavarian State Opera synopsis].

Three structural layers run at once: the present (the auction, marked in italics in the synopsis), memory (Armand's, his father's, Marguerite's), and the inner ballet (Manon) [documented: Bavarian State Opera]. A Polish commentator calls it a "Chinese box" narrative [documented: taniecPOLSKA]. The music divides too: concertos with orchestra for the public world, solo piano for the private [argued]. The great duets for Marguerite and Armand are known by her dress colour, the purple (or lilac), the white and the black pas de deux [probable for the names].

### Design and art
Jürgen Rose, Cranko's designer (see `onegin-1965`), made the sets and about a hundred costumes in 1840s Paris dress [probable for the number]. Marguerite first meets Armand in a violet dress, and the sight of it at the auction sets off his memory [documented: Bavarian State Opera]. Her white country dress is organza, "the most filigreed dress I ever designed", which can tear in the pas de deux, so every company that dances it keeps rolls of the fabric for repairs [documented: Rose, 2017, via Landgraf]. The stage is a shell of a Paris apartment with furniture under dust sheets, auction numbers and a piano, which turns into theatre, ballroom and country house [probable]. The design keeps the auction present as a frame: things count, because she was bought and is now being sold [argued]. A Polish note recalls that in the 1840s the Opéra was "an exposition of girls for sale" [documented as a quoted phrase: taniecPOLSKA].

### Premiere and reception
Marguerite: Marcia Haydée; Armand: Egon Madsen; Manon: Birgit Keil; Des Grieux: Richard Cragun; Prudence: Jean Allenby; Gaston: Vladimir Klos; Nanina: Ruth Papendick; Monsieur Duval: Reid Anderson; Olympia: Nora Kimball; the Duke: Marcis Lesins; Count N.: Mark A. Neal [documented: Neumeier Foundation]. US premiere by Stuttgart, Kennedy Center, 15 May 1979 [documented: ABT]. Critics' quotes from 1978: not found in this pass.

### Versions and lineage
- Stuttgart 1978; Hamburg revision 1981 (Haydée, Kevin Haigen, Lynne Charles, Jean-Christophe Maillot) [documented: ABT].
- Film 1986 (some sources say 1987), directed by Neumeier, with Haydée [documented: Bavarian State Opera says 1986].
- In repertory at ABT, Paris Opéra, Bavarian State Ballet, Bolshoi, La Scala, Dutch National Ballet, Royal Danish Ballet, Korean National Ballet, Polish National Ballet, Vienna State Ballet [documented: Neumeier Foundation], always in Rose's designs [documented: taniecPOLSKA].
- Hamburg tours from Munich (1981) to East Berlin, Buenos Aires, Tokyo and St Petersburg [documented: Hamburg State Opera].
- The rights are held by Neumeier and his foundation; stagings are by his own répétiteurs [probable].

### Readings and disputes
- **A love story deepened by a mirror.** The Bavarian State Opera praises "unprecedented dramaturgical finesse" and sees the Manon layer as reflecting the lovers' "wretched fate" [documented]. On this reading, the inner ballet is a device of depth.
- **A story about the market in women.** The taniecPOLSKA commentary reads the theatre setting as "a double wink": Manon is a courtesan and the opera house was a place where courtesans were displayed [documented]. On this reading, the frame of auction and theatre is the argument.
- **Manon as warning or as ideal.** In the Munich synopsis Marguerite is "disgusted" by Manon's frivolity at first and finally identifies with her completely [documented]. The ballet can be read as Marguerite becoming what she feared, or as her claiming Manon's freedom [argued].
- **Short or long.** Ashton cut Dumas to four scenes in thirty minutes for two stars; Neumeier keeps the subplots, the father, the friends and adds a second couple across three hours [documented, both]. Critics who value concentration prefer Ashton; dancers who value roles prefer Neumeier [argued].
- **Dates.** The film is dated 1986 (Bavarian State Opera) and 1987 elsewhere [contested].

### Facts worth telling
- The ballet begins with an auction of the heroine's furniture [documented].
- A second pair of lovers, Manon and Des Grieux, haunt the first [documented].
- The white dress is so fragile that companies keep spare fabric [documented: Rose].
- Haydée danced Marguerite while directing the company [documented].
- The music is all Chopin, but none of it was written for the theatre [documented for the pieces].
- Neumeier also designs its lighting [documented: ABT credit].

### Set it beside
- **`marguerite-armand-1963`** (this batch): same story, opposite scale.
- **`manon-1974`** (this batch): MacMillan's full Manon four years earlier; Neumeier puts a small Manon inside his ballet.
- **`onegin-1965`** (this batch): the Stuttgart duet-drama it extends, with the same designer.
- **`dances-at-a-gathering-1969`** (this batch): Chopin's piano used without story, against Chopin's piano used for the most story-heavy ballet in the batch.

### What a maker can take from it
- A frame (an auction of things) can carry the whole theme without a word.
- Put a smaller version of your story inside your story; the audience reads one by the other.
- Design for wear: a costume that tears needs a supply chain, so plan it.

### Beyond the stage
The 1986 film [documented]. Ballet tourism round Hamburg's Neumeier repertory [probable].

## Across these works

**Refused at home, made elsewhere.** Four of the thirteen works were turned down by the house that later wanted them. The Royal Opera House board refused *Song of the Earth* twice (1959, 1965) and *Onegin* once; both were made in Stuttgart and then taken back to London, *Song* within six months, *Onegin* only in 2001. *Miss Julie* was made for a touring company and taken by the Royal Swedish Opera within a month. Bausch made her *Sacre* for an audience that threw tomatoes. In this period the provincial or secondary stage (Västerås, Stuttgart, Wuppertal, the 92nd Street Y) is where the lasting works begin [argued from the dossiers].

**Hardly anyone commissioned a score.** Not one of the thirteen has a score written for it. Each is assembled from existing music: Rangström's songs on piano, Tchaikovsky's piano pieces for *Onegin*, Massenet's forgotten operas for *Manon*, about thirty Liszt pieces for *Mayerling*, a Liszt sonata for *Marguerite*, Chopin three times, spirituals for *Revelations*, Haydn and a rag for Tharp. Twice the obvious opera (Tchaikovsky's *Onegin*, Massenet's *Manon*) was deliberately avoided. The arranger (Stolze, Lucas with Gaunt, Lanchbery, Searle) becomes a hidden co-author [documented for each; the pattern argued].

**Made on bodies, then handed on.** Almost every work was cut to particular dancers, and each tells a different story of handover. *Marguerite* was locked to Fonteyn and Nureyev for 37 years. *Onegin* changed shape because Haydée loved lifts and Fonteyn did not. *Manon* was made on two ballerinas because one was injured; *Mayerling* lost its first Rudolf before the premiere. Bausch's *Sacre* went from Wuppertal to Paris, London and 38 dancers from 14 African countries. *The Nutcracker* is handed on every December to local children. The trusts and estates (Balanchine, MacMillan, Cranko's heir, the Pina Bausch Foundation, Neumeier) now decide who may dance what [documented].

**One object carries the world.** The designs that last are the ones built round one emblem: the tree that grows to 41 feet, the floor of peat, the wall of rags, Death's half-mask, the red dress, the bowler hat, the costume colours that name Robbins's dancers, Karinska's imitation stones [argued].

**Women who die, and who makes them die.** The narrative works of the batch are dominated by thread T15: Julie, Marguerite (twice), Manon and Mary Vetsera all die, and Tatiana survives only by refusing love. Most were made by men. But the two most violent deaths of women here, Julie's and the Chosen One's, were made by women, Cullberg and Bausch; and Tharp, the third woman choreographer, made the only comedy. The gender of the maker does not predict the fate of the heroine [argued].

**New York abstracts, Europe narrates.** The three New York ballet works (*Jewels*, *Dances at a Gathering*, *Push Comes to Shove*) are plotless; the European ones, from London, Stuttgart and Stockholm, are literary dramas. *The Nutcracker* is the exception that pays for the rest [argued].

**Companies contradict themselves.** NYCB says its *Nutcracker* follows Dumas while quoting Balanchine saying Hoffmann; NYCB and Kirstein disagree on when Balanchine met Arpels; NYCB and the 1969 *Times* name different lighting designers for *Dances*; the Ailey company and Wikipedia name different designers for *Revelations*; the Cullberg archive and SKBL disagree on who first danced Kristin; Tharp's own site claims a "first" that the *Times* gives to her earlier *Deuce Coupe*. Company pages are the commonest sources here, and not neutral witnesses [documented].

**Surprises.** *Miss Julie*'s music was by a composer three years dead. Liszt was the lover of the real Lady of the Camellias. The famous white-tutu photographs of Fonteyn's Marguerite show a dress she did not dance in.
