## In the Upper Room / In the Upper Room (1986)
`id: in-the-upper-room-1986` · threads: T10, T11 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Public preview as an untitled work-in-progress, 7 July 1986, Little Theatre, Saratoga Springs, New York; billed world premiere 28 August 1986, Murray Theatre, Ravinia Festival, Highland Park, Illinois; Twyla Tharp Dance [documented, Tharp Foundation performance history; Glass recording notes]. Choreographer Twyla Tharp. Composer Philip Glass (commissioned score, produced by Kurt Munkacsi, conducted by Michael Riesman; played on tape). No scenario. Costumes Norma Kamali; lighting Jennifer Tipton; no set [documented, Tharp Foundation]. One act in nine numbered parts, 39 minutes, 13 dancers [documented]. Commissioned by the Norman and Rosita Winston Foundation [documented]. The version seen today: the same choreography restaged by Tharp's former dancers (Shelley Washington above all) for ballet companies, most often American Ballet Theatre (from 10 December 1988) [documented, ABT archive].

### In one line
Two teams, one in sneakers and one on pointe, run out of a wall of fog in relays until the whole cast is soaked and the stage seems to burn.

### How it came to be
In 1985 Tharp had staged a Broadway *Singin' in the Rain* that critics disliked [documented, Bierut 2005, citing Tharp's memoir]. Her company had also finished a hard year of touring. It took a year off and reassembled in May 1986 [documented, Tharp Foundation]. Tharp wanted to go back to basics. She remembered her plain early piece *The Fugue* (1970) and its "hard day's work" ideal, and set out to make a dance about endurance, power, speed and timing [documented, Bierut quoting *Push Comes to Shove*, 1992]. She had been training with the boxing coach Teddy Atlas, and boxing moves went into the steps [documented, Bierut; probable as to the extent].

She commissioned Philip Glass for a new score. Glass said he left "a lot of space for the dance to happen" so that the dancers "complete it" [documented, Tharp Foundation]. The fashion designer Norma Kamali made the clothes and Jennifer Tipton, Tharp's long-time lighting partner, made the light. Tharp gave Tipton and the set designer Santo Loquasto one order: three men "must just appear out of nowhere" [documented, Bierut quoting Tharp]. The dance was shown untitled in July 1986. The name *In the Upper Room* first appeared in print on 3 February 1987, for the Brooklyn Academy of Music season [documented, Tharp Foundation].

### Roots and reference points
- **Tharp's own crossover dances.** *Deuce Coupe* (1973, for the Joffrey) and *Push Comes to Shove* (1976, for ABT and Baryshnikov) had already put modern dancers and ballet dancers on one stage [documented, Tharp Foundation]. Marcia B. Siegel noted that Tharp "has done that one at least twice before" (*Christian Science Monitor*, 10 February 1987) [documented].
- **Sport and the gym.** Jogging, shadow-boxing, yoga and sprinting are in the vocabulary [documented, Tharp Foundation]. Kamali was famous for 1980s workout clothes and the red swimsuit in the 1976 Farrah Fawcett poster [documented, PBT education pack].
- **Minimalist music.** Glass's pulse continues the minimalist line Robbins had used in *Glass Pieces* (1983) [documented that *Glass Pieces* exists; link argued].
- **Balanchine.** Siegel saw it as "a speeded-up Balanchine-type ballet" [documented, CSM 1987].
- **The title.** No source found in this pass explains it. The phrase is the gospel song title made famous by Mahalia Jackson and the room of the Last Supper; any link is **argued**, not documented.

### The world it built
Nine sections, building from a duet to the full company [documented, Tharp Foundation section list]. Two women open the piece; Tharp thought of them as Chinese temple guard dogs ("china dogs") [documented, company programme note]. The dancers in running shoes are the "stompers"; two women on pointe are the "bomb squad," later widened into a "ballet cadre" [documented, programme note]. The rule is relay and accumulation: teams enter, exit into the fog and come back changed. Tharp called the movement "fierce, driving, and relentless" and wanted unison passages that "burn the retina" [documented, programme note]. Much of the travelling is jogging, sometimes backwards [documented]. In the finale earlier material returns twisted, then the dancers scatter into the dark. Two "stomper" men vanish backwards while throwing a punch. The "china dogs" pull their fists down like a window blind and the curtain falls [documented, programme note]. Glass's score, for ensemble with electric keyboards, grows louder and more pressurised towards the end [documented, Siegel 1987]. The relation is pulse to pulse: the dancers ride the music's repetition rather than its melody [argued].

### Design and art
The design is three things: fog, light, clothes. Smoke machines fill the stage with an even haze. Tipton's narrow, sharp beams pick dancers out of it, so they seem to materialise "out of nowhere"; the designer Michael Bierut compared the effect to James Turrell (*Design Observer*, 2005) [documented]. The back of the stage is a black velvet void [documented, programme note]. Kamali's costumes begin as loose black-and-white striped jumpsuits, like pyjamas. As the dance goes on the dancers strip layers and reveal red: red tops, red leotards, red pointe shoes [documented, Tharp Foundation; Siegel 1987]. A Los Angeles critic asked whether the stripes meant "referees? prisoners?" (*LA Times*, 23 April 1988) [documented]. The colour script is the story: monochrome to red, cool to hot, clothed to sweating skin [argued]. Famous images: Richard Avedon's promotional photographs and Tom Brazil's BAM 1987 photographs (copyright; link-only) [documented, Tharp Foundation].

### Premiere and reception
Thirteen dancers: Jamie Bishton, John Carrafa, Richard Colton, Erzsebet Foldi, Stephanie Foster, Julie Nakagawa, Kevin O'Day, Cathy Oppenheimer, Kevin Santee, Karen Stasick, Ellen Troy, Shelley Washington, William Whitener [documented, ABT archive]. Siegel called it "the most original, accomplished, and exciting work we've been offered in ages" and read it as being "about the end of the world" (CSM 1987) [documented]. Dale Harris (*Wall Street Journal*) wrote that the audience "can hardly help but give it an ovation" [documented, Glass recording notes]. Not everyone agreed: Lewis Segal in the *LA Times* (12 December 1988) found the work "more than ever unfinished—or perhaps impossible to finish" [documented; author name probable]. Tharp later said it was her only piece that "generates a standing ovation at almost every performance" [documented, quoted by Bierut]. Olivier Award 1991 to Tharp and Tipton [documented]. Money: public funds from NYSCA and the NEA and several foundations are thanked in the original programme; no budget found.

### Versions and lineage
Tharp's company disbanded in 1988 and she joined ABT, taking the work with her [documented, Ballet Spot 2021; ABT]. ABT premiere 10 December 1988, Costa Mesa; New York 13 May 1989 [documented]. ABT danced it at the Palais Garnier, Paris, 5 July 1991 [documented]. Later stagings include Dutch National Ballet (1998), Birmingham Royal Ballet (1999), Royal Swedish Ballet (2000), Miami City Ballet (2007), Zürich (2009), Royal Winnipeg (2011) and Vienna (2026) [documented, Tharp Foundation list]. The Royal Ballet (Covent Garden) staging date was not found in this pass; the register's claim needs a check. The work is transmitted in person by former cast members; no published notation was found.

### Readings and disputes
- **Apocalypse or celebration.** Siegel (CSM 1987) reads a dance about the end of the world, pushed to a high "beyond which even she can't see." The Tharp Foundation calls it "a transcendent experience" for dancers and audience. Both readings rest on the same exhaustion [argued].
- **Fusion or two camps.** Siegel argues that Tharp uses the two styles as ballet uses soloists and corps, to give variety in an egalitarian company. Segal (LA Times 1988) argues that "fusion never came" and the two teams stay apart [both documented].
- **Which premiere?** The Glass recording notes date the premiere to 7 July 1986 at Saratoga; the Tharp Foundation calls Saratoga a "preview" and Ravinia the premiere [documented; contested only in wording].
- **A design-led dance?** Bierut argues that Tipton is "the evening's unheralded star" [documented]. The company texts present it as a synthesis of equals [documented].

### Facts worth telling
- It premiered without a title; the name was printed only in February 1987 [documented, Tharp Foundation].
- The two pointe women were nicknamed the "bomb squad" [documented].
- The opening women are "china dogs," after temple guardian statues [documented].
- Kamali also designed the red swimsuit in the best-selling Farrah Fawcett poster [documented, PBT pack].
- It runs 39 minutes without a break and is famous for exhausting its cast [documented runtime; probable for the rest].
- The score is played from tape, not live [documented, Siegel; LA Times 1988].
- Tipton and Tharp shared the 1991 Olivier Award [documented].

### Set it beside
- `push-comes-to-shove-1976`: Tharp's first ballet-company crossover, comic and ragtime, where *Upper Room* is grave and athletic.
- `in-the-middle-1987`: made nine months later in Paris; both are plotless, both ride an amplified pulse, but Forsythe tilts ballet itself where Tharp sets ballet beside other kinds of movement.
- `jeux-1913` and `train-bleu-1924`: earlier ballets about sport, which show sport as a subject; Tharp makes sport a method [argued].
- `flight-pattern-2017`: another work where light and haze build the world and the mass of bodies is the subject.

### What a maker can take from it
- A world can be built from three elements (fog, beams, two colours) if each obeys one rule.
- Give your teams names ("stompers," "bomb squad"): a private vocabulary helps a team hold a concept.
- A costume that changes during the work can carry the arc of a world with no story.

### Beyond the stage
Glass's score was released on *Dancepieces* (CBS/Sony, 1987; five of nine sections) and later complete on Orange Mountain Music [documented, philipglass.com; Tharp Foundation]. Bierut's essay uses the work to teach "experience design" to graphic designers [documented]. Tharp's book *The Creative Habit* (2003) is widely read outside dance [documented, ABT biography].
