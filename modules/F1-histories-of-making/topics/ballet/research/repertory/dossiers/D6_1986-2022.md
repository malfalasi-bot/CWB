## In the Upper Room / In the Upper Room (1986)
`id: in-the-upper-room-1986` · threads: T10, T11 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Public preview as an untitled work-in-progress, 7 July 1986, Little Theatre, Saratoga Springs, New York; billed world premiere 28 August 1986, Murray Theatre, Ravinia Festival, Highland Park, Illinois; Twyla Tharp Dance [documented, Tharp Foundation performance history; Glass recording notes]. Choreographer Twyla Tharp. Composer Philip Glass (commissioned score, produced by Kurt Munkacsi, conducted by Michael Riesman; played on tape). No scenario. Costumes Norma Kamali; lighting Jennifer Tipton; no set [documented, Tharp Foundation]. One act in nine numbered parts, 39 minutes, 13 dancers [documented]. Commissioned by the Norman and Rosita Winston Foundation [documented]. The version seen today: the same choreography restaged by Tharp's former dancers (Shelley Washington above all) for ballet companies, most often American Ballet Theatre (from 10 December 1988) [documented, ABT archive].

### In one line
Two teams, one in sneakers and one on pointe, run out of a wall of fog in relays until the whole cast is soaked and the stage seems to burn.

### How it came to be
In 1985 Tharp had staged a Broadway *Singin' in the Rain* that critics disliked [documented, Bierut 2005, citing Tharp's memoir]. Her company had also finished a hard year of touring. It took a year off and reassembled in May 1986 [documented, Tharp Foundation]. Tharp wanted to go back to basics. She remembered her plain early piece *The Fugue* (1970) and its "hard day's work" ideal, and set out to make a dance about endurance, power, speed and timing [documented, Bierut quoting *Push Comes to Shove*, 1992]. She had been training with the boxing coach Teddy Atlas, and boxing moves went into the steps [documented, Bierut; probable as to the extent].

She commissioned Philip Glass for a new score. Glass said he left "a lot of space for the dance to happen" so that the dancers "complete it" [documented, Tharp Foundation]. The fashion designer Norma Kamali made the clothes and Jennifer Tipton, Tharp's long-time lighting partner, made the light. Tharp gave Tipton and the set designer Santo Loquasto one order: three men "must just appear out of nowhere" [documented, Bierut quoting Tharp]. The dance was shown untitled in July 1986. The name *In the Upper Room* first appeared in print on 3 February 1987, for the Brooklyn Academy of Music season [documented, Tharp Foundation].

### Roots and reference points
- **Tharp's own crossover dances.** *Deuce Coupe* (1973, for the Joffrey) and *Push Comes to Shove* (1976, for ABT and Baryshnikov) had already put modern dancers and ballet dancers on one stage [documented, Tharp Foundation]. Marcia B. Siegel noted that Tharp "has done that one at least twice before" (*Christian Science Monitor*, 10 February 1987) [documented].
- **Sport and the gym.** Jogging, shadow-boxing, yoga and sprinting are in the vocabulary [documented, Tharp Foundation]. Kamali was famous for 1980s workout clothes and the red swimsuit in the 1976 Farrah Fawcett poster [documented, PBT education pack].
- **Minimalist music.** Glass's pulse continues the minimalist line Robbins had used in *Glass Pieces* (1983) [documented that *Glass Pieces* exists; link argued].
- **Balanchine.** Siegel saw it as "a speeded-up Balanchine-type ballet" [documented, CSM 1987].
- **The title.** No source found in this pass explains it. The phrase is the gospel song title made famous by Mahalia Jackson and the room of the Last Supper; any link is **argued**, not documented.

### The world it built
Nine sections, building from a duet to the full company [documented, Tharp Foundation section list]. Two women open the piece; Tharp thought of them as Chinese temple guard dogs ("china dogs") [documented, company programme note]. The dancers in running shoes are the "stompers"; two women on pointe are the "bomb squad," later widened into a "ballet cadre" [documented, programme note]. The rule is relay and accumulation: teams enter, exit into the fog and come back changed. Tharp called the movement "fierce, driving, and relentless" and wanted unison passages that "burn the retina" [documented, programme note]. Much of the travelling is jogging, sometimes backwards [documented]. In the finale earlier material returns twisted, then the dancers scatter into the dark. Two "stomper" men vanish backwards while throwing a punch. The "china dogs" pull their fists down like a window blind and the curtain falls [documented, programme note]. Glass's score, for ensemble with electric keyboards, grows louder and more pressurised towards the end [documented, Siegel 1987]. The relation is pulse to pulse: the dancers ride the music's repetition rather than its melody [argued].

### Design and art
The design is three things: fog, light, clothes. Smoke machines fill the stage with an even haze. Tipton's narrow, sharp beams pick dancers out of it, so they seem to materialise "out of nowhere"; the designer Michael Bierut compared the effect to James Turrell (*Design Observer*, 2005) [documented]. The back of the stage is a black velvet void [documented, programme note]. Kamali's costumes begin as loose black-and-white striped jumpsuits, like pyjamas. As the dance goes on the dancers strip layers and reveal red: red tops, red leotards, red pointe shoes [documented, Tharp Foundation; Siegel 1987]. A Los Angeles critic asked whether the stripes meant "referees? prisoners?" (*LA Times*, 23 April 1988) [documented]. The colour script is the story: monochrome to red, cool to hot, clothed to sweating skin [argued]. Famous images: Richard Avedon's promotional photographs and Tom Brazil's BAM 1987 photographs (copyright; link-only) [documented, Tharp Foundation].

### Premiere and reception
Thirteen dancers: Jamie Bishton, John Carrafa, Richard Colton, Erzsebet Foldi, Stephanie Foster, Julie Nakagawa, Kevin O'Day, Cathy Oppenheimer, Kevin Santee, Karen Stasick, Ellen Troy, Shelley Washington, William Whitener [documented, ABT archive]. Siegel called it "the most original, accomplished, and exciting work we've been offered in ages" and read it as being "about the end of the world" (CSM 1987) [documented]. Dale Harris (*Wall Street Journal*) wrote that the audience "can hardly help but give it an ovation" [documented, Glass recording notes]. Not everyone agreed: Lewis Segal in the *LA Times* (12 December 1988) found the work "more than ever unfinished—or perhaps impossible to finish" [documented; author name probable]. Tharp later said it was her only piece that "generates a standing ovation at almost every performance" [documented, quoted by Bierut]. Olivier Award 1991 to Tharp and Tipton [documented]. Money: public funds from NYSCA and the NEA and several foundations are thanked in the original programme; no budget found.

### Versions and lineage
Tharp's company disbanded in 1988 and she joined ABT, taking the work with her [documented, Ballet Spot 2021; ABT]. ABT premiere 10 December 1988, Costa Mesa; New York 13 May 1989 [documented]. ABT danced it at the Palais Garnier, Paris, 5 July 1991 [documented]. Later stagings include Dutch National Ballet (1998), Birmingham Royal Ballet (1999), Royal Swedish Ballet (2000), Miami City Ballet (2007), Zürich (2009), Royal Winnipeg (2011) and Vienna (2026) [documented, Tharp Foundation list]. The Royal Ballet (Covent Garden) staging date was not found in this pass; the register's claim needs a check. The work is transmitted in person by former cast members; no published notation was found.

### Readings and disputes
- **Apocalypse or celebration.** Siegel (CSM 1987) reads a dance about the end of the world, pushed to a high "beyond which even she can't see." The Tharp Foundation calls it "a transcendent experience" for dancers and audience. Both readings rest on the same exhaustion [argued].
- **Fusion or two camps.** Siegel argues that Tharp uses the two styles as ballet uses soloists and corps, to give variety in an egalitarian company. Segal (LA Times 1988) argues that "fusion never came" and the two teams stay apart [both documented].
- **Which premiere?** The Glass recording notes date the premiere to 7 July 1986 at Saratoga; the Tharp Foundation calls Saratoga a "preview" and Ravinia the premiere [documented; contested only in wording].
- **A design-led dance?** Bierut argues that Tipton is "the evening's unheralded star" [documented]. The company texts present it as a synthesis of equals [documented].

### Facts worth telling
- It premiered without a title; the name was printed only in February 1987 [documented, Tharp Foundation].
- The two pointe women were nicknamed the "bomb squad" [documented].
- The opening women are "china dogs," after temple guardian statues [documented].
- Kamali also designed the red swimsuit in the best-selling Farrah Fawcett poster [documented, PBT pack].
- It runs 39 minutes without a break and is famous for exhausting its cast [documented runtime; probable for the rest].
- The score is played from tape, not live [documented, Siegel; LA Times 1988].
- Tipton and Tharp shared the 1991 Olivier Award [documented].

### Set it beside
- `push-comes-to-shove-1976`: Tharp's first ballet-company crossover, comic and ragtime, where *Upper Room* is grave and athletic.
- `in-the-middle-1987`: made nine months later in Paris; both are plotless, both ride an amplified pulse, but Forsythe tilts ballet itself where Tharp sets ballet beside other kinds of movement.
- `jeux-1913` and `train-bleu-1924`: earlier ballets about sport, which show sport as a subject; Tharp makes sport a method [argued].
- `flight-pattern-2017`: another work where light and haze build the world and the mass of bodies is the subject.

### What a maker can take from it
- A world can be built from three elements (fog, beams, two colours) if each obeys one rule.
- Give your teams names ("stompers," "bomb squad"): a private vocabulary helps a team hold a concept.
- A costume that changes during the work can carry the arc of a world with no story.

### Beyond the stage
Glass's score was released on *Dancepieces* (CBS/Sony, 1987; five of nine sections) and later complete on Orange Mountain Music [documented, philipglass.com; Tharp Foundation]. Bierut's essay uses the work to teach "experience design" to graphic designers [documented]. Tharp's book *The Creative Habit* (2003) is widely read outside dance [documented, ABT biography].

- Marcia B. Siegel, "'In the Upper Room': carefully crafted, constantly interesting," *Christian Science Monitor*, 10 February 1987: https://www.csmonitor.com/1987/0210/ltwyla.html [seen].

## In the Middle, Somewhat Elevated / In the Middle, Somewhat Elevated (1987)
`id: in-the-middle-1987` · threads: T10, T09, T13 · confidence of the header: documented (day of premiere contested)

**Header.** Premiere 30 May 1987, Palais Garnier, Paris Opéra Ballet [documented, Kansas City Ballet and Pittsburgh Ballet Theatre credits; Showa University ballet archive]; 29 May 1987 in other company credits [documented, Pacific Northwest Ballet; Compañía Nacional de Danza]: see Disputes. Choreographer William Forsythe. Music Thom Willems "in collaboration with Leslie Stuck" (electronic, on tape) [documented, PNB; KC Ballet]. No scenario. Stage, costume and lighting design William Forsythe [documented]. One act, about 26 to 28 minutes, nine dancers (six women, three men) [documented, PNB; Boston Ballet; ROH Collections]. Commissioned by Rudolf Nureyev, then director of dance at the Paris Opéra [documented, Guardian 2015]. The version seen today: the stand-alone piece, staged by Forsythe's répétiteurs (Glen Tuggle, Kathryn Bennetts, Thierry Guiderdoni and others) for companies worldwide [documented].

### In one line
Classical steps are speeded up, tilted off their axis and stretched to breaking point, under hard light and a slamming electronic score, while two small golden cherries hang overhead.

### How it came to be
Forsythe, born in New York in 1949, had trained with the Joffrey and danced and choreographed at the Stuttgart Ballet. In 1984 he became director of Ballett Frankfurt [documented, PNB biography; Holland Festival 1988]. Nureyev, running the Paris Opéra Ballet from 1983, was bringing living choreographers to the company and had raised a young generation of stars from the corps [documented, Guardian 2015]. He invited Forsythe [documented]. Forsythe made the piece on four of those stars, Sylvie Guillem, Isabelle Guérin, Laurent Hilaire and Manuel Legris, with five other dancers [documented, Guardian; Wikipedia "Impressing the Czar"]. Forsythe had already planned it as the abstract central act of a longer satire on ballet and Western culture. He completed that work, *Impressing the Czar*, in Frankfurt the next year (1988; shown at the Holland Festival on 3 to 5 June 1988) [documented, Holland Festival archive; KC Ballet]. The Paris piece was the one that travelled. It made Forsythe, in the Guardian's words, an international name "to be reckoned with" (Guardian, 7 March 2015) [documented].

### Roots and reference points
- **The academic class.** Forsythe calls it "a theme and variations in the strictest sense" that "extends and accelerates" classical figures until they appear "at odds with their own origins" [documented, Forsythe note in PBT programme].
- **Balanchine.** Forsythe's speed, off-centre partnering and bare stage descend from Balanchine's leotard ballets (`agon-1957`, `four-temperaments-1946`) [probable; widely stated].
- **Rudolf Laban.** Forsythe studied Laban's spatial geometry and later built it into his *Improvisation Technologies* (1999) [probable].
- **The building.** The title names the two gilded cherries hung above the stage, a tiny echo of the gilt of the Palais Garnier [documented, CND; KC Ballet].
- **The Petipa joke.** *Impressing the Czar* is named after Tsar Alexander III's cool reception of Petipa's *Sleeping Beauty* (1890); the Wikipedia entry names Nicholas II, which is a date error, since Nicholas came to the throne in 1894 [documented that the title refers to the *Beauty* reception; tsar's name corrected, documented by dates].

### The world it built
The structure is a classroom one: a main theme, first danced by a ballerina, is developed as more dancers join, through variations, duets and ensembles [documented, CND]. Dancers walk on and off as if in a studio, sometimes standing at the side to watch [probable]. The tension is between "apparent and nonchalant disdain" and "strict and severe technical demands" [documented, CND]. Signature passages: the central duet for "Sylvie and Laurent," still called by the original dancers' names [documented, Ballet Spot 2020; Semperoper video title]. Movement rules: hips pushed out of line, extreme extensions (the "six o'clock" leg), partnering that pulls a woman off balance so the man must catch weight rather than present it, sudden stops [documented, Voices of British Ballet; KC Ballet; probable for detail]. Willems's score hammers, stops and restarts. It does not set the tempo phrase by phrase; dancers count against it [argued]. The Guardian describes it as sending "electronic shudders through the piece" [documented].

### Design and art
Forsythe designed everything. The stage is a black box with the wings exposed [probable]. Light is hard and often low, from the side; the stage sometimes dims almost to darkness [probable]. Costumes are practice clothes: leotards and tights in dark green or teal, some women in short skirts or tights over leotards [probable; colours vary by staging]. The cherries are the only object. They are small, gold and placed "in the middle, somewhat elevated." The joke is that a ballet named after its décor has almost none [argued]. The look borrows from rehearsal, not fantasy: what the Opéra hid (sweat, effort, the studio) is what it shows [argued].

### Premiere and reception
Original cast included Guillem, Guérin, Hilaire and Legris; the other five not found in this pass. Guillem later said she thought "of nothing ... except the steps," needing "line and power" [documented, Guardian 2015, quoting her at the 1992 Royal Ballet revival]. The Guardian (2015) calls it the work that "illuminated everything around it" [documented]. Contemporary French reviews of 1987 were not found in this pass. The Royal Ballet of Flanders' performance won the 1992 Olivier Award [documented, Wikipedia citing *The Stage*].

### Versions and lineage
- Paris 1987; Frankfurt 1988 as Act II of *Impressing the Czar* (set Michael Simon, costumes Férial Simon in the full work) [documented, Holland Festival].
- Royal Ballet: ROH Collections gives a production premiere of 13 February 1992 [documented]; Voices of British Ballet says 1991 [documented]: contested. Guillem, Hilaire and Darcey Bussell were in Royal Ballet casts [documented].
- Further stagings: Compañía Nacional de Danza (11 December 1992), Pacific Northwest Ballet (2000), Semperoper Dresden (2004), Kansas City (2019), English National Ballet (2015), Boston, Mariinsky and many others [documented for those named].
- Transmission: by Forsythe's répétiteurs; no published score in notation. Forsythe's *Improvisation Technologies* CD-ROM (ZKM, 1999) records his method, not this ballet [probable].

**Note on *Artifact* (1984).** Forsythe's first full-evening work for Frankfurt, premiered 5 December 1984 [probable; one secondary source]. Four parts, about two hours [documented, Cavia Naya 2012]. Music: piano by Eva Crossman-Hecht built on the Chaconne from Bach's Partita No. 2 in D minor; the Chaconne itself in Nathan Milstein's recording for Part II; a sound collage by Forsythe [documented, NYT 2009; theartsdesk 2012; Sulcas 2011]. Three characters walk through a large corps: a Woman in Historical Costume, a Man with a Megaphone and a grey "Other Person" [documented, Sulcas 2011]. The woman claps like a rehearsal director and recites from a tiny word list ("Rocks, Dirt, Sand, Soot, Dust") [documented, Sulcas]. In Part II the main curtain crashes down again and again during a double pas de deux, then rises on a changed picture [documented, NYT 2017]. Forsythe called it "a story ballet without a story" [documented in one secondary source; probable]. *Artifact* asks what ballet's history leaves behind; *In the Middle* takes its classroom and speeds it up. Boston Ballet's 2017 staging was the full work's North American debut [documented, Harvard Crimson 2017].

### Readings and disputes
- **Deconstruction or extension.** Many critics call Forsythe a deconstructor of ballet. Roslyn Sulcas argues that ballet remains "the cornerstone" and that his early works make "the tension between academic forms and those forms pushed to their extremes" explicit ("Watching the Ballett Frankfurt," in Steven Spier ed., *William Forsythe and the Practice of Choreography*, Routledge, 2011) [documented]. Forsythe's own note speaks of extension, not destruction [documented].
- **Postdramatic theatre.** Hans-Thies Lehmann (*Postdramatic Theatre*, 1999) groups Forsythe with theatre that explores its own means; Gerald Siegmund reads the 1980s works as studies of ballet's transmission and conventions [documented, as reported by Aaltonen, Theatre Academy Helsinki].
- **Effect on bodies.** Voices of British Ballet says Guillem's extreme extensions changed the Royal Ballet's "look and style" [documented]. Some writers later linked the hyper-extended leg to injury and to a narrower ideal body [argued; not sourced in this pass].
- **Date.** 29 or 30 May 1987: companies' licensed credits disagree, which suggests the Forsythe office has issued both [contested; a Paris Opéra archive check is needed].
- **Royal Ballet entry.** 1991 or 13 February 1992 [contested, see above].

### Facts worth telling
- Sections are known by the first dancers' names: "the Sylvie," "the Laurent" [documented].
- The title describes the only scenery: two gold cherries [documented].
- It was conceived as the second act of a longer satire [documented].
- The *Impressing the Czar* finale puts the whole cast in schoolgirl uniforms [documented, KC Ballet].
- Forsythe designed the set, costumes and lighting himself [documented].
- *Artifact*'s curtain falls mid-phrase as part of the choreography [documented].

### Set it beside
- `agon-1957`: Balanchine's twelve dancers in practice clothes; Forsythe keeps the bare stage but adds weight, noise and tilt.
- `in-the-upper-room-1986`: the American crossover of the same year; Tharp contrasts two techniques, Forsythe deforms one.
- `chroma-2006`: McGregor's hyper-extended bodies inherit Forsythe's tilted classicism [argued].
- `jewels-1967`: a plotless evening built on a theme (stones); Forsythe's cherries are a parody of the decorative title [argued].

### What a maker can take from it
- Take the rules of a tradition and push each one a little further than it can go: the result still reads as the tradition.
- A single tiny object can name and frame a whole world.
- Design the "behind the scenes" as the scene: the studio can be the set.

### Beyond the stage
Forsythe later made "Choreographic Objects," installations shown at Tate Modern, MoMA and the Venice Biennale [documented, Bayerische Staatsoper biography]. Countless online videos of the "Sylvie and Laurent" duet circulate from company channels (e.g. Semperoper Ballett) [documented].

## The Hard Nut / The Hard Nut (1991)
`id: hard-nut-1991` · threads: T13, T04, T22, T06 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 12 January 1991, Théâtre Royal de la Monnaie, Brussels, Mark Morris Dance Group with Monnaie forces; 13 performances to 27 January [documented, MMDG performance listing]. Choreographer and scenario Mark Morris, after E. T. A. Hoffmann, *Nussknacker und Mausekönig* (1816), including its inner tale of the hard nut [documented, MMDG]. Music Tchaikovsky, *The Nutcracker*, op. 71, complete, with orchestra and children's chorus [documented, MMDG]. Sets Adrianne Lobel; costumes Martin Pakledinaz; lighting James F. Ingalls; "production based on the work of Charles Burns," the American cartoonist [documented, MMDG]. Two acts (47 and 43 minutes), 33 dancers [documented, MMDG]. The version seen today: Morris's own, revived by MMDG at the Brooklyn Academy of Music and on tour, in the original designs [documented].

### In one line
*The Nutcracker* moves to a boozy American suburban Christmas drawn like a black-and-white comic, and Act II tells Hoffmann's forgotten story of the hard nut.

### How it came to be
In 1988 the Monnaie's director, Gerard Mortier, made Morris director of dance at the Belgian national opera house, succeeding Maurice Béjart [probable; widely reported]. Morris's company spent three seasons in Brussels and made ten works there [documented, Wikipedia]. For a large last piece, Morris said, he was talking with friends about "one of the big Tchaikovsky dances, and it just turned into *Nutcracker*" (Time Out New York, 2010) [documented]. He was a fan of Charles Burns, whose comics show 1950s and 1960s American childhood as both cosy and horrifying [documented, Pakledinaz interview, TheaterMania 2010]. Burns was asked to inspire the design but was not a theatre person. Pakledinaz said the idea of collaboration "was confusing to him," so the designers studied his drawings and worked in his manner [documented, TheaterMania]. Design took about a year [documented]. The premiere came "just short of the 100th anniversary" of the 1892 ballet [documented, Wikipedia]. Then MMDG went home to the United States.

### Roots and reference points
- **Hoffmann.** Most *Nutcrackers* follow Dumas's softened French version. Morris restores Hoffmann's inner tale: Princess Pirlipat is turned ugly by the Rat Queen and only the hard nut Krakatuk, cracked by a young man, can cure her [documented, MMDG synopsis].
- **Tchaikovsky's score, uncut.** Morris uses the whole score and its children's chorus [documented].
- **Charles Burns.** Comic art from *Raw* and *Black Hole*: hard black line, flat white, suburban menace [documented that Burns was the source; style description argued].
- **American television and pop culture.** The fireplace becomes a television; Harlequin and Columbine dolls become a Barbie and a robot; the party dances become the hokey-pokey, the stroll and the bump [documented, Dance Enthusiast 2016; MMDG synopsis].
- **Baryshnikov's ABT *Nutcracker* (1976).** Like it, Morris cuts the Sugar Plum Fairy and gives the grand pas de deux to the young couple [documented, Wikipedia].
- **Gender play in Morris's own work**, such as his *Dido and Aeneas* (1989), in which he danced both Dido and the Sorceress [probable].

### The world it built
Act I: Dr and Mrs Stahlbaum's Christmas Eve party. Children Fritz, Marie and Louise wait in the den. Guests drink from a drinks cart pushed by a maid; Mrs Stahlbaum drains several glasses and falls over [documented, Jowitt 2016]. Drosselmeier brings mechanical toys and gives Marie the Nutcracker; Fritz breaks it [documented]. The battle with the rats follows, then the snow [documented]. Act II: Drosselmeier tells the story of Pirlipat. His nephew travels the world to find the nut. Spanish, Arabian, Chinese, Russian and French dancers know nothing about it [documented, Jowitt]. The nut finally falls from above; Pirlipat rejects the young man who cracks it; Marie declares her love and the spell breaks [documented, Wikipedia]. Cross-casting is a rule: the maid is a man on pointe; the Rat King is a woman on pointe; Mrs Stahlbaum is danced in drag; snowflakes and flowers are men and women in identical costumes [documented, Dance Enthusiast; Jowitt]. Morris said: "Why are snowflakes women? ... Once you're frozen, it doesn't matter what your sex is" [documented, quoted on MMDG press page]. Signature passage: the Waltz of the Snowflakes, 22 dancers running and leaping in squads while throwing handfuls of paper snow on the beat [documented, Jowitt; Hutchinson 2020]. Morris is known for setting steps to the musical structure; critics noted that he "pays strict attention to Tchaikovsky" (*LA Times*, 15 December 1992) [documented].

### Design and art
Lobel's sets are huge black-and-white drawings on scrims, in Burns's line; images such as a close-up of Drosselmeier's face loom behind them [documented, Jowitt]. The rule of colour was set at the first meeting: the black-and-white world gets red and green at the party [documented, Pakledinaz]. Pakledinaz added pink for Marie, "a little girl," and royal purple and gold when the parents become the King and Queen of the inner tale [documented]. Snowflake headdresses were modelled on Dairy Queen soft-serve swirls; the tutus are "hard snowflakes" because Morris wanted them to look frozen [documented, Time Out 2010]. For the mixed-sex corps, a crop top covered the chest and the headdress "neutralised" faces [documented]. The flower stamens copy the zigzags in Burns's drawings, and a single phallic calla lily is painted on the backdrop [documented, Pakledinaz; Jowitt]. The snow is French paper snow, thrown from bins in the wings; it took weeks to find the right kind [documented, Hutchinson 2020]. The tree has white electric lights switched on by the maid on the musical cue [documented].

### Premiere and reception
Early cast: Morris created for himself the pot-smoking hippie at the party; Kraig Patterson created the maid [documented, Dance Enthusiast]. Penny Hutchinson danced in the premiere run [documented, WWU 2020]. Full original cast not found in this pass. Reviews of the Brussels premiere were not found in this pass. When MMDG brought it to Brooklyn in 1992, the *LA Times* called it "tacky, nasty, brassy and delicately decadent" (15 December 1992) [documented]. Deborah Jowitt (2016) found it "outrageously funny but also poignant" [documented]. Money: Pakledinaz said he "couldn't believe" how much money the Monnaie gave them [documented]; no figure found. The sets are so large that it rarely tours [documented, Time Out].

### Versions and lineage
Brussels 1991; US premiere at BAM (December 1991 or 1992; the 1992 BAM season is documented in the LA Times review) [probable for 1991]. Filmed for PBS *Great Performances: Dance in America* (shown 1992 in Los Angeles; 1991 per Wikipedia: contested) [documented]. VHS and LaserDisc 1992; DVD 2007 [documented]. Ovation TV viewers voted it the favourite in its "Battle of the Nutcrackers" in 2007 to 2009 [documented, Wikipedia]. Pakledinaz remade costumes when Morris took over Dr Stahlbaum in 2010; most others were twenty years old [documented]. It is not licensed to ballet companies; MMDG keeps it [probable].

### Readings and disputes
- **Parody or love letter.** Many critics treat it as a camp send-up of ballet's Christmas cash cow. Joan Acocella, in *Mark Morris* (Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 1993), and Jowitt (2016) argue that its irony protects real feeling, ending in love [probable for Acocella; documented for Jowitt].
- **Queer reading.** The cross-casting, the calla lily and the adult Mrs Stahlbaum's "spring awakening" (Jowitt) invite a queer reading [documented]. Pakledinaz framed the mixed snow as practical: one silhouette, not "male snowflake" and "female" [documented]. Both are true of the same costume.
- **Which decade?** Wikipedia and Hutchinson set it in the 1960s; the Dance Enthusiast and Jowitt say the 1970s; the register says 1970s [contested; the programme should decide].
- **Who conceived it?** Time Out says Burns "conceived the work along with Morris"; Pakledinaz says Burns "knew he wasn't the designer" [contested].

### Facts worth telling
- The snowflakes' headdresses are Dairy Queen ice-cream swirls [documented].
- The snow is thrown by the dancers, not dropped from the flies [documented].
- The maid is a man on pointe; the Rat King a woman on pointe [documented].
- There is no Sugar Plum Fairy [documented].
- It premiered in Belgium, not America [documented].
- Morris later moved from the hippie role to Dr Stahlbaum [documented].

### Set it beside
- `nutcracker-1892`: the source; Morris keeps every note and changes every image.
- `swan-lake-bourne-1995` (and Bourne's *Nutcracker!*, 1992): the British remake movement a year later; Bourne recasts by period and institution, Morris by comic art and gender.
- `coppelia-1870`: the original doll-maker; Morris's Drosselmeier and his robot toys belong to T04.
- `alice-wheeldon-2011`: another Victorian-to-fantasy family party, played for spectacle rather than irony.

### What a maker can take from it
- Borrow an outside artist's visual language (here a cartoonist) to re-see a familiar world.
- Go back to the original source: the forgotten part of a story can be the fresh part.
- Write the colour rules early (black and white, then red and green) so every designer can work alone.

### Beyond the stage
PBS film, home video and DVD [documented]. Burns's own fame grew with the graphic novel *Black Hole* (1995 to 2005) [probable].

## Le Parc / The Park (1994)
`id: le-parc-1994` (new) · threads: T18, T22, T15 (inverted), T01 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 9 April 1994, Palais Garnier, Paris Opéra Ballet [documented, Ballet Preljocaj; Mariinsky; Romaeuropa archive]. Choreographer Angelin Preljocaj. Music Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart (movements from symphonies, serenades, divertimenti and piano concertos), with sound design by Goran Vejvoda for the gardeners' prologue, entr'actes and epilogue [documented]. No librettist; scenario by Preljocaj after French 17th- and 18th-century love literature [documented]. Sets Thierry Leproust; costumes Hervé Pierre; lighting Jacques Chatelet (Éric Soyer added for the 2027 Ballet Preljocaj restaging); choreologist Dany Lévêque [documented, Ballet Preljocaj]. Three acts played without interval, about 1 hour 30 to 1 hour 40; 21 or 22 dancers (both counts are given by the company) [documented]. The version seen today: the Paris Opéra's own, revived often, and stagings by the Mariinsky (from 14 April 2011), the Bavarian State Ballet and, from 2027, Preljocaj's own company [documented].

### In one line
Four gardeners in dark glasses steer a young man and woman, and a crowd of courtiers, through a formal French garden of love, from first glance to a final kiss in which she flies.

### How it came to be
Preljocaj, born in 1957 near Paris to Albanian parents, came from contemporary dance, not ballet [probable]. His company, founded in 1984, was known for blunt, physical works such as a Balkan *Les Noces* [documented, Romaeuropa review 1994; Mariinsky essay]. *Le Parc* was his first full ballet made for the Paris Opéra [documented, Concertonet 2013]. The Opéra's director of dance in 1994 was Patrick Dupond [documented, Romaeuropa credits]. Preljocaj asked a question in 1994: "How is love doing ... faced with AIDS?" [documented, company text, his words]. He looked for "an art of love" in the French literature of the 17th and 18th centuries [documented]. He took the stars Isabelle Guérin and Laurent Hilaire for the lovers [documented]. The Mariinsky's essay recalls that the premiere caused "jubilant bewilderment": "classical stars trusting an avant-garde choreographer?" [documented]. The work was on tour at Romaeuropa in Rome by July 1994 [documented] and won a Benois de la Danse in Moscow in 1995 [documented].

### Roots and reference points
Preljocaj named four literary sources [documented, company text]:
- Madame de La Fayette, *La Princesse de Clèves* (1678): love held back by duty.
- Madeleine de Scudéry's *Carte du Tendre* (1654, in *Clélie*): a map of the road to the heart, with towns called Tenderness and lakes called Indifference. Preljocaj calls it a "precious" board game of conquest [documented].
- Choderlos de Laclos, *Les Liaisons dangereuses* (1782): calculated seduction.
- Marivaux's comedies of disguise, such as *Le Jeu de l'amour et du hasard* (1730) [probable for the play; the company names Marivaux].
- Visual sources: the French formal garden of Le Nôtre, and Watteau's paintings of *fêtes galantes* [documented for Watteau in the Mariinsky essay; garden documented in the company text].
- Ballet: the Mariinsky essay argues the work descends from *The Sleeping Beauty*, a story of a court that sleeps for a century and wakes [argued, Mariinsky].

### The world it built
Three acts, each in the same pattern: an ensemble opening, divertissements, then a duet on the slow movement of a piano concerto, each duet more intense than the last [documented, Concertonet 2013]. Act I opens with the Adagio of the "Linz" Symphony (K. 425), which returns before Act III like an operatic prelude [documented]. Act I: the sexes observe each other (Adagio and Fugue, K. 546), play games of approach (German Dances, K. 571), and meet (Andantino of Piano Concerto No. 14, K. 449) [documented, Romaeuropa running order]. Act II: "tender attractions" to the Rondo of *Eine kleine Nachtmusik* and more [documented]. Act III: the night duet and the release [documented]. The gardeners dance to Vejvoda's electronic soundtrack and so belong to another time [documented]. A Rome critic in 1994 found their entr'actes, "now with Egyptian moves," "frankly incomprehensible" (Chiara Vatteroni, *Il Piccolo*) [documented]. Movement rules: courtly bows and ballet lines broken by sudden, carnal gestures; in one duet a woman puts her fingers in her mouth as if eating them [documented, Mariinsky essay]. Signature passage: the last duet, known as the "flying kiss." The man spins with his mouth locked to hers, without holding her, while she, arms round his neck, flies out horizontally [documented, Mariinsky; Bayerische Staatsoper].

### Design and art
Leproust's garden is nature "controlled, designed, precisely 'unnatured'" [documented, company text]. Hedges are cut as if with a blowtorch; tree branches are pruned into cages "to hold hearts prisoner"; a heavy sky of clouds threatens storms [documented]. Critics describe a "geometric set with clean lines" [documented, Concertonet]. Pierre's costumes follow the Enlightenment, but the women move from opulent court dress, with panniers and corsets, to "light and airy gowns," and the men shed coats to shirts [documented, Bayerische Staatsoper; ResMusica]. The garden of Act III is lit a nocturnal blue "constellated with stars" that melt at dawn [documented, Concertonet]. The gardeners' dark glasses mean that "love is blind" [documented, company text]. The Mariinsky essay describes them with torches on their foreheads, like archaeologists digging up the gallant age [documented]. The design idea: a historical surface held inside a modern frame [argued].

### Premiere and reception
Lovers: Isabelle Guérin and Laurent Hilaire; Clotilde Vayer, Elisabeth Maurin and Manuel Legris also danced in the 1994 Rome run [documented]. *Le Figaro* (11 April 1994) called it "a masterstroke" and "a masterpiece of invention, taste and sensibility" [documented, company press page]. *Ballet 2000* (summer 1994) wrote that "Le Parc can't help but give pleasure" [documented]. Luigi Rossi (*Famiglia Cristiana*, 10 August 1994) praised how Preljocaj fitted "the climate and history of the Opéra" [documented]. By 2009 ResMusica called it "a timeless classic," given for the sixth time [documented]. Hilaire later coached the lead role as ballet master [documented, ResMusica]. No box-office figures found.

### Versions and lineage
Paris Opéra 1994 and many revivals (1995, 1997, ..., 2009, 2013, 2026) [documented for 2009, 2013, 2026]. A film of the Guérin and Hilaire cast won the 1999 Grand Prix International de Vidéo-Danse [documented; director not found]. Mariinsky 14 April 2011 [documented]; Bavarian State Ballet (date not found; streamed on Staatsoper.tv) [documented]; Ballet Preljocaj restaging 2027 with Soyer's lighting [documented]. Notation: a choreologist (Lévêque) is credited, which implies a written score of the dance [probable].

### Readings and disputes
- **AIDS and restraint.** Preljocaj frames the work as a response to love under AIDS: resistance heightens desire [documented, his 1994 text]. The Mariinsky essay reads the lovers as "children of a more cruel time" [documented].
- **A ballet about ballet's history.** The Mariinsky essay reads it as an heir of *The Sleeping Beauty* [argued]. The company presents it as literary [documented]. Both see a court world revived by moderns.
- **Gender.** The "war of the sexes" is the company's phrase [documented]. A feminist reading would ask who controls the game: four male gardeners steer everyone [argued; no scholar found in this pass].
- **Which concerto for the kiss?** Concertonet (2013) puts the kiss on the Adagio of Piano Concerto No. 23 (K. 488); ResMusica (2009) describes the Act III adagio on the Andantino of No. 15 [contested; probably different duets, needs the score].
- **"The longest kiss."** Concertonet calls it "reputed to be the longest in choreographic history" [documented as reputation; no measurement exists].

### Facts worth telling
- The gardeners wear sunglasses because "love is blind" [documented].
- The hedges look cut with a blowtorch [documented].
- The music mixes Mozart with modern electronic sound [documented].
- Preljocaj directed and choreographed the Air France advertising film *L'Envol* (2011), listed on the company's *Le Parc* page; that it reuses the flying-kiss duet is probable, not checked [documented for the credit].
- It won a Benois de la Danse in 1995 [documented].
- Preljocaj was 37 at the premiere [documented, Mariinsky].

### Set it beside
- `sleeping-beauty-1890`: the court ballet that the Mariinsky essay calls its ancestor.
- `manon-1974`: an 18th-century French novel of desire, told as narrative; Preljocaj takes only the codes, not a plot.
- `in-the-middle-1987`: made on the same Paris stars seven years earlier; both use the Opéra's étoiles against its traditions.
- `like-water-for-chocolate-2022`: forbidden love made physical through a single image (food there, a kiss here).

### What a maker can take from it
- Build a world from a set of social codes (manners, maps, games) rather than a plot.
- Put a "frame figure" (the gardeners) from another time inside a historical world: it makes the past readable now.
- One signature image, if strong enough, can travel far beyond the work (here into an airline advertisement).

### Beyond the stage
The 1999 film; Air France's *L'Envol* (2011, agency BETC Euro RSCG) [documented]. Staatsoper.tv streaming [documented].

### Sources and open images
- Ballet Preljocaj, *Le Parc* (1994) and *Le Parc* restaging (2027): https://preljocaj.org/creation/le-parc and https://preljocaj.org/en/creation/le-parc-reprise_ballet_preljocaj [seen].
- Mariinsky Theatre, *Le Parc* repertory page and essay: https://www.mariinsky.ru/en/playbill/repertoire/ballet/park_preljocaj [seen].
- Romaeuropa Festival archive 1994 (running order and press): https://romaeuropa.net/archivio/festival/anno-1994/le-parc [seen].
- Concertonet review, Palais Garnier, 7 December 2013: http://www.concertonet.com/scripts/review.php?ID_review=9528 [seen].
- Delphine Goater, "Le Parc d'Angelin Preljocaj, ou les jeux de l'amour," ResMusica, 15 March 2009: https://www.resmusica.com/2009/03/15/les-jeux-de-l-amour-2 [seen].
- Bayerische Staatsoper, Staatsoper.tv page: https://www.staatsoper.de/en/tv/translate-to-english-le-parc-staatsopertv [seen].

## Rites / Rites (1997)
`id: rites-page-1997` · threads: T13, T16, T23, T14 · confidence of the header: documented

> Protocol note: Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander readers are advised that this dossier names people who have died. Bangarra's materials are covered by its Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property (ICIP) policy; all its images are link-only here.

**Header.** Premiere 29 October 1997, State Theatre, Victorian Arts Centre (now Arts Centre Melbourne), Melbourne International Festival of the Arts; The Australian Ballet with Bangarra Dance Theatre [documented, 1999 programme; Australian Ballet history; Arts Centre Melbourne 2022]. Choreographer Stephen Page, artistic director of Bangarra. Music Igor Stravinsky, *The Rite of Spring* (1913), played live (State Orchestra of Victoria, conductor Charles Barker in 1997; a reduction by Jonathan McPhee on the 1999 tour) [documented, programmes]. Set Peter England; costumes Jennifer Irwin; lighting Mark Howett [documented]. One act in six sections: Awakening, Earth, Wind, Fire, Water, Dreaming [documented, Bangarra]. Commissioned by Ross Stretton, new artistic director of the Australian Ballet, for the festival directed by Clifford Hocking [documented]. Length: Bangarra lists 20 minutes, which is short for the full score and needs a check [contested]. The version seen today: the original, revived in 1999, 2006 (in the programme *Gathering*) and 2008; not staged by other companies [documented].

### In one line
Stravinsky's sacrifice score becomes a cycle of the elements of an ancient Australian landscape, danced by Indigenous and classical dancers together, opened and closed by a Yolngu songman.

### How it came to be
Stephen Page, who describes himself as a Munundjali and Nunukul man of the Yugambeh and Quandamooka Nations, born in 1965,, had led Bangarra since 1991 [documented, Page in 2022 podcast; Potter 2009]. In 1996 he made *Alchemy* for the Australian Ballet [documented, 1999 programme]. Ross Stretton saw Bangarra's *Ochres* (1994) and *Alchemy*, and asked Page to come back "with your company" to make a work to *The Rite of Spring* [documented, Page, 2022 transcript]. Page later laughed that he "had no idea" what Stretton meant at first [documented]. The festival director Clifford Hocking set one condition: "a decidedly Australian view" of the Rite, "whether it be from Ayers Rock or Bondi Beach" [documented, Arts Centre Melbourne]. The making came months after the federal government refused to apologise to the Stolen Generations, and in the year of the first Australian Reconciliation Convention [documented, Brown 2022; education resource]. Page wrote that he hoped it would "challenge ... preconceptions about indigenous peoples" and move people "along the path of reconciliation" [documented, 1997 programme]. It was, in the companies' words, the earliest known work made on dancers of both companies together [documented].

### Roots and reference points
- **The Rite of Spring (1913)**: Stravinsky's score, Nijinsky's lost choreography and Roerich's pagan Russia (`rite-of-spring-1913`). Page keeps the score and its elemental force but drops the virgin sacrifice [documented that sections are elemental; argued as to the sacrifice].
- **Yolngu song and ceremony** from North East Arnhem Land, through Djakapurra Munyarryun, Wanguri clan leader and songman, Bangarra's cultural consultant [documented, Arts Centre Melbourne; Potter 2009].
- **The Dreaming**, a non-linear time in which past, present and future coincide; Page calls the elements "complex, ephemeral moments occurring simultaneously in a non-linear time frame" [documented].
- **Bangarra's own works**: *Praying Mantis Dreaming* (1992), *Ochres* (1994) [documented].
- **Use of traditional song** is "with kind permission of Nundhirrbala Clan, Numbulwar," on the 2006 cast sheet [documented]: a credit line that has no equivalent in any earlier Rite.

### The world it built
Six "snapshots or flashpoints," each with "its own ritual" [documented, Page]. Djakapurra Munyarryun opens and closes it, "like an emanation of rock and earth" [documented, Arts Centre Melbourne]. The 2006 cast sheet lists Wind, Fire Spirits, Fire, and Water with a "Black Naga," a traditional element danced by Patrick Thaiday [documented]. Dreaming gathers the whole ensemble: on "the soaked and ochred, slippery stage, canons of grounded movement circled out from Djakapurra" [documented, Brown 2022]. The rule of the world: ballet's vertical, lifted line meets Bangarra's grounded, earth-directed weight; classical women (Miranda Coney, Lucinda Dunn) and men (Steven Heathcote) dance beside Bangarra dancers [documented]. The Bangarra 1997 cast: Albert David, Marilyn Miller, Lea Francis, Djakapurra Munyarryun, Frances Rings, Russell Page, Gina Rings and Sidney Saltner [documented, Knowledge Ground]. Music and dance: Steven Heathcote said Page "tapped into the power and the subtleties of the music" [documented]. In New York in 1999 the orchestra waited while Munyarryun sang a Gathering Song before Stravinsky's first bassoon note [documented].

### Design and art
Peter England's set gave "powerful images of the landscape" on the backcloth [documented, Potter 2009]. He also designed a coolamon (a carrying vessel) in consultation with Munyarryun [documented, Arts Centre Melbourne]. Mark Howett's light "played across the stage floor and on the backcloth" [documented, Potter]. Jennifer Irwin's costumes were "sometimes body-hugging, sometimes loosely draped" [documented, Potter]. Irwin has worked with Page since he was 19; she describes her Bangarra costumes as "a skin," made with textured fabric, dyeing and painting, raffia, feathers and leaves, to which dancers add ochre that "bleeds and cakes" [documented, Bangarra interview]. The water of the final section soaks the stage [documented]. Ochre and water make the stage floor itself the Country [argued]. The festival director called the design "set in its own timeless world" [documented, 1997 programme].

### Premiere and reception
Shared bill with Stephen Baynes's *At the Edge of Night* and Twyla Tharp's *The Storyteller* [documented, 1997 programme]. Lee Christofis (*The Australian*, 1997) wrote that "Page returns everything to the earth and another dreaming" [documented]. A review quoted by the Australian Ballet said: "it is the most significant cultural event in this country in decades" [documented; author not shown]. The Guardian (2008 tour) praised how far it went "in transplanting traditional ritual culture on to the ballet stage" [documented, Bangarra page]. The New York run (City Center, October 1999) was the tour's success [documented, Australian Ballet history]. Michelle Potter, the Australian dance historian, judged the steps "relatively comfortable choreography," "an easy mix" of vocabularies, unlike Nijinsky's shattering original (*NLA News*, 2009) [documented]. Funding: an Arts 21 project of the Victorian Government's Community Support Fund [documented, 1999 programme].

### Versions and lineage
Melbourne and Sydney 1997; Australian tour (Sydney Opera House 21 April) and US tour (New York City Center; Virginia) 1999; *Gathering* with *Amalgamate* (2006, Melbourne 17 to 29 March, Sydney 7 to 29 April); Théâtre du Châtelet, Paris, 29 to 30 September 2008, and Sadler's Wells, London, 7 to 11 October 2008 [documented, Knowledge Ground]. The Australian Ballet and ABC hold the 1997 film [documented]. The companies later made *Amalgamate* (2006) and *Warumuk* (2012) [documented]. In the wider Rite lineage it sits between Bausch (`sacre-bausch-1975`) and the African-cast Bausch of 2021 (`common-grounds-acogny-2021`).

### Readings and disputes
- **Reconciliation.** Page and the companies frame it as a work of reconciliation and "shared history" [documented]. Yolande Brown, a Bidjara artist and its 2022 curator, says it "firmly closed the chapter" on works about Indigenous cultures made without them [documented, education resource].
- **Comfort.** Potter (2009) reads it as less radical than its source: the shock was in who was on stage, not in the steps [documented].
- **Sacrifice removed or kept?** Most writers present Rites as elemental, not sacrificial [documented]. The register lists it under T16 (sacrifice); this pass found no chosen-one figure, so the T16 tag is **argued** at best.
- **Dates.** Register and some web pages give 17 to 29 March 1997; those are the 2006 *Gathering* dates [documented]. Michelle Potter's "Stretton Legacy" list gives 29 October 1998, a slip for 1997 [documented both].
- **"We should sing first."** Page: "we are 60 thousand years older, so in a way he should respect us" [documented, Australian Ballet history]. A claim of precedence in time as authority over the score.

### Facts worth telling
- The traditional songs are credited "with kind permission" of a named clan [documented].
- In New York the orchestra paused for a song before the Stravinsky began [documented].
- Bangarra means "to make fire" in Wiradjuri [documented, Arts Centre Melbourne].
- The stage ends soaked with water and ochre [documented].
- Commissioned with one rule: "a decidedly Australian view" [documented].
- The same programme had a Tharp premiere [documented].

### Set it beside
- `rite-of-spring-1913`: same score, opposite ending: a girl sacrificed for spring there; the earth renewed here.
- `sacre-bausch-1975`: earth on the stage floor, as in Rites; Bausch's earth is a battlefield of gender, Page's is Country.
- `raka-1967`: South African ballet using African imagery with no African artists, under apartheid; the T23 wall contrast.
- `creation-du-monde-1923`: Paris imagines African creation; Rites is made by those whose creation story it tells.

### What a maker can take from it
- Who is on the team is part of the concept: authorship and permission are design decisions.
- A borrowed structure (a famous score) can be re-read through another culture's cosmology without being parodied.
- Credit sources as the culture requires (clan permission, songman), not only as copyright law requires.

### Beyond the stage
Arts Centre Melbourne online exhibition and podcast (2022) [documented]. Page's Olympic ceremony work (Atlanta 1996 handover; later Sydney 2000) shows the same blending at mass scale [documented for 1996].

- Bangarra, *Rites*: https://www.bangarra.com.au/productions/rites [seen]; Bangarra Knowledge Ground (credits, touring, casts, 1997 programme): https://bangarra-knowledgeground.com.au/productions/rites [seen].
- Programmes 1997 and 1999 (PDF via Knowledge Ground) [seen]; *Gathering* cast sheet 2006 [seen].
- Yolande Brown (curator), *Rites: Dancing towards a shared history*, Arts Centre Melbourne, 2022: https://stories.artscentremelbourne.com.au/rites [seen]; podcast transcript and education resource (Shane Carroll) [seen].

## Chroma / Chroma (2006)
`id: chroma-2006` (new) · threads: T10, T09, T11 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 17 November 2006, Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, London, The Royal Ballet, in a triple bill with Balanchine's *The Four Temperaments* and Christopher Wheeldon's *DGV: Danse à grande vitesse* [documented, ROH Collections; BBC News 17 November 2006]. "Concept, direction and choreography" Wayne McGregor [documented, Studio Wayne McGregor]. Music Joby Talbot (four pieces: *Cloudpark*, *'...a yellow disc rising from the sea...'*, *Transit of Venus*, *Hovercraft*) and Jack White III of the White Stripes (*Aluminum*, *The Hardest Button to Button*, *Blue Orchid*, arranged by Talbot); orchestrations Christopher Austin; played live [documented, ROH Collections; Ailey credits]. No scenario. Set John Pawson; costumes Moritz Junge; lighting Lucy Carter [documented]. One act, about 25 minutes (29 in Ailey's timing); ten dancers [documented]. The version seen today: the original design, restaged by Antoine Vereecken and others for companies including San Francisco Ballet, the Bolshoi, Royal Danish Ballet, National Ballet of Canada, Boston Ballet, Alvin Ailey American Dance Theater (2013), the Australian Ballet (2014) and Dutch National Ballet [documented, Studio Wayne McGregor].

### In one line
Ten dancers in pale slips burst through one opening in a white architectural box, to orchestral rock, and twist ballet's lines to extremes.

### How it came to be
McGregor came from contemporary dance; his company Random Dance worked with scientists and digital media [probable]. He had made works for the Opera House's small Linbury Studio Theatre; *Chroma* was his second for the main stage [documented, Wikipedia; Australian Ballet primer]. He had read the architect John Pawson's book *Minimum* (1996) and was "captivated by this notion of subtraction" [documented, McGregor's programme note]. He asked Pawson for "amazing plain canvases in which anything can happen" [documented, Wikipedia]. Pawson had never designed for the stage [documented]. For music, McGregor liked Talbot's *Hovercraft*, written for the Kensington Symphony Orchestra, and asked what else might fit [documented, primer]. Talbot had just made an album of orchestral "re-imaginings" of White Stripes songs, *Aluminium*, which Jack and Meg White "loved," Talbot said [documented, BBC 2006]. McGregor said he made the piece in three weeks, four to five hours a day [documented, Wikipedia]. Its success led the Royal Ballet to make him resident choreographer, the first from contemporary dance [documented that it led to the post; "first" not checked, so not claimed].

### Roots and reference points
- **Minimalist architecture.** Pawson's houses and monasteries reduce a room to planes, light and one opening [documented, Pawson].
- **The word.** "Chroma" is purity of colour; its second sense, "freedom from white," gave the idea [documented, primer; Fjord Review].
- **Rock music in ballet.** The Royal Ballet had danced to the Beatles and Jimi Hendrix before [documented, BBC 2006]. *Chroma* was nicknamed "the White Stripes ballet" [documented, primer].
- **Forsythe's tilted classicism.** The hyper-extended, off-axis body follows the line from `in-the-middle-1987` [argued].
- **Balanchine's leotard ballets.** Shown on the same night: *The Four Temperaments* (1946) [documented].

### The world it built
No story; sections follow the seven music tracks [documented]. Rules: all women off pointe, the only McGregor Royal Ballet commission entirely so [documented, primer]. One entrance and exit, the aperture in the back wall [documented, Pawson quoted in Fjord Review]. Dancers stand along the sides and watch; then they rush in [probable]. Movement: speed against stillness, fluid against angular; legs at extreme extension, spines rippling, heads ducking, hips jutting [documented, primer; Brown 2010]. Signature passages: the full-cast stomp on *The Hardest Button to Button* [documented as a filmed excerpt]; the duets of the first cast, "pencil-drawn lines" of Cojocaru and Watson and a "more classical duet" for Lamb and Bonelli [documented, primer]. Music: the Guardian heard "action-movie capers, romantic piano tinklings, gladiatorial fanfares" (2013) [documented]. The rock songs drive; Talbot's own pieces give the slow passages [argued].

### Design and art
Pawson's set is a pale, almost empty room, "closed on three sides except for a large aperture on the back wall" [documented, Pawson in Australian Ballet programme 2014]. Pawson describes the motifs as "inside and outside, entrance and exit, light and shadow, void and plenitude" [documented, johnpawson.com]. The set never moves, but Carter's light changes how it reads: the back wall seems nearer or further, the opening dominant or receding [documented]. The light turns the box white, grey, blue or green [documented, Architecture in Notes]. Junge's costumes are short slips and pants in pale tones, each dancer "slipped inside a tube of colour to compliment their unique skin tone" (Australian Ballet, 2014) [documented, Fjord Review]. Ismene Brown disliked "the unappealing little grey and beige vests and skimpy pants" [documented, theartsdesk 2010]. The meaning: the body becomes "absolutely architectural," McGregor wrote [documented]. The architect's photographer was Richard Davies [documented].

### Premiere and reception
Original cast: Federico Bonelli, Alina Cojocaru, Lauren Cuthbertson, Sarah Lamb, Steven McRae, Ludovic Ondiviela, Tamara Rojo, Eric Underwood, Jonathan Watkins and Edward Watson [documented]. Run: 17 to 29 November 2006 [documented, ROH Collections; BBC]. Debra Craine (*The Times*): "ravishing on all fronts" [documented, quoted by the company]. Judith Mackrell (*Guardian*): "the tension between chaos and minimalism ... ratchets up to thrilling extremes" [documented, quoted by the company]. Awards 2007: Olivier Award for Best New Dance Production; Critics' Circle National Dance Award (classical choreography); South Bank Show Award [documented, Wikipedia]. Talbot told the BBC that rock was "not a gimmick" to draw non-ballet audiences [documented].

### Versions and lineage
Royal Ballet revivals 2008, 2010, 2013 and 2016 [documented, ROH Collections and reviews]. In November 2016, for McGregor's tenth year, it was danced by Royal Ballet and Alvin Ailey dancers together [documented]. Restaged by many companies (see Header). Notation: not found; transmitted by restagers who encourage dancers to "mould" the steps into their bodies (Vereecken, *Sydney Morning Herald*, 2014) [documented, quoted by Fjord Review]. The line it starts: McGregor's *Infra* (2008), *Limen* (2009) and, for narrative, `woolf-works-2015`.

### Readings and disputes
- **Architecture of the body.** McGregor and Pawson present the body as architecture in a "freedom space" [documented]. Gracia Haby (*Fjord Review*, 2014) sees "the body in flux become architecture" [documented].
- **A trademark, not a discovery.** Ismene Brown (2010) argues that McGregor's early "intriguing accidents" became "permanently ironed in, like Issey Miyake pleats" [documented]. G. J. Dowler (Classical Source, 2016) calls his world "a cold place," his dancers "like automata," but calls *Chroma* his masterpiece [documented]. The two critics agree on the style and disagree on this work.
- **Pop or gimmick?** The White Stripes link brought press attention; Talbot denied a gimmick [documented]. The primer argues it inspired "a whole new gamut of pop ballets" [documented as their claim].
- **Bodies.** The demand for hyper-mobility raises the question of which bodies can dance it; no study found in this pass [argued].

### Facts worth telling
- It was Pawson's earliest stage design [documented].
- The set never moves; only the light changes it [documented].
- The women never go on pointe [documented].
- It was made in about three weeks [documented, McGregor via Wikipedia].
- Jack White approved the arrangements [documented, Talbot to BBC].
- The 2016 revival mixed two companies on stage [documented].

### Set it beside
- `in-the-middle-1987`: the parent of the hyper-extended classical line; Forsythe's black box against Pawson's white one.
- `in-the-upper-room-1986`: another plotless piece where light builds the space; Tharp hides the edges in fog, Pawson draws them hard.
- `triadic-ballet-1922`: Schlemmer turned dancers into geometry with costume; McGregor does it with almost no costume [argued].
- `four-temperaments-1946`: the Balanchine work it shared its first night with.

### What a maker can take from it
- Subtract: one opening, one material, one rule can make a whole world.
- Let a fixed set change by light: the cheapest way to make one space into many.
- Cross a "high" and "low" source (an architect and a rock band) so each makes the other strange.

### Beyond the stage
Talbot's *Aluminium* album (2006) [documented, BBC]. ROH filmed excerpts and a 2007 interview [documented]. McGregor later choreographed for film and music videos [probable].

## Alice's Adventures in Wonderland / Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (2011)
`id: alice-wheeldon-2011` · threads: T06, T01, T18, T20 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 28 February 2011, Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, London, The Royal Ballet; co-production with the National Ballet of Canada [documented, christopherwheeldon.com; Wikipedia; Dance Magazine]. Choreographer Christopher Wheeldon. Music Joby Talbot (commissioned; orchestration Christopher Austin and Talbot; large percussion section and four female voices) [documented]. Scenario Nicholas Wright, playwright, after Lewis Carroll (1865) [documented]. Designer (sets and costumes) Bob Crowley; lighting Natasha Katz; projections Jon Driscoll and Gemma Carrington; sound Andrew Bruce (Autograph); puppets Toby Olié; magic consultant Paul Kieve [documented, RBO; British Theatre Guide]. Two acts in 2011, re-cut into three acts for the 2012 revival; about 1 hour 40 minutes of music [documented, DanceTabs 2012; Wikipedia]. The version seen today: the three-act version, in the original designs, at the Royal Ballet and in the repertories of the National Ballet of Canada, Royal Danish Ballet, the Mariinsky, Bavarian State Ballet, the Australian Ballet, Royal Swedish Ballet and the New National Theatre Tokyo [documented, Wheeldon site].

### In one line
A teenage Alice at an Oxford garden party falls through a bag into a Wonderland where her family and guests return as the Queen of Hearts, the Mad Hatter and the rest, and she follows a boy accused of stealing a tart.

### How it came to be
Wheeldon trained at the Royal Ballet School, danced with the Royal Ballet and then New York City Ballet, and made his name with plotless ballets such as *Polyphonia* (2001) [probable]. In 2011 the Royal Ballet had not commissioned a new full-length story ballet since 1995 [documented, Guardian; DanceTabs says "fifteen years"]. Dance Magazine counted back to Twyla Tharp's "short-lived" *Mr Worldly Wise* (dated 1996 there; 1995 in other sources) [documented; date contested]. Wheeldon wanted "something lighter and more friendly" because the company already had "a wealth of full-blooded ballets" [documented, Wikipedia quoting him]. He brought in Nicholas Wright to shape Carroll's "episodic dream tale" into a story [documented, Guardian 6 March 2011]. Karen Kain, director of the National Ballet of Canada, heard of the project and proposed the co-production [documented, Wikipedia]. Talbot said he "wanted to find a new sound, the right timbre for Wonderland" [documented]. It was made on Lauren Cuthbertson, just back from a long illness [documented, BTG]. It sold out its seven performances before it opened [documented, Dance Magazine]. Wheeldon dedicated it to Ninette de Valois [documented, BTG].

### Roots and reference points
- **Lewis Carroll (Charles Dodgson), 1865**, and the real Liddell family at Christ Church, Oxford; Carroll himself appears as a character who becomes the White Rabbit [documented, cast list].
- **Ashton's English story ballets**: *La Fille mal gardée* (`fille-mal-gardee-ashton-1960`), *Tales of Beatrix Potter*, *Enigma Variations*; and de Valois's *Checkmate* (`checkmate-1937`) with its card-like court [documented as reviewers' comparisons].
- **The Rose Adagio** from `sleeping-beauty-1890`, parodied by the Queen of Hearts and four quaking courtiers [documented].
- **The double cast of the dream** (real people become fantasy figures), as in *The Nutcracker* (`nutcracker-1892`) and *The Wizard of Oz* [documented for the doubling; Oz argued].
- **Lotte Reiniger's silhouette animation**, which Crowley acknowledged as a debt [documented, BTG].
- **Musical theatre and tap**: the Mad Hatter tap-dances [documented].

### The world it built
Prologue: a garden party outside the deanery at Christ Church, Oxford. Alice and her sisters, Carroll, her parents, a magician, a rajah, a vicar and Jack, the gardener's boy whom Alice loves [documented, Guardian; cast list]. Jack is accused of stealing a tart and dismissed. Alice falls down the rabbit hole through Carroll's Gladstone bag [documented, BTG]. Every adult is doubled: Mother becomes the Queen of Hearts, Father the King, the Magician the Mad Hatter, the Rajah the Caterpillar, Jack the Knave of Hearts [documented, cast list]. Episodes: the Duchess's kitchen (the Duchess played by the actor Simon Russell Beale in the first cast), the Caterpillar's Arabian dance with a chorus line of "sparkling pointe shoes," the tea party, the flower waltz, the Queen's "Tart Adagio," a croquet game with flamingos and hedgehogs, the cards, the Knave's trial [documented, Dance Magazine; BTG]. Alice solos before projected words, "Who are you?" [documented]. Talbot's score: "ticking time, jazzy dissonance, fairground music, castanets, romantic lyricism," with ballet quotations embedded for the Tart Adagio and a tango for the Queen [documented, BTG]. Rule of the world: logic of the dream; every object can change scale; Alice "seldom leaves" the stage [documented, Dance Magazine].

### Design and art
Crowley's design is a machine of tricks: projections of the rabbit hole with a marionette Alice tumbling down; a Cheshire Cat built as a giant puppet whose segments "coalesce and dissolve" [documented, Dance Magazine; Classical Source]. Colours are "bright reds, greens, blacks and whites," while Alice "flits through it all in muted lilac" [documented, BTG]. There are Victorian paper flower cut-outs, paper boats, a "sponge cake trampoline," meat cleavers and an axe dripping heart-shaped blood [documented]. Doors whizz round by remote control, confetti rains, and dancers waltz in the aisles of the stalls [documented, Classical Source]. One critic saw in the projections Soviet avant-garde cinema, the spiral of Hitchcock's *Vertigo*, Vorticists and Futurists [documented as reading, BTG]. The prologue backcloth is realistic; the dream world flattens into graphic colour [documented]. Carroll's original illustrator John Tenniel is the shared visual memory behind every Alice; Crowley does not copy him [argued].

### Premiere and reception
First cast: Lauren Cuthbertson (Alice), Sergei Polunin (Jack/Knave), Edward Watson (Carroll/White Rabbit), Zenaida Yanowsky (Mother/Queen of Hearts), Christopher Saunders (Father/King), Steven McRae (Magician/Mad Hatter), Eric Underwood (Rajah/Caterpillar), Simon Russell Beale (Duchess) [documented, Wikipedia]. Debra Craine (*The Times*): "a big artful piece of popular theater" [documented, quoted by Wheeldon]. Laura Thompson (*Telegraph*): "absurdist, dark and almost beyond praise" [documented]. Sarah Crompton (*Telegraph*): it "needed a little more dance and a little less action" [documented, Wikipedia]. Barbara Newman (*Dance Magazine*): "the choreography fails to keep up with the musical and visual invention" [documented]. Vera Liber called it "critic proof" [documented, BTG]. It was costly ("this elaborate, costly production," Newman) [documented]; no figure found.

### Versions and lineage
2011 Royal Ballet; National Ballet of Canada North American premiere 2011 with Jillian Vanstone [documented]. 2012 revival re-cut into three acts (the first act had run 70 minutes) and strengthened the Knave's part [documented, DanceTabs; Classical Source]. Televised (BBC) and released on DVD [documented that a TV screening happened, DanceTabs comment; DVD 2017 cast in Wikipedia]. Bachtrack's January 2025 statistics list it among the most performed full-length works, performed by at least seven companies other than the Royal Ballet [documented, register citing CENSUS §1.1]. The team (Wheeldon, Talbot, Crowley, Katz) went on to `the-winters-tale-2014` and `like-water-for-chocolate-2022`.

### Readings and disputes
- **Spectacle over dance.** Newman (Dance Magazine, 2011), Crompton (*Telegraph*, 2011) and G. J. Dowler (Classical Source, 2012) argue that design swallows choreography: "the eye was drawn to ... the effect and not the content" [documented]. Wheeldon's supporters (Craine; Royal Ballet) answer that it is "popular theater" by design [documented].
- **Cash cow.** Dowler called the 2012 revival a hoped-for "cash-cow" and the new interval a source of catering money [documented, said as a joke]. Box-office data: sold out [documented]; income not found.
- **Alice's age.** Wheeldon presents a teenager, "not ... a little blonde Disney Alice" (BBC, 2011) [documented]; this adds a romance (Jack) that Carroll does not have [documented by comparison]. Some critics found Jack "less strongly drawn" [documented, DanceTabs].
- **Carroll and the real Alice.** The prologue puts Carroll at the Liddell family party; biographers dispute the nature of Dodgson's friendship with Alice Liddell. The ballet keeps him gentle and melancholy [documented casting; the biographical dispute is outside this pass, probable].
- **"First" claims.** The Guardian says the first full-length story ballet since 1995; the BBC says the first new full-length for 15 years and the first specially composed score for 20 years [documented, all]: consistent with each other but not with Dance Magazine's 1996 [contested on the date of *Mr Worldly Wise*].

### Facts worth telling
- Alice enters Wonderland through a Gladstone bag [documented].
- An actor (Simon Russell Beale) danced the Duchess [documented].
- The Mad Hatter tap-dances [documented].
- The Queen of Hearts parodies the Rose Adagio [documented].
- Crowley credited Lotte Reiniger's silhouette films [documented].
- The ballet grew from two acts to three after one season [documented].
- It is dedicated to Ninette de Valois [documented].

### Set it beside
- `nutcracker-1892`: a family party that becomes a dream; Alice copies the doubling device.
- `hard-nut-1991`: the same device played for irony; Wheeldon plays it for wonder.
- `shim-chung-1986` and `fish-beauty-1959`: two-worlds ballets from Korea and China where the other world is moral, not absurd.
- `checkmate-1937`: de Valois's game-board court, the British ancestor of the card scenes.

### What a maker can take from it
- Double your cast: if every fantasy figure is a real person in disguise, the audience can follow a dream.
- Hire a writer for structure even when there will be no words.
- Watch the balance: when design and effect are rich, the core medium (here dance) must still carry the key moments.

### Beyond the stage
Cinema relays and DVD [documented for DVD]. Talbot's score released on record [probable]. Alice is among the most-licensed new full-length ballets of the century [documented via Bachtrack, see register].

## The Winter's Tale / The Winter's Tale (2014)
`id: the-winters-tale-2014` (new) · threads: T18, T15, T07, T19 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 10 April 2014, Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, London, The Royal Ballet; co-production with the National Ballet of Canada [documented, ABT archive; Wheeldon site; Wikipedia]. Choreographer Christopher Wheeldon. Music and orchestration Joby Talbot (commissioned; an onstage band in Act II) [documented, DanceTabs]. Scenario: Wheeldon after Shakespeare (c. 1611); the play was suggested by the theatre director Nicholas Hytner [documented, DanceTabs]. Sets and costumes Bob Crowley; lighting Natasha Katz; projections Daniel Brodie; silk effects Basil Twist [documented, ABT; Classical Source]. Conductor David Briskin (National Ballet of Canada) [documented]. Prologue and three acts (Prologue and Act I 49 minutes, Act II 37, Act III 24) [documented, ROH cinema programme]. The version seen today: the original production, in the Royal Ballet and National Ballet of Canada repertories (North American premiere 2015) and staged by American Ballet Theatre [documented that ABT holds it in its repertory archive; date not found].

### In one line
A king's sudden jealousy destroys his wife and son; sixteen years later their lost daughter, raised by shepherds, brings the court back to life, and a statue of the dead queen breathes.

### How it came to be
After *Alice* (2011) the same team (Wheeldon, Talbot, Crowley, Katz) wanted to tackle something darker [probable]. Hytner suggested *The Winter's Tale*. Wheeldon said ballet could deal with the jumps in Shakespeare's "problem" play better than most stage productions [documented, DanceTabs]. The premiere was timed to Shakespeare's 450th anniversary; a performance fell on 23 April, his traditional birthday, and a live cinema relay went to more than 400 UK screens on 28 April [documented, The Arts Shelf]. It was the Royal Ballet's first full-length Shakespeare since MacMillan's *Romeo and Juliet* (1965) [documented, press release as reported]. It was Wheeldon's seventh work and second full-length for the company [documented]. A second cast led by Marianela Nuñez and Thiago Soares danced in the first run [documented].

### Roots and reference points
- **Shakespeare's *The Winter's Tale***: jealousy in Sicilia, the baby abandoned on the coast of Bohemia, "Exit, pursued by a bear," the sheep-shearing festival, the statue scene [documented by synopsis].
- **Robert Greene's *Pandosto* (1588)**, Shakespeare's own source [probable; not used directly by the ballet].
- **MacMillan's *Mayerling*** (`mayerling-1978`): the opening black backdrop and diagonal light "strongly recall" its graveside scene, and Watson's Leontes channels MacMillan's Rudolf [documented, theartsdesk 2014].
- **The Pygmalion statue** (`pygmalion-salle-1734`): Hermione's statue coming to life echoes ballet's oldest maker-and-made story [argued].
- **Romantic painting**: a Caspar David Friedrich painting on the palace wall and plain frock coats evoke "an 1820s Germany of repressive suspicion" [documented, Weibye, theartsdesk].
- **Folk dance**: the Bohemian peasants wear "colourful Central Asian skirts and patched jerkins" [documented]; another critic called the act "Balkan" [documented, Classical Source].

### The world it built
The structure folds Shakespeare's first three acts into one Act I, after an invented Prologue in which two boy kings are parted and reunited as men [documented, Classical Source; programme]. Act I, Sicilia: Hermione is heavily pregnant; Leontes's jealousy grows; he orders the baby abandoned; Mamillius, the young prince, dies of distress and Hermione collapses [documented]. Coda: Antigonus is shipwrecked on Bohemia's shore and killed by a bear made of silk waves; a shepherd and his son find the baby [documented, DanceTabs]. Act II, Bohemia, sixteen years later: Perdita and Prince Florizel dance under a great tree; the spring festival, an onstage band, the crowning of a May Queen; Polixenes, in disguise, unmasks and condemns the match [documented, programme]. Act III, Sicilia: Leontes mourns at the clifftop graves; Perdita is recognised; Paulina reveals the statue of Hermione, which comes to life [documented]. Movement rules: in Act I "the wrenched distortions" of Leontes's movement "mimic the unravelling syntax of the original verse" (Guardian, 2014) [documented]. Hermione dances full out while pregnant, which one critic found "ludicrously implausible" [documented, DanceTabs]. Act II answers with "an extraordinary folk vitality" (Macaulay, NYT 2014) [documented]. Music: the Sicilian court gets "angry claustrophobia"; Bohemia, light and colour [documented, Guardian]. Dowler found the score "the weakest element," lacking "big melodies" for the duets [documented].

### Design and art
Crowley mixes the physical and the projected: large moving Π-shaped structures and staircases; a backcloth carrying projected clouds, snow, a tempest at sea, a tree passing through the seasons [documented, Classical Source]. Sicilia is "chill white marble" [documented, Guardian], with steep stairs down which Leontes, and then the dying boy "teddy in hand," stumble [documented, theartsdesk]. Bohemia's tree is "fabulous, gnarly, green and glass-hung," a Tree of Life spreading across the stage [documented]. Basil Twist's silks become the sea and then the bear [documented]. The two kings' retinues are coloured in contrasting cool and warm shades [documented]. Perdita wears purple "to resemble Hermione" [documented, DanceTabs]. Silver paper fluttering from the ceiling had become "a Wheeldon/Crowley/Talbot/Katz trademark" [documented, theartsdesk]. Crowley's costumes "do not define place or time" [documented, Dowler]. The colour script is the plot: winter white, spring green, a return to a warmed white [argued].

### Premiere and reception
Cast: Edward Watson (Leontes), Lauren Cuthbertson (Hermione), Sarah Lamb (Perdita), Steven McRae (Florizel), Federico Bonelli (Polixenes), Zenaida Yanowsky (Paulina), Bennet Gartside (Antigonus), Joe Parker (Mamillius), Gary Avis (Father Shepherd), Valentino Zucchetti (Brother Clown), Beatriz Stix-Brunell (Young Shepherdess) [documented]. The Guardian headlined "a game-changer for Wheeldon" [documented]. Alastair Macaulay (*New York Times*): "nothing in Mr Wheeldon's version is unworthy of the great original" [documented, quoted by Wheeldon]. Joan Acocella (*New Yorker*): "brutal choreography and dramatic intelligence" [documented, quoted by Wheeldon]. Mark Monahan (*Telegraph*): "a modern classic" [documented]. Hanna Weibye (theartsdesk) found Watson over-acting "to the point of bathos" [documented]. Rupert Christiansen later called it "a crude reduction of that most delicately mysterious of plays" (*Spectator*, 2022) [documented]. No box-office figures found.

### Versions and lineage
Royal Ballet 2014, revived several times (2018 streamed digitally, per RBO link) [documented for a 2018 digital listing]; National Ballet of Canada 2015, with Piotr Stanczyk and Hannah Fischer [documented, Wikipedia]; American Ballet Theatre (date not found). Earlier Winter's Tale ballets: not found in this pass. The team's third full-length is `like-water-for-chocolate-2022`.

### Readings and disputes
- **Tragedy restored to the Wheeldon team.** The Guardian (2014) and Macaulay read it as proof that the *Alice* team could do tragedy [documented]. Christiansen (2022) and Dowler (2014) doubt its depth [documented]. Both sides admire the design.
- **Hermione's body.** DanceTabs objects to a heavily pregnant woman dancing full out; one could argue that the visible bump makes Leontes's jealousy physical and readable without words [documented objection; argued defence].
- **MacMillan's shadow.** Weibye sees too much of *Mayerling* in Leontes [documented]; one could reply that quoting MacMillan places the work in the Royal Ballet's own dramatic tradition [argued].
- **Where is Bohemia?** "Central Asian" (Weibye), "Balkan" (Dowler), timeless (Crowley's brief) [documented]: the folk world is a composite, not a place.
- **The problem play.** Hytner's and Wheeldon's argument that dance solves the play's jumps in time and genre [documented] against critics who find the comedy of Act II too long and Act III too short (24 minutes) [argued; the timing is documented].

### Facts worth telling
- The bear is made of silk [documented].
- There is a band on stage in Bohemia [documented].
- Hermione dances heavily pregnant, with a prosthetic bump [documented].
- It was the company's first full-length Shakespeare since 1965 [documented].
- More than 400 UK cinemas showed it live [documented].
- A Caspar David Friedrich painting hangs in Leontes's palace [documented].

### Set it beside
- `romeo-juliet-macmillan-1965`: the previous Royal Ballet full-length Shakespeare.
- `pygmalion-salle-1734`: the statue that wakes, 280 years earlier.
- `giselle-1841`: a peasant girl and a disguised prince; here the girl is secretly royal and the ending is happy.
- `alice-wheeldon-2011`: same team; the same device of a fantasy world opposed to a real one, made tragic.

### What a maker can take from it
- Give each of your two worlds its own colour, music and movement grammar; then the crossing between them is the story.
- Invent a prologue when your audience cannot read the backstory.
- Turn a famous problem (how to stage "exit, pursued by a bear") into your signature effect.

### Beyond the stage
Live cinema relay 2014 and DVD/streaming [documented for cinema]. ROH digital programme [documented].

## Woolf Works / Woolf Works (2015)
`id: woolf-works-2015` · threads: T18, T10, T22 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 11 May 2015, Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, London, The Royal Ballet; run to 26 May [documented, Studio Wayne McGregor; British Theatre Guide]. "Concept, direction and choreography" Wayne McGregor. Music Max Richter (commissioned; orchestra and electronics, with recordings) [documented, RBO]. Dramaturg Uzma Hameed [documented]. Design: Ciguë (Act I), We Not I (Act II) and McGregor (Act III); costumes Moritz Junge; lighting Lucy Carter; film Ravi Deepres; make-up Kabuki; sound Chris Ekers [documented, ABT archive]. Three acts: "I now, I then" (34 minutes), "Becomings" (35), "Tuesday" (25); about 95 minutes of performance [documented, ABT synopsis]. It was McGregor's first full-length ballet for the Royal Ballet [documented, RBO]. The version seen today: the original production, revived by the Royal Ballet (most recently January to February 2026) and staged by American Ballet Theatre (North American premiere 11 April 2024, Costa Mesa; New York 25 June 2024 with Alessandra Ferri) [documented].

### In one line
Three acts drawn from three Virginia Woolf novels (*Mrs Dalloway*, *Orlando*, *The Waves*) and her life, ending with her walk into the river.

### How it came to be
McGregor, the Royal Ballet's resident choreographer since 2006 and its first from contemporary dance, had made short abstract works such as `chroma-2006` [documented, RBO]. For his first full evening he chose Woolf. He began by working "closely with dramaturg Uzma Hameed" on her writings [documented, Studio Wayne McGregor]. He said the piece is "not a literal description of Woolf's writing" but a "collage structure" in "an unfolding stream of consciousness" [documented]. He brought back Alessandra Ferri, the Italian ballerina who had been Kenneth MacMillan's muse at the Royal Ballet in the 1980s and had retired from ballet in 2007 [documented that she returned to the ROH stage; MacMillan link documented, BTG; retirement date probable]. She was 52 [documented, Guardian; BTG]. Richter had worked with McGregor before, on *Infra* (2008) [documented, RBO]. The programme booklet was, one critic wrote, "a work of scholarship" with a pull-out chronology of Woolf's life [documented, Parry, DanceTabs 2015].

### Roots and reference points
- **Virginia Woolf**: *Mrs Dalloway* (1925), *Orlando* (1928), *The Waves* (1931), plus letters, diaries and essays [documented].
- **Woolf's own voice**: the only known recording of her, the BBC broadcast "On Craftsmanship" (1937), opens Act I [documented, RBO; BTG 2026].
- **Her suicide note** to her husband Leonard (March 1941), read in voice-over by the actress Gillian Anderson, opens Act III [documented, DanceTabs; BTG].
- **Vita Sackville-West**, the model for Orlando [documented as reading, BTG 2026].
- **MacMillan's dramatic ballet**, through Ferri; one critic saw her slipping from her partner like "Giselle" [documented, Parry].
- **Minimalism in music**: critics heard Philip Glass and Arvo Pärt in Richter [documented, Parry; BTG].
- **Merce Cunningham**: one critic thought of Cunningham in the busy, independent groups of Act II [documented, BTG 2026].

### The world it built
Act I, "I now, I then": the society hostess Clarissa Dalloway prepares a party on one day in London; the shell-shocked veteran Septimus Smith walks towards a psychiatrist; they never meet in the novel, but the ballet makes them stalk each other "as if haunted by a shared sense of what might have been" (Guardian) [documented]. Ferri dances Woolf and the older Clarissa at once; roles "slip and bleed into each other" [documented, BTG]. The movement here is lyrical and calm, "gone are his sci-fi hyperextensions" [documented, BTG]. Act II, "Becomings": *Orlando*'s hero lives through centuries and changes sex; twelve dancers in "golden Elizabethan doublet and hose" and "tiny black tutus" race through beams of light to a fast score with tango rhythms [documented, BTG 2015 and 2026]. This is McGregor's hyper-flexible style at full speed [documented]. Act III, "Tuesday" (the first word of Woolf's last letter): after the letter is read, Ferri and Federico Bonelli dance an elegiac duet before a slow film of the sea; a soprano voice laments; six children rise with skipping ropes, the six voices of *The Waves*; the dancers become the waves that carry her away [documented, Parry; RBO]. The rule: each act has its own design team, its own movement grammar and its own sound [documented, McGregor].

### Design and art
Act I (Ciguë, a Paris architecture practice): three large wooden frames, free-floating portals on which London's cityscape is projected and from which dancers watch; Woolf's manuscript "scrolls in columns across the stage" [documented, BTG; Guardian]. Act II (We Not I with Carter): "a sulphurous black space illumined by beams of light" and lasers; dancers pass "through planes and bars of coloured light" [documented, Parry]. A later critic read clouds like Constable's racing by [documented as reading, BTG 2026]. Act III (McGregor): a huge film of a grey, heaving sea fills the back; the stage becomes the river [documented]. Junge's costumes move from Edwardian dress (Act I) to gold doublets and black tutus (Act II) to grey, flowing clothes (Act III) [documented in part; Act III colour probable]. The idea: Woolf's "granite and rainbow," body and essence [documented, Studio Wayne McGregor, quoting Woolf's phrase].

### Premiere and reception
First cast: Alessandra Ferri, Federico Bonelli, Edward Watson (Septimus), Tristan Dyer, Beatriz Stix-Brunell, Francesca Hayward, Gary Avis (Richard Dalloway), Sarah Lamb, Natalia Osipova, Melissa Hamilton, Akane Takada, Steven McRae, Paul Kay, Eric Underwood (the Virgin Queen in "Becomings"), Matthew Ball [documented]. The audience gave a standing ovation [documented, Guardian 17 May 2015]. Judith Mackrell (*Guardian*, five stars): "exhilarating and ravishingly expressive"; McGregor "choreographing in a richer, freer space" [documented]. Jann Parry (DanceTabs) called it "a chimera," made of "disparate elements," and found "Becomings goes on too long" [documented]. Awards: Olivier Award for Best New Dance Production; Critics' Circle National Dance Award for classical choreography; Ferri won the Olivier for Outstanding Achievement in Dance and the Critics' Circle award for Best Female Dancer [documented, RBO]. Parry noted it was a "multi-media framework that only a subsidised theatre could afford" [documented]; no budget found.

### Versions and lineage
Royal Ballet 2015, revived 2017 and later, including 2026 with Osipova as Clarissa [documented for 2026]; ABT 2024 [documented]. Staged by Amanda Eyles, Mikaela Polley and Antoine Vereecken [documented, ABT]. McGregor and Richter went on to *MaddAddam* (2022), after Margaret Atwood [documented, RBO]. In the register lineage it sits with literary adaptations that drop plot for interior life (T18).

### Readings and disputes
- **Literary ballet without plot.** McGregor: not a translation but a collage "in the spirit of Woolf's writing" [documented]. Parry: will it encourage "baffled spectators to read the novels"? "anyone's guess" [documented]. Both accept that the ballet does not tell the books' stories.
- **A new McGregor or the same one.** Vera Liber (BTG, 2015): "a new McGregor," lyrical and calm in Act I [documented]. In 2026 she still found Act II "McGregor's most recognisable jerky, hyper-flexible style" [documented]. The work contains both.
- **Mental illness on stage.** The RBO text says Act I draws on Woolf's "own mental illness as subject matter" [documented]. A reader may ask whether staging her suicide as beautiful waves aestheticises it; no critic found in this pass makes the case at length [argued].
- **Gender in "Becomings."** Orlando's change of sex is the act's subject; a man (Underwood) dances Elizabeth I [documented]: the T22 tag rests on this act.

### Facts worth telling
- Woolf's real voice opens the ballet [documented].
- Gillian Anderson reads Woolf's suicide note [documented].
- Act III's title, "Tuesday," is the first word of that note [documented, BTG].
- Ferri came back to the Royal Opera House stage at 52 [documented].
- Each act has a different designer [documented].
- Six Royal Ballet School children appear in the last act [documented, Parry].

### Set it beside
- `onegin-1965` and `manon-1974`: literary ballets that tell the plot; McGregor tells the mind.
- `giselle-1841`: a woman dances to death in a white act; Ferri's last duet was read as a Giselle [documented, Parry].
- `chroma-2006`: McGregor's abstract style, here placed inside a literary frame.
- `flight-pattern-2017`: two years later on the same stage, a massed grey crowd and a mourning woman.

### What a maker can take from it
- Adapt a writer's method (stream of consciousness), not only her stories.
- Give each part of a triptych its own designer, with one through-line (here, Woolf herself).
- A real archival voice (a recording) can anchor a fictional world.

### Beyond the stage
Cinema relays and DVD (2017) [probable]. Richter's score released as *Three Worlds: Music from Woolf Works* (Deutsche Grammophon, 2017) [probable].

## The Seasons' Canon / The Seasons' Canon (2016)
`id: the-seasons-canon-2016` (new) · threads: T10, T16 (inverted), T12 · confidence of the header: documented (premiere day contested)

**Header.** World premiere 24 September 2016, Palais Garnier, Paris Opéra Ballet, at the season's opening gala (with the traditional *Défilé* and Forsythe's *Blake Works I*) [documented, Opéra de Paris gala page; NYT 25 September 2016; Wikipedia]; first repertory performance 26 September 2016, in the bill Sehgal / Peck / Pite / Forsythe (to 9 October) [documented, Opéra de Paris; PNB gives 26 September as "premiere"]. Choreographer Crystal Pite. Music Max Richter, *Recomposed by Max Richter: Vivaldi – The Four Seasons* (2012), for string orchestra, harp and harpsichord [documented, PNB]. No scenario. Set Jay Gower Taylor; "reflected light backdrop concept" Taylor with Tom Visser; costumes Nancy Bryant; lighting Tom Visser [documented, Opéra de Paris]. One act, about 31 to 35 minutes; 54 dancers [documented, PNB; ResMusica]. The version seen today: Paris Opéra's (revived 2018 and later), Pacific Northwest Ballet (4 November 2022; co-production with Boston Ballet, which danced it in October 2024) [documented, PNB; Wikipedia].

### In one line
Fifty-four bare-chested dancers in khaki trousers move as one living mass through Vivaldi's seasons, remade by Richter, like a landscape breathing.

### How it came to be
Pite, a Canadian (born 1970, probable), danced in Ballet British Columbia and then in William Forsythe's Ballett Frankfurt, which hired her in 1995 [documented, Opéra de Paris]. In 2002 she founded her company Kidd Pivot in Vancouver [documented, ROH press release 2022]. She was known for large ensembles that move like swarms, such as *Emergence* (2009) [documented that *Emergence* exists and is hers, PNB; swarm idea probable]. Benjamin Millepied, director of dance at the Paris Opéra, invited her to make a work for the gala that opens the season. He resigned months before the premiere and Aurélie Dupont succeeded him [documented, Wikipedia]. Pite was "largely unknown in France" [documented]. She had four weeks, which she called "a sprint" [documented]. She said the Paris dancers have "a very different skill set and vocabulary" and that their classical "architecture ... is so ecstatic and beautiful" [documented, quoted by Wikipedia]. She quoted Annie Dillard on nature's "grace tangled in a rapture with violence" and called the work "a gesture, an offering" of thanks to the natural world [documented, PNB programme note]. The piece won her the 2017 Benois de la Danse for choreography [documented, Opéra de Paris news, 2017].

### Roots and reference points
- **Vivaldi's *Four Seasons*** (published 1725) and Richter's 2012 recomposition, which keeps fragments and loops them [documented, PNB].
- **Annie Dillard**, the American nature writer (*Pilgrim at Tinker Creek*, 1974), in the programme [documented quote; book probable].
- **Geology**: the choreography was "inspired by the movements of tectonic plates" [documented, Wikipedia citing an interview].
- **Forsythe**, her former director, on the same gala programme [documented].
- **Béjart and Kylián**: Laura Cappelle (*Financial Times*) called the mass effects "practically a throwback to Maurice Béjart" and saw Jiří Kylián's early influence in the short duets [documented, quoted by Wikipedia].
- **The musical canon**: a canon is a melody imitated by voice after voice; Pite makes "human canons, where one dancer's gesture sets off the next" (ResMusica 2018) [documented].

### The world it built
The structure follows Richter's seasons: spring, summer, autumn, winter, with sections that blend. The rule is collective: one body made of 54. In the canons, an arm movement passes along a line of dancers "like a single breath" [documented, ResMusica 2018]. Solos and duets rise out of the mass and are "swallowed up" by "the ectoplasmic collective creature" when they try to escape (Jade Larine, *Fjord Review*) [documented]. Étoiles have featured parts but stay in the group; Pite said "it was important to me that they were women into the community as well" [documented]. First cast featured Marie-Agnès Gillot ("a regal jellyfish queen," Larine), Ludmila Pagliero, Alice Renavand, François Alu (a "tempestuous solo" in Summer) and Alessio Carbone [documented]. Movement: grounded, in socks, with fluid spines suggesting "an underwater world" [documented, PNB; Fjord Review]. Delphine Goater (ResMusica) saw "a telluric and primitive vision of a tribe" [documented].

### Design and art
Nancy Bryant's costumes: khaki-green trousers; men bare-chested, women in sheer mesh tops; every dancer's throat painted blue-green, like a strange collar [documented, Wikipedia; ResMusica: "une étrange minerve verte"]. Specialist make-up artists apply it; PNB used five per show [documented, PNB]. The trousers have an inner gaiter, so each pair takes "about a minute and a half" to put on; PNB built 65 pairs [documented]. Jay Gower Taylor's set is a reflective gold backdrop, 30 feet tall and 60 feet long at PNB, which catches Visser's light [documented, PNB]. The light makes "fantastic images" on it, "a cross between northern lights and magnetic fields" (ResMusica 2016) [documented]. Everyone looks the same: a deliberate removal of rank, in the Opéra, the most ranked company in ballet [argued]. Taylor is Pite's husband and her long-time designer [documented, BTG 2017].

### Premiere and reception
Gala audience at Garnier, then a standing ovation at the first repertory night [documented, ResMusica: "applaudi debout"]. Goater (ResMusica, 28 September 2016) called Pite's entry into the "pantheon" of choreographers who can move large companies "fracassante" (shattering) [documented]. Cappelle (FT) gave five stars [documented]. Larine (*Fjord Review*) said Pite "steals the show" from Peck and Forsythe and restored "the unity" of the Paris corps [documented]. Roslyn Sulcas (*NYT*): "massed blocks of movement ... to often thrilling effect," but "the piece feels all about effect" [documented, quoted by Wikipedia]. Caroline Dehouck (ResMusica, 2018) found the sequencing in tableaux broke its breath [documented].

### Versions and lineage
Paris 2016, revived 2018 (with Thierrée, Shechter and Pérez) and later [documented]. PNB 2022 (the second company in the world) and Boston Ballet 2024 [documented]. Staged by Eric Beauchesne and Anna Herrmann [documented, PNB]. Its successor on the Pite line is `flight-pattern-2017`, made six months later in London.

### Readings and disputes
- **Ode to nature.** Pite: an "offering," a way of "giving thanks" [documented]. Goater and Larine read a primal tribe or sea creature [documented].
- **Effect over meaning.** Sulcas: "all about effect" [documented]. Supporters answer that the effect is the meaning: the mass is the subject [argued].
- **A cure for Paris.** Larine argues that the work healed a corps "accused" of losing its unity under Millepied [documented]. Opéra politics are part of its reception [argued].
- **Dates.** 24 September (gala) or 26 September (first repertory night) [documented both]. The Opéra's 2017 Benois news item even dates its creation "September 2017," a slip [documented].
- **Ritual without sacrifice.** Critics read a ritual tribe (T16), but no chosen one is killed; the register should treat it as T16 inverted, a rite of community [argued].

### Facts worth telling
- Pite made it in four weeks [documented].
- Every dancer's throat is painted green [documented].
- The trousers take a minute and a half to put on [documented, PNB].
- The commissioner left before the premiere [documented].
- The gold backdrop at PNB is 60 feet long [documented].
- PNB was only the second company to dance it, six years later [documented].

### Set it beside
- `rite-of-spring-1913`: seasons and a tribe again; Nijinsky's ends in a death, Pite's in a cycle.
- `les-noces-1923`: Nijinska's massed peasants moving as one block, an ancestor of Pite's collective [argued].
- `rites-page-1997`: the elements as cycle, from Indigenous cosmology rather than a nature writer.
- `in-the-middle-1987`: Forsythe on the same stage, 29 years earlier; Pite was his dancer.

### What a maker can take from it
- Make the crowd the protagonist, and let individuals emerge and dissolve.
- Use one costume and one mark (the green throat) for everyone to show a collective.
- A canon (one idea passed along a line) is a structure any medium can use.

### Beyond the stage
Richter's *Recomposed* album (Deutsche Grammophon, 2012) is widely used in film and advertising [probable]. Paris Opéra films and photos (Julien Benhamou) circulate under copyright.

- Opéra national de Paris, 2016 gala and Sehgal / Peck / Pite / Forsythe pages: http://www.operadeparis.fr/en/season-16-17/ballet/gala and https://www.operadeparis.fr/en/season-16-17/ballet/seghal-peck-pite-forsythe [seen].
- Opéra national de Paris, "The 2017 Benois de la Danse": https://www.operadeparis.fr/en/news/the-2017-benois-de-la-danse [seen].

## Flight Pattern / Flight Pattern (2017)
`id: flight-pattern-2017` · threads: T08, T10, T23 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 16 March 2017, Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, London, The Royal Ballet, in a triple bill with David Dawson's *The Human Seasons* and Christopher Wheeldon's *After the Rain*; run 16 to 24 March [documented, ROH Collections; BTG 2017; DanceTabs]. Choreographer Crystal Pite. Music Henryk Górecki, Symphony No. 3, "Symphony of Sorrowful Songs" (1976), first movement only, with soprano (Jennifer Davis in 2017) [documented, Wikipedia; BTG]. No scenario. Set Jay Gower Taylor; costumes Nancy Bryant; lighting Tom Visser; staging Deirdre Chapman [documented, ROH Collections]. One act, about 30 minutes; 36 dancers [documented, Voices of British Ballet; BTG]. The version seen today: since 2022, the first part of Pite's two-act *Light of Passage* (premiere 18 October 2022), set to the whole symphony [documented, ROH press release 7 September 2022].

### In one line
Thirty-six people in grey coats move as one exhausted crowd towards a door of light, while one woman, given coat after coat like dead children, cannot go on.

### How it came to be
In 2016 the Royal Ballet commissioned Pite; it was the first time in 18 years that a woman had been asked to make a work for its main stage [documented, Guardian 17 March 2017; Voices of British Ballet; Operaen interview]. The world was "in the thick of the Syrian refugee crisis," Pite recalled [documented, Operaen.no]. Górecki's Third came to mind, but she resisted: "It's too famous. It's too well-known. I can't" [documented]. She went through piles of orchestral music "and came right back to Górecki" [documented]. She called it, she believed, "the best-selling contemporary classical symphony CD" ever [documented as her claim]. She used only the first movement. The title means both the theme, a pattern of flight, and the method: a crowd "moving in specific, repeated patterns," with individuals breaking away and returning; it also "reflects the fact that these humanitarian crises happen again and again" [documented, Pite]. Pite says she makes a new piece only every 18 months and labours over it [documented]. The cast was drawn largely from the youngest dancers of the company [documented, BTG].

### Roots and reference points
- **Górecki's Symphony No. 3** (1976): its first movement sets a 15th-century Polish lament of Mary for her son [documented, DanceTabs]. (BTG links the symphony to words written on a Gestapo cell wall; that text belongs to the second movement [documented that the BTG says so; movement attribution probable].)
- **The 2015 to 2016 refugee crisis in Europe** [documented].
- **Earlier Royal Ballet works on displacement**: Hofesh Shechter's *Untouchable* (2015) and McGregor's *Multiverse* (2016), danced by many of the same dancers [documented, DanceTabs].
- **The pietà**: McNally's figure is "the Madonna lamenting for her son" (BTG) [documented as reading].
- **Bird flocks**: "like albatrosses, wings wide ... flock like starlings" (BTG) [documented as reading].
- **Pite's own ensemble works**, *Emergence* (2009) and `the-seasons-canon-2016` [documented].

### The world it built
Part one: the crowd. Thirty-six dancers in dark coats "sway, inch, ripple, and later skate" across the stage [documented, BTG]. The coats' skirts "amplify their surges of movement" [documented, DanceTabs]. Lines and queues make tension: everyone waits [documented, Wikipedia]. Part two: the duet. When the strings fall back and the soprano begins, Kristen McNally holds a coat across her arms "as though it were the corpse of a child"; Marcelino Sambé tries to console her in "anguished lifts"; more coats are piled on her arms [documented, DanceTabs]. Part three: the community gathers in counterpoint as the strings drop out one by one; snow falls; the migrants line up and pass through a doorway in the closing walls, flinging up their arms [documented, DanceTabs]. Two are left behind: McNally rocking, Sambé tearing at himself in silent howls [documented]. The music: Górecki's slow canon for strings builds for minutes from the double basses up; Pite's crowd moves in the same layered canon [documented that the movement "reflects the rhythmical counterpoint"; argued as to strict parallel].

### Design and art
Nancy Bryant's costumes are identical, in grey; coats come off to show grey vests and loose trousers [documented, Wikipedia]. Jay Gower Taylor's set is dark panels that open and close and change the shape of the stage, ending as two walls with "a chink of light in heavy doors the full height of the stage" [documented, Wikipedia; BTG]. Tom Visser keeps the light dark until the end, when a column of light shines between the walls [documented]. Snow falls at the close [documented]. The coat is the work's one object, used as clothing, bedding and a dead child [documented]. Critics read the final doorway two ways: freedom, or a gas chamber disguised as a shower [documented, DanceTabs; BTG]. The palette, "shades of Van Gogh grey" (BTG), is the opposite of ballet's display [documented as reading].

### Premiere and reception
Original duet: Kristen McNally and Marcelino Sambé [documented]. Judith Mackrell (*Guardian*): "brave and beautiful," a "gripping vision of the refugee crisis" [documented, headline and standfirst]. DanceTabs gave it five stars and called it the most "emotionally involving" of three Royal Ballet works on displacement [documented]. Vera Liber (BTG) asked whether Pite risks "being accused of exploitation, poverty porn" and "Should destitution be made shabby-chic beautiful ... to touch hearts?" before calling McNally "magnificent" [documented]. Awards: Olivier Award for Best New Dance Production, 2018; National Dance Award nominations for Pite and McNally [documented, Wikipedia]. Sponsors: the Taylor family, Richard and Delia Baker, Sue Butcher (2017); the J. P. Jacobs Charitable Trust (2019 revival) [documented, ROH Collections].

### Versions and lineage
Royal Ballet 2017; revived 2019 [documented]. Expanded into *Light of Passage* (18 October to 3 November 2022): part one *Flight Pattern*; part two *Covenant*, about children; part three *Passage*, with a couple from Sadler's Wells's non-professional Company of Elders, on the passage to death; conductor Zoi Tsokanou [documented, ROH press release; Voices of British Ballet]. The *New York Times* (21 October 2022) called *Light of Passage* "equally crowd-pleasing and equally simplistic" [documented]. The Norwegian National Ballet presented a Pite programme in Oslo in 2023, introduced with an interview about *Flight Pattern* and *Light of Passage*; which works it contained was not checked [documented for the interview].

### Readings and disputes
- **Empathy or aestheticised suffering.** Mackrell and DanceTabs read a work of witness that makes the crisis felt [documented]. Liber (BTG) raises the "poverty porn" question; the NYT (2022) calls the later whole "simplistic" [documented]. Both sides grant the craft.
- **Specific or universal.** Pite keeps the subject "non-specific," so it "could belong to any age or country" (DanceTabs) [documented]. Critics then add specific readings: Soviet gulags, Nazi camps, Syria [documented]. Universality invites each viewer's own history.
- **"First woman in 18 years."** Repeated by the Guardian, Voices of British Ballet and the NYT [documented]. Who the last woman was (around 1999) was not found in this pass; the claim is about the main stage only, since women made works for the smaller Linbury theatre [probable].
- **Whose story?** No refugee artists are credited on the team [documented by credits]. Compare Rites (`rites-page-1997`), where the depicted culture co-authored the work [argued].

### Facts worth telling
- It uses only one movement of the symphony; the other two were added five years later [documented].
- The coats become the dead children [documented].
- It was the first main-stage work by a woman at the Royal Ballet in 18 years [documented].
- The set designer, Jay Gower Taylor, is Pite's husband [documented, BTG].
- Pite nearly rejected the music for being too famous [documented].
- Non-professional older dancers appear in its 2022 expansion [documented].

### Set it beside
- `green-table-1932`: Jooss's dance of war and death, the earlier great European political ballet.
- `giselle-khan-2016`: a contemporary migrant world remade from a classic, a year earlier in London.
- `the-seasons-canon-2016`: the same team's collective, as nature rather than history.
- `in-the-upper-room-1986`: another crowd that runs to exhaustion, there for joy, here for survival.

### What a maker can take from it
- One object used three ways (coat, bed, child) can carry a whole narrative.
- Let your title name both your subject and your structure.
- When your subject is real suffering, decide who is on the team and how you will answer the question of exploitation.

### Beyond the stage
Górecki's Symphony No. 3 became a best-selling recording in 1992 (Dawn Upshaw, London Sinfonietta, Nonesuch) [probable]. Cinema relay of *Light of Passage* [probable].

Paired with Pina Bausch's *The Rite of Spring* (1975; sets and costumes Rolf Borzik), restaged for a specially formed ensemble of dancers from African countries by Josephine Ann Endicott and Jorge Puerta Armenta [documented]. The version seen today: the same double bill, still touring (Sadler's Wells 2022 and 2024; Heerlen 2024; Park Avenue Armory, New York) [documented].

### In one line
Two women in their late seventies, one from Senegal and one from France, share a day of memory, water and grief, before a new ensemble from across Africa dances Bausch's earth-covered sacrifice.

### How it came to be
The Pina Bausch Foundation, run by Bausch's son Salomon Bausch, "transmits" her works to other ensembles. For the *Rite* it wanted, for the first time, a company formed only for the piece [documented, Foundation]. It turned to the École des Sables at Toubab Dialaw, Senegal, founded by Germaine Acogny, called "the mother of contemporary African dance" [documented, Foundation; Dance Magazine 2020]. Acogny had first seen Bausch's *Rite* danced by the Paris Opéra Ballet and felt "it was an African rite" [documented, Dance Magazine; GRIOT]. Sadler's Wells recalled that a trip by Bausch to Senegal was planned in 2008 but her health failed; she died in 2009 [documented, quoted in SFCV]. Acogny and Malou Airaudo, a founding member of Tanztheater Wuppertal in 1973 who had danced the Chosen One in Bausch's *Rite*, made the duet between November 2019 (at the École des Sables) and February 2020 (at the Folkwang University in Essen, where Airaudo taught) [documented, Dance Magazine 2022; Théâtre de la Ville; SFCV]. The premiere in Dakar was cancelled days before it was due in March 2020, when Covid closed Senegal [documented]. The dancers' last run-through, on the beach at Toubab Dialaw at sunset, was filmed as *Dancing at Dusk* by Florian Heinzen-Ziob [documented]. Rehearsals resumed in August 2021; the premiere came in Madrid, "in front of a sold-out house" [documented, Foundation].

### Roots and reference points
- **Bausch's *Rite of Spring* (1975)** (`sacre-bausch-1975`): a stage covered in peat, women in slips, men bare-chested, a red dress for the Chosen One, who dances herself to death [documented, GRIOT; Hyperallergic].
- **Nijinsky's *Rite* (1913)** (`rite-of-spring-1913`) and Stravinsky's score [documented].
- **Acogny's technique**, a modern African dance she codified at Mudra Afrique in Dakar (1977 to 1982, with Maurice Béjart) and at the École des Sables (founded 1998 with Helmut Vogt) [probable].
- **Acogny's earlier Rite**: she danced the Chosen One in Olivier Dubois's solo *Mon élue noire* and won a Bessie Award for it (Dance Magazine dates the work "2017"; other sources date its creation 2014) [documented award; date contested].
- **Comparative ritual**: Acogny: "In Africa, as well as in European cultures such as ancient Greece, it was quite usual to sacrifice young, female virgins" [documented, Dance Magazine 2020]. This is her claim, and it is a contested generalisation [argued].
- **Nature**: Acogny likes wood, Airaudo the beach, "so we bring onto the stage the water, the wood" [documented, Dance Magazine 2022].

### The world it built
The duet: two silhouettes against an orange light; they walk apart, then embrace, "cradle each other in a confusion of limbs," turn their backs, sit on stools [documented, Nouchi, BTG 2024]. They wash their feet; Acogny dips a cloth in water and glides across the stage "as if she were taking strides across an ice-skating rink" [documented, Matilda Marseillaise 2022]. They hold a pole "as if rowing" [documented, BTG]. Images of children appear without explanation [documented, Matilda Marseillaise; form of the images not found]. The light moves from sunrise to a "dark blueish-purple" night; Airaudo stamps once and it ends [documented]. It is danced on a hard floor, unlike the soil of the *Rite*: Acogny said "the story we developed has nothing to do with *The Rite of Spring*" [documented, Dance Magazine 2022]. Music: string quartet and keyboard, "a mélange of classical and African sounds" [documented, BTG]. After the interval the stage is covered in soil in full view, "an undeclared act of physical theatre" [documented, BTG]. Then the *Rite*: about 30 to 38 dancers; men bare-chested in black trousers, women in beige slips; the Chosen One in red [documented].

### Design and art
For the duet, Kepekli's light evokes "the sunrise, the sky, the environment" [documented, Acogny]. Leidner's costumes are simple [probable; details not found]. Objects: water, cloth, a wooden pole, stools [documented]. The two designs of the evening form one argument: a hard, bare floor for two old women's memory; earth for a young crowd's sacrifice [argued]. In the *Rite*, Borzik's 1975 design is kept: earth, slips, a red dress [documented]. Critics noted "the beige dresses on the women's black skin" [documented, Matilda Marseillaise]. In *Dancing at Dusk*, sand replaced peat because the soil order had not arrived; "dresses flowing in the wind, sand sticking to their skin" [documented, Hyperallergic; Dance Magazine].

### Premiere and reception
Madrid 23 to 26 September 2021, then Copenhagen (Østre Gasværk, 29 September to 3 October 2021), St. Pölten and Luxembourg; Adelaide Festival opening, March 2022 [documented]. At Sadler's Wells in June 2022 Covid cases cut the *Rite* to 24 dancers and the duet "had to be cancelled" [documented, Guardian 12 June 2022]. Reviews of the duet are gentle: "a gentle love letter to friendship between two women" (Rachel Nouchi, BTG, November 2024) [documented]; "a moving piece detailing a day in the life of two women," though "we never find out why" they grieve (Matilda Marseillaise, March 2022) [documented]; Nathalie Broschat (tanzweb, 2024) wrote of "utmost respect and awe for their age, knowledge and bodies" [documented]. Funding: German Federal Cultural Foundation, the North Rhine-Westphalia culture ministry, the Goethe-Institut's International Coproduction Fund, and later Van Cleef & Arpels's Dance Reflections [documented].

### Versions and lineage
*Dancing at Dusk* film (2020, streamed by Sadler's Wells) [documented]. Live tour from 2021 [documented]. The ensemble changes: "38 dancers from 14 African countries" (2020), "37 dancers ... from 13" (Foundation, 2021), 34 from 13 (Sadler's Wells, 2024) [documented: all three; the counts differ because the ensemble changed]. In the register's Rite family: Nijinsky 1913, Béjart 1959, Bausch 1975, Page 1997, the African-cast Bausch 2020/2021.

### Readings and disputes
- **Transmission or translation?** The Foundation stresses fidelity: the steps were learned "verbatim" from Bausch's former dancers [documented, Dance Magazine 2020]. Acogny stresses what the dancers bring, "a very special energy" [documented]. The same production is both a copy and a new reading.
- **Is the Rite "African"?** Acogny: "When I first heard the Stravinsky music, I felt it was an African rite" and "it is something universal" [documented]. Hyperallergic's Jay Chen noted that the original *Rite* "would probably seem egregiously racist without adaptation" [documented]. Acogny claims the work; critics question its primitivism. Both are about who has the right to the ritual.
- **Gender in the Rite.** Nouchi (2024) found it "voyeuristic" and "uncomfortable" to watch men manhandle the women [documented]. The duet beforehand gives the evening two old women as its other image of womanhood [argued].
- **Dates and counts.** Register: "2021, Dakar/Europe, probable"; the premiere was Madrid, 23 September 2021 [documented]. The dancers' ages are given as 79 and 75 (SFCV, 2024) and as 78 and 80 (BTG, 2024) [contested].

### Facts worth telling
- The Dakar premiere was cancelled by Covid days before it opened [documented].
- The last rehearsal was danced on a beach and became a film [documented].
- Sand replaced peat because the soil had not arrived [documented].
- Airaudo danced the Chosen One with Bausch [documented, Théâtre de la Ville].
- The soil is laid in the interval in front of the audience [documented].
- In June 2022, Covid cancelled the duet at Sadler's Wells [documented].

### Set it beside
- `rites-page-1997`: the Rite re-read through Aboriginal cosmology, with no chosen one; here Bausch's steps are kept exactly.
- `sacre-bejart-1959`: Béjart, who co-founded Mudra Afrique with Acogny, made his own Rite as a mass rite of sex [probable link].
- `giselle-masilo-2017`: another African woman artist remaking a canonical work, rewriting rather than keeping the steps.
- `flight-pattern-2017`: a crowd in grey; here the crowd is the ensemble, and the duet is the answer.

### What a maker can take from it
- Pair a monumental work with a small, personal one: each changes how the other is seen.
- A changed cast can be a reading, even when every step is kept.
- When plans collapse, record the moment: the beach rehearsal became the work's most famous image.

### Beyond the stage
*Dancing at Dusk – A moment with Pina Bausch's The Rite of Spring* (dir. Florian Heinzen-Ziob, polyphem Filmproduktion, 2020) [documented]. The Foundation's website documents the transmission [documented].

## Like Water for Chocolate / Like Water for Chocolate (2022)
`id: like-water-for-chocolate-2022` · threads: T18, T24, T01 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 2 June 2022, Royal Opera House, Covent Garden, London, The Royal Ballet; co-production with American Ballet Theatre (North American premiere 29 March 2023, Segerstrom Center for the Arts, Costa Mesa; New York 22 June 2023, Metropolitan Opera House) [documented, ROH press release 10 May 2022; ABT]. Choreographer Christopher Wheeldon. Scenario Wheeldon and Joby Talbot, "inspired by the book by Laura Esquivel" (*Como agua para chocolate*, 1989) [documented, ROH cast sheet]. Music Joby Talbot; orchestrations Ben Foskett; music consultant and premiere conductor Alondra de la Parra; solo guitar Tomás Barreiro; guest singer Siân Griffiths [documented]. Designer Bob Crowley; lighting Natasha Katz; video Luke Halls [documented]. Three acts (55, 35, 25 minutes) [documented, cast sheet]. The version seen today: the original co-production, danced by both companies; the Royal Ballet announced its first revival after 2022 [documented].

### In one line
On a Mexican ranch, a youngest daughter forbidden by her mother to marry pours her love into her cooking, and the food makes everyone who eats it weep, burn or run away.

### How it came to be
Wheeldon read Esquivel's novel and was struck by "its cast of strong female characters" and by Mexico, a country he had come to know through his friendship with the Mexican conductor Alondra de la Parra [documented, RBO "Essentials"]. He "worked closely with" Esquivel to reshape the story [documented]. Esquivel said it was "a great pleasure to have them visit Mexico for extensive research and to develop the scenario together" [documented, quoted by Wheeldon]. De la Parra and her sister Camilla helped Crowley research 20th-century Mexican culture for the designs [documented, RBO]. The premiere season ran 2 to 17 June 2022, with three casts [documented, press release]. It was the third full-length ballet by the team of *Alice* and *The Winter's Tale* [documented]. It was filmed live on 9 June 2022 for cinemas, directed for the screen by Ross MacGibbon [documented, cast sheet]. The roles of Tita, Pedro, Mama Elena and Gertrudis were each "generously supported" by named donors, as the cast sheet shows [documented].

### Roots and reference points
- **Laura Esquivel, *Como agua para chocolate* (1989)**: twelve chapters, one for each month, each opening with a recipe; set during the Mexican Revolution (1910 to 1920); the youngest daughter's duty to care for her mother [documented, theartsdesk; synopsis].
- **The film** of 1992, directed by Alfonso Arau from Esquivel's screenplay [probable].
- **Magical realism**, as in Gabriel García Márquez [probable as a genre link].
- **Mexican architecture**: Crowley's designs were "inspired by Mexican architect Luis Barragán," his coloured walls and light [documented, Guardian 2022].
- **Mexican dance rhythms**: Talbot drew on danzón and petenera, among others [documented, RBO].
- **MacMillan's bedroom duets**: Christiansen found the last pas de deux "relies heavily on MacMillan" [documented, *Spectator*].
- **Balanchine's warning**: "there are no mothers-in-law in ballet," quoted by Jenny Gilbert against the project [documented, theartsdesk].

### The world it built
Tita lives on the family ranch. Pedro swears to love her forever; Mama Elena forbids it because the youngest daughter must care for her mother until she dies [documented, synopsis]. Pedro marries Tita's sister Rosaura to stay near Tita [probable, novel]. Tita's emotions enter her food: at the wedding the guests weep; after quail in rose-petal sauce her sister Gertrudis is so inflamed that the revolutionary soldier Juan Alejandrez "carries Gertrudis away on horseback" [documented, Spectator quoting the synopsis]. Dr John Brown offers Tita another love; the cook Nacha and the ghost of Mama Elena haunt the house [documented, cast list]. The work spans about twenty years, a dozen characters, three acts and twelve scenes [documented, Christiansen]. The ending: Tita and Pedro united in an "apocalyptic final scene" [documented, Gilbert]. Movement rules: "earthier rhythms, freer arms and more sensual movements of the torso" to evoke Mexico [documented, RBO]. Music rules: each main character has an instrument: guitar for Pedro, ocarina for Tita, cedar flute for Nacha, chimes and wooden rattles for Mama Elena [documented, RBO]. Talbot and his Mexican advisers chose to "leave out the castanets and harmonic turns" that would feed stereotypes of Latin American music [documented, RBO].

### Design and art
Crowley and Katz "magic the stage into a sun-gilded landscape foregrounded by screens of Mexican lace" [documented, Gilbert]. Christiansen notes "hot colours, back projections and lacy drapes" [documented]. The Guardian saw "a sense of heat and isolation" from Barragán's architecture [documented]. In the aphrodisiac scene Gertrudis dances naked on the dining table while figures (rose petals or flames; "the costumes are unclear," Gilbert) stream from beneath it [documented]. Halls's video carries landscape and time [probable]. The design's ethical rule was research with Mexican advisers rather than postcard Mexico [documented intention, RBO]. Whether it succeeded is a reading [argued].

### Premiere and reception
First cast (2 June): Francesca Hayward (Tita), Marcelino Sambé (Pedro), Laura Morera (Mama Elena), Matthew Ball (Dr John Brown) [documented, press release]; filmed cast (9 June) adds Mayara Magri (Rosaura), Meaghan Grace Hinkis (Gertrudis), Christina Arestis (Nacha), Cesar Corrales (Juan Alejandrez), Gary Avis (Don Pasqual) [documented]. Gilbert gave the review the headline "confusing and ill-conceived," praising only the principals as "luminous" [documented, theartsdesk 7 June 2022]. Christiansen: "I suspect this was a rush job" and "more than ballet can handle" [documented, *Spectator* 9 June 2022]. The Guardian called it "delectable," with choreography in "a telepathic relationship" with the score [documented, 3 June 2022]. The *Telegraph*: "a sprawling, sensual feast"; *The Independent*: "swift and stylish" [documented, quoted by Wheeldon]. The NYT review headline: ballet is "spectacle" and succeeds best "when not meeting the demands of storytelling" [documented]. Audience: "squealed and shrieked its approval" (Christiansen) [documented]. Tickets £5 to £115 [documented, press release].

### Versions and lineage
Royal Ballet June 2022; ABT March 2023 (Cassandra Trenary, Herman Cornejo) and New York June 2023, 12 performances [documented]. Whether ABT's version was revised: not found in this pass. Royal Ballet revival announced after 2022 (year not checked) [documented]. In the register's lineage it joins the T24 "woman against family" wall with `raise-the-red-lantern-2001` and `shim-chung-1986`.

### Readings and disputes
- **Can ballet tell this story?** Gilbert and Christiansen argue no: too many relatives, too much plot, and food cannot be danced [documented]. The Guardian and *Telegraph* argue yes, through feeling and spectacle [documented]. The NYT takes a middle road: it works as spectacle, not as story [documented headline].
- **Who tells Mexico?** A British team adapting a Mexican novel: the company points to Esquivel's partnership, the de la Parra sisters and Barreiro [documented]. A critique from Mexican or Latinx critics was not found in this pass; this is a gap, not an absence.
- **Woman's liberation or melodrama?** Wheeldon calls the heroines "strong independent women who rule dominantly, love fiercely and overcome the phantoms of their past" [documented]. Christiansen saw Mama Elena forced into "every Cruella de Vil cliché" [documented].
- **Credit.** The register gave scenario to Wheeldon and Talbot; the cast sheet confirms it [documented]; Esquivel is "inspired by" credit, not co-author, though she says they "developed the scenario together" [documented: both].

### Facts worth telling
- Every main character has an instrument (Tita an ocarina) [documented].
- Castanets were deliberately left out [documented].
- The novel has a recipe in every chapter [documented, Gilbert].
- Gertrudis dances naked on the table [documented].
- A Mexican woman conductor led the premiere [documented].
- Donors sponsor individual roles [documented].

### Set it beside
- `raise-the-red-lantern-2001`: a woman trapped by a household's rules, told in Beijing through lanterns, where Mexico uses food.
- `le-parc-1994`: desire made visible by one image (the flying kiss) instead of by many scenes.
- `alice-wheeldon-2011`: the same team; the same risk of design overtaking dance.
- `estancia-1952`: an Argentine ranch ballet made from inside Latin America, set beside a British Mexico.

### What a maker can take from it
- Give each character an object or a sound (here an instrument): it lets an audience follow a crowded plot.
- Name the stereotypes you will refuse (castanets) before you begin.
- Count your plot: if a synopsis needs four pages, the world may be too full for the medium.

Cinema relay 2022 and the 1992 film of the novel [documented for the relay; film probable]. Esquivel's novel has been translated widely [probable].
