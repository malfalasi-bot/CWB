## Giselle, ou les Wilis / Giselle (1841)
`id: giselle-1841` · W file: W1 §5

### Design and art
W1 names Ciceri and Lormier but says little about the look. The 1841 livret asks for a "dark and gloomy forest on the banks of a pond", with the tombstone at the left and "the bluish gleam of the moon" giving "a cold and misty appearance" [documented: livret as translated by Pacific Northwest Ballet, PNB repertory page]. That blue came from gas light, which the Opéra could dim and colour with gauzes; Act II is a lighting design as much as a set. Ciceri's Act I village was reused from Adam's *La Fille du Danube* (1838), so the new money went into the night act [documented in W1]. Lormier dressed the Wilis in white Romantic skirts of layered tarlatan or muslin, calf length, with small wings at the back of the bodice; the wings were the sign that these are spirits [probable: Lormier designs, BnF-BMO, Gallica]. Myrtha carries a branch of myrtle, her emblem; in the fugue it "is shattered by the superior power of the cross" on Giselle's grave [documented: Irmgard Berry quoted by English National Ballet on Mary Skeaping's 1971 staging, 2024]. So the props carry the theology: pagan plant against Christian cross. Trapdoors and flying wires let Wilis rise and vanish; their failures delayed the premiere [documented in W1]. The ballet's best-known images are not stage photographs but lithographs of Grisi, which sold the look as a collectible [probable].

### Readings and disputes
- **How does Giselle die?** Three answers sit in the record. The 1841 livret has Giselle seize the sword but have it taken away; Marian Smith (in Alastair Macaulay, "Giselle: Ninety-Seven Questions, with Hundreds of Answers", 2020) says "the original libretto does say plainly that the sword is taken away from her" and the early stagings followed it [documented]. Gautier's own "Letter to Heine" (*La Presse*, 5 July 1841) says the point "had already found its mark" [documented as quoted by Smith, same]. Macaulay's 2002 essay "Giselle, and her problems" reads the 1841 scenario as saying the sword pierces her breast, as one of several causes [documented]. The Stepanov notation (1899–1903) has no stabbing (Doug Fullington, same Q&A) [documented]; Ratmansky hears "no suicide in the music" [documented]. Peter Wright's Royal Ballet staging makes it a suicide, to explain her burial outside consecrated ground [documented: Petipa Society]. Teach as **contested**: the record contradicts itself.
- **A ballet about class, or about women?** Sally Banes (*Dancing Women*, 1998) reads it as a marriage plot that fails, with the Wilis as female community and threat [probable]. Soviet stagings read it as class: Yuri Slonimsky describes making Hilarion the honest peasant and Albrecht the aristocratic villain, which the Petipa Society calls "illogical" because Albrecht is still forgiven [documented: Petipa Society page].
- **Myth: an unbroken Paris tradition.** The ballet left the Paris repertory in 1868 and returned only in 1924; the "French" *Giselle* came back from Russia [documented in W1].
- **Act II as anticlimax or as the point.** Macaulay argues that if the ballet is read as a tragedy of death, "the whole of Act Two will be an anti-climax" [documented: Macaulay 2002]; Smith reads both acts as one musical-dramatic design [probable: Smith 2000].

### Facts worth telling
1. The premiere, 28 June 1841, fell on Grisi's twenty-second birthday [documented: Macaulay 2020].
2. Gautier asked his co-librettist only that Giselle should have "a pretty death" [documented: Macaulay 2002].
3. Justamant's 1860s staging manual runs to 236 pages, with stick figures: men in black ink, women "and will o' the wisps" in red [documented: PNB].
4. Mary Skeaping's 1971 staging followed Pavlova: the sword is snatched away and the weak heart kills her [documented: ENB 2024].
5. The "character Wilis" of several nations in the livret seem never to have reached the stage, on the costume evidence [documented: Smith in Macaulay 2020].

### Set it beside
- `rite-of-spring-1913`: dancing to death again, but chosen by the group and danced alone, without love or rescue.
- `giselle-khan-2016`: the same rules (betrayal, a female community of the dead) moved to a garment-factory wall; the cross becomes the factory wall.
- `bayadere-1877`: another dead beloved met in a white act, reached through opium, not a grave; the vision is the hero's, not the woman's.
- `sylphide-1832`: the inverse; there the man chases the spirit and kills her, here the spirit saves the man.

## Лебединое озеро / Swan Lake (1877; 1895)
`id: swan-lake-1877-1895` · W file: W1 §10

### Design and art
W1 gives only a probable palette. The best-documented design fact is a negative one: **Odile was not a black swan in 1895.** Pierina Legnani wore "a royal blue costume with striking, multi-coloured glittered designs", with no feathers [documented: Marius Petipa Society, *Swan Lake* page; same claim on Wikipedia, "Swan Lake (1895)"]. A 1901 review has Kschessinska in "an elegant black dress", still without swan motifs [documented as quoted by the Petipa Society]. A photograph of Karsavina as Odile (c. 1914) shows a light costume [documented: same]. The feathered black Odile appears in Nemirovich-Danchenko's 1920 Bolshoi revival of Gorsky's staging, and in the West after Alexandra Fedorova-Fokine's *The Magic Swan* for the Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo (Metropolitan Opera, 1941), where Tamara Toumanova wore black so that audiences who knew only the lake act could tell her from Odette [documented: Petipa Society; Wikipedia]. So the white-black contrast that now defines the ballet's poster is a twentieth-century design choice. The swans of 1895 wore knee-length tutus with feathered bodices and small feather caps or headbands [probable: Mariinsky photographs]. Ratmansky's 2016 Zurich reconstruction, designed by Jérôme Kaplan, put Odile back in a multicoloured, glittering dress based on Legnani's [documented: Petipa Society]. The 1877 Moscow décor (Shangin, Valts, Groppius) was blamed by Modest Tchaikovsky for "the poverty of the production" [documented as quoted, Wikipedia].

### Readings and disputes
- **Was 1877 a failure?** The standard story says yes: critics called the score "too noisy, too 'Wagnerian'" [documented in W1]. Roland John Wiley (*Tchaikovsky's Ballets*, 1985) and those who follow him point out that the staging ran six seasons; Wikipedia, citing this line, gives **41 performances**, "many more than several other ballets" [documented]; History Hit (Amy Irvine, 2022) says "all 33 performances" from 1877 to 1883 sold out [documented]. The count itself is **contested**; the "flop" is better called a modest repertory life with a poor production.
- **One dancer or two?** It is now a rule that one ballerina dances Odette and Odile; Wikipedia notes it "is believed the ballet originally called for two different dancers" in 1877 [documented as Wikipedia's claim; **contested**, not settled in this pass]. The doubling is read as the century's split image of woman (virgin/seductress) by feminist critics [argued; Banes, *Dancing Women*, 1998, probable].
- **Ivanov or Petipa?** Wiley (*The Life and Ballets of Lev Ivanov*, 1997) argues the lake acts are Ivanov's own, more musical and inward; older credits read "Petipa" alone [probable].
- **Myth: Legnani invented the 32 fouettés for Odile.** Emma Bessone had done 14 in *The Haarlem Tulip* (1887); Legnani first did 32 in *Cinderella* (1893), and an 1893 *Sketch* interview has her turning "thirty-two pirouettes" in *Aladdin* in London [documented: Petipa Society, Legnani page]. Shiryaev says she did them *en dedans*, not *en dehors* as today [documented as reported, same].

### Facts worth telling
1. The "Black Swan pas de deux" was never so titled by Petipa; its music began as a pas de deux for two merrymakers in Act I of 1877 [documented: Petipa Society].
2. Olga Preobrazhenskaya thought the fouettés "a vulgar trick" and turned a circle of turns instead; Plisetskaya later did the same [documented: Petipa Society].
3. Soviet television showed *Swan Lake* during the August 1991 coup [documented in W1].
4. Only Act II was widely known in the West before the 1940s, which is why the costume contrast was needed [documented: Petipa Society].
5. Alexander Ekman's Oslo version uses 5,000 litres of water [documented in W1].

### Set it beside
- `swan-lake-bourne-1995`: the swans become male and feral; the double (the Stranger) tests the prince's desire, not his vow.
- `dying-swan-1905`: Fokine's two-minute solo for Pavlova keeps only the bird and its death; the swan as a single body, not a flock.
- `swan-lake-masilo-2010`: Odette as a man in a tutu in South Africa; the forced marriage becomes the plot, and AIDS the ending.
- `sylphide-1832`: the older winged maiden; there the lover kills her by a gift, here by a broken vow.

## Спящая красавица / The Sleeping Beauty (1890)
`id: sleeping-beauty-1890` · W file: W1 §8

### Design and art
W1 lists the 1890 scene painters and leaves the look to scope 04; three productions carry the design history. **1890**: Vsevolozhsky drew the costumes himself in Louis XIV dress for the Prologue and later French styles after the hundred-year sleep, against "heavily marbled Versailles-inspired backdrops" and a moving panorama for the journey to the castle [documented: Kirsten Tambling, *Apollo*, June 2023]. The production "consumed more than a quarter of the production department's annual budget" for 1890–91 [documented: Maureen Gupta, dissertation chapter, rev. 2008]. **1921**: Bakst's *Sleeping Princess* mixed Louis XIV and Louis XV, with ostrich feathers, panniers and high wigs; Aurora's bed was topped by an imperial eagle and one staircase copied the Winter Palace steps [documented: Tambling 2023]. His six sets drew on Ferdinando Galli Bibiena, Bérain and Boquet; the Paris house Muelle-Rossignol made costumes with solid embroidery and appliqué rather than stencil [documented: NGA Australia, "Ballets Russes: The Art of Costume"; Huddersfield thesis]. **1946**: Oliver Messel made over 200 costumes and four sets for the Royal Opera House reopening [documented: University of Bristol Theatre Collection]. Under rationing he used gilded pipe-cleaners for gold work, chandelier drops backed with sweet papers for jewels, plastered string, cellophane and dishcloths [documented: V&A text quoted in the Huddersfield thesis]. His Lilac Fairy tutu was bell-shaped in 1946 and flattened into a plate when revised in the late 1950s, because tutus follow fashion [documented: V&A, Lilac Fairy design O123630].

### Readings and disputes
- **Monarchist allegory or spectacle?** Damien Mahiet (*Dance Research* 34.2, 2016) and Lincoln Kirstein read the ballet as a celebration of absolutist order and Franco-Russian alliance [probable]; Tim Scholl (*"Sleeping Beauty": A Legend in Progress*, 2004) treats it as a *féerie* whose meaning each revival rewrites [probable]. Covered more fully in scope 01.
- **The Tsar's "very nice".** At the dress rehearsal Alexander III said only "very nice"; the story that Tchaikovsky was "sorely miffed" is widely told [documented: Jann Parry, *The Observer*, 18 June 2000]. Whether this was coolness or a court formula is **argued**; the diary note is commonly quoted but was not seen at source in this pass.
- **How badly did 1921 fail?** NGA Australia says it closed after **114** performances; the Library of Congress says **115** [documented: both]. The Alhambra's advance was "nearly £20,000" (LoC); Gupta gives the debt for which sets were seized as "reportedly £11,000", settled in 1924 for £2,000 [documented: both]. The figures disagree; teach the range.
- **Myth: the ballet was always a classic.** It was Petipa's spectacle, lost to the West until 1921, a failure there, and a national symbol only in 1946 [argued from the above].

### Facts worth telling
1. On 19 February 1946 the audience at Covent Garden came early because transport was poor, "their fine clothes smelled strongly of mothballs" [documented: Tambling 2023].
2. Fonteyn's mother was sent out to find "brocades, feathers, braids, gimps and all kinds of unrationed bits" [documented: Tambling 2023].
3. Stoll impounded the 1921 costumes; because they were barely worn and long stored, many survive in good condition [documented: NGA Australia].
4. Nijinska added dances in 1921 and appeared as the Humming Bird Princess [documented: LoC Nijinska Collection].
5. Messel recreated his 1946 designs for ABT's *Sleeping Princess* in 1976 [documented: Archives Hub, Messel Collection].

### Set it beside
- `balet-comique-1581`: the court ballet that *Beauty* quotes: allegory of royal order, here dressed as a fairy tale.
- `sleeping-beauty-ratmansky-2015`: Bakst's 1921 look rebuilt as "authentic" Petipa, so a design choice of 1921 is used to stand for 1890.
- `nutcracker-1892`: the same team two years later, with the court replaced by a bourgeois parlour and a sweetshop.
- `jewels-1967`: Balanchine's "Diamonds" as an abstract homage to the Petersburg court style, with no story at all.

## Щелкунчик / The Nutcracker (1892)
`id: nutcracker-1892` · W file: W1 §9

### Design and art
W1 names the scene painters (Konstantin Ivanov, Bocharov) and the costume designers (Vsevolozhsky, Ponomarev) but not what they made. The best surviving description is of the snow: the critic Konstantin Skalkovsky wrote of "downy pompoms on the white dresses", headdresses "in the form of rays of stars" and "clusters of wands" swaying in the dancers' hands, so that the opening grouping read as "an artistic allegory of a snowdrift" [documented: *Novosti i birzhevaya gazeta*, 8 Dec 1892, quoted by the Marius Petipa Society]. More than fifty dancers built a snowfall that grew into a blizzard and ended in a *tableau vivant* [documented: same]. The look was the most praised part of a coolly received evening. Two later design decisions matter more for what learners see now. Balanchine (NYCB, 1954) put the wands with snowballs back in 1968 "recalling the elaborate costumes" of the Mariinsky, and added a hidden sliding track so the Sugar Plum Fairy seems to glide [documented: PNB, Balanchine *Nutcracker* page]. And Willam Christensen's 1944 San Francisco staging was made under wartime rationing: ten yards of fabric per buyer, so company members queued in turn, and jewellery and flowers bought at Goodwill for $5 [documented: National Endowment for the Arts, "So You Think You Know The Nutcracker?", 2021]. The tree that grows in Act I is a stage-machinery effect present from 1892 [probable].

### Readings and disputes
- **Was it the celesta's debut?** The NEA says *The Nutcracker* "is thought to be the first work to employ the celesta with a full symphony orchestra", brought from Paris in secret [documented]; NYCB says it "was not the first composition to feature the celesta—nor even the first by Tschaikovsky himself" (he had used it in *The Voyevoda*, 1891) [documented: NYCB, 2024; *Voyevoda* probable]. **Contested** as phrased; both agree it is the instrument's defining use.
- **First outside Russia: Prague or London?** Tchaikovsky Research gives a complete performance at the Prague National Theatre on 4/17 August 1908, staged 24 times [documented]; Wikipedia says the first complete performance outside Russia was in England in 1934 [documented]. The Prague record is the earlier and better sourced; the London claim is a Western-canon habit.
- **A ballet of empire, or of childhood?** Jennifer Fisher ("Arabian Coffee in the Land of the Sweets", *DRJ*, 2003–4) reads Act II as goods from empire coded as peoples [probable]; NYCB's 2024 essay reads Dumas's changes (Marie Stahlbaum becomes Clara Silberhaus) as softening Hoffmann's "political edge" into child's wonder [documented]. Phil Chan (*Final Bow for Yellowface*, 2020) argues the Chinese dance can be kept but must be redesigned [probable].
- **Who choreographed it?** Petipa planned, Ivanov staged; Balanchine's version credits only "Lev Ivanov" for the original [documented: PNB], while Wiley (1997) gives Ivanov more independence [probable].

### Facts worth telling
1. Benois called the 1892 battle scene "disorderly pushing about from corner to corner … quite amateurish" [documented: Petipa Society].
2. Balletomanes complained they had to wait until after midnight to see the ballerina, who appears only in Act II [documented: Petipa Society].
3. Pavlova's *Snowflakes* (1911), based on the snow waltz, gave Americans their earliest glimpse of the ballet [documented: Wilson Center, 2020].
4. On opening night in 1954 Balanchine and Robbins sat down in Karinska's workshop and sewed costumes [documented: PNB].
5. Balanchine's advice to Christensen was to forget the original and make his own [documented: WTTW, 2017].

### Set it beside
- `coppelia-1870`: Hoffmann again, and dolls that seem alive; there the illusion is exposed, here it is believed.
- `hard-nut-1991`: Mark Morris moves the party to a 1970s suburban living room and restores Hoffmann's Hard Nut tale.
- `petrushka-1911`: the toy as a suffering soul in a public fair, against the toy as a prince in a private home.
- `sleeping-beauty-1890`: the same commissioner and composer; court spectacle shrinks to a family Christmas.

## Баядерка / La Bayadère (1877)
`id: bayadere-1877` · W file: W1 §7

### Design and art
W1 lists the five scene painters; it says less about the devices. In 1877 the Shades scene was set "in an illuminated castle in the sky" [documented: Marius Petipa Society]; the move to Himalayan peaks came in 1900, with new sets by Adolf Kvapp, Konstantin Ivanov, Pyotr Lambin and Orest Allegri and costumes by Yevgeny Ponomarev [documented: Ballet Alert! background on the Mariinsky 2002 reconstruction; Mariinsky credits]. Lambin's model for the Shades survives in the St Petersburg State Museum of Theatre and Music and was used to rebuild the set in 2002 [documented: same]. The Shades wear white tutus with gauze veils "stretched about their arms" [documented: Petipa Society]. In Nikiya's veil variation one end of a long tulle scarf is fixed to a wire in the flies; when she lets go it rises "as if supernaturally guided" [documented: Petipa Society]. Against this abstraction stand the props of the Indian acts: the Mariinsky's own programme calls its "plush tigers and elephants on wheels … touchingly amusing today" [documented: Olga Makarova, Mariinsky repertoire page]. The ramp is the scene's single architectural idea: a slope upstage that reads as a descent from another world [argued].

### Readings and disputes
- **Pure form or Orientalist fantasy?** Arlene Croce and the formalist tradition read the Shades as the first "symphonic" ballet, Vera Krasovskaya calling it "the beginning of the symphonic ballet" [documented as quoted: nureyev.org]. Postcolonial critics, after Edward Said (*Orientalism*, 1978), and Phil Chan (*Final Bow for Yellowface*, 2020) read the whole as an India invented in Europe, in which the "ideal" is white and classical and the "Indian" is costume and caricature [probable]. The two readings can be held together: the abstraction depends on the exoticism it leaves behind [argued].
- **Why did Act IV disappear?** Four explanations circulate. A lack of stage technicians after war and revolution, so it was dropped when the ballet returned in 1919 [documented as the claim of nureyev.org]; the Petrograd flood of 1924 ruining the sets [documented as "possible": Ballet Alert!]; "the rationalism of the Soviet view of the world" rejecting divine retribution [documented: Mariinsky programme]; an outright Soviet ban [documented as "some people think": Hungarian State Opera programme, Márk Gara]. Dates range from 1919 to the late 1920s. **Contested.**
- **How many Shades?** W1 gives 32, then 48 from 1900; the Petipa Society says 64 in 1877, in "eight rows of eight"; the Mikhailovsky says "the original 48"; the Kirov brought 32 to Paris in 1961 [documented: each source]. The number changed with the stage and the company.
- **Doré: memory or legend?** The Mikhailovsky says the idea came from a Doré engraving "according to the choreographer's own memoirs" [documented as the theatre's claim]; no page of Petipa's memoirs was seen in this pass, so treat as **probable**.

### Facts worth telling
1. The Golden (Bronze) Idol, now its best-known solo, was made by Nikolai Zubkovsky for himself in 1948, to a Persian march Minkus wrote for *Le Papillon* [documented: Petipa Society; Hungarian State Opera].
2. Cyril Beaumont could describe only five of seven original scenes in 1937 [documented: Gara].
3. In 1941 the ballet ended with Solor's suicide; this was later replaced by Solor left among the Shades [documented: Bolshoi Theatre page].
4. The West first saw the Shades in 1961, on the Kirov tour during which Nureyev defected [documented: Hungarian State Opera].
5. Makarova told the corps that "each member of it should feel like a ballerina" [documented: Australian Ballet, 2014].

### Set it beside
- `giselle-1841`: the Wilis' white act thirty-six years earlier; there the dead woman forgives, here the dead woman haunts.
- `les-sylphides-1909`: Fokine's plotless white act, made as a reverie, which keeps the Shades' form and drops the opium dream.
- `scheherazade-1910`: the Ballets Russes Orient: colour and sex instead of white geometry, sold to Paris rather than to a court.
- `swan-lake-1877-1895`: Ivanov danced Solor in 1877 and drew on the Shades for the lake acts [documented as claimed: Australian Ballet].

## Coppélia, ou la Fille aux yeux d'émail / Coppélia (1870)
`id: coppelia-1870` · W file: W1 §6

### Design and art
W1 names the designers; the look itself is thinly recorded. The subtitle is a design brief: "the girl with the enamel eyes", a doll whose glass or enamel eyes are the sign of the made thing [argued]. Act I is a "sunlit square of a village in Galicia", with Coppélius's house and its balcony window where the doll sits reading [documented for the setting: Kansas City Ballet programme; probable for the window]. Charles-Antoine Cambon painted the square and the workshop; Édouard Despléchin and Jean-Baptiste Lavastre painted the remaining scene; Paul Lormier and Alfred Albert designed costumes [documented: Wikipedia; Museum of Music History, 2020]. The workshop is a cabinet of automata in national and racial "types", the place Mara Mandradjieff (*DRJ* 55.3, 2023) locates the caricature [documented in W1]. The costume device that carries the plot is Coppélia's own dress, which Swanilda puts on to impersonate her, so one costume is worn by a "doll" and then by a woman playing a doll [probable]. Franz in 1870 was Eugénie Fiocre in breeches and doublet, a woman's legs on show for the Jockey Club [documented: Wikipedia; Degas had painted her in *La Source*, probable]. The national dances (mazurka, csárdás) were costumed as Polish and Hungarian festival dress [probable]. Designs by Lormier and Albert survive at BnF-BMO [probable]; no detailed account of the 1870 colours was found in this pass.

### Readings and disputes
- **Comedy of mechanisation, or of race?** Older readings (Ivor Guest, *The Ballet of the Second Empire*, 1953–55, 1974) treat it as the Second Empire's last bright comedy before the fall [probable]; Mandradjieff (2023) reads the automata as "human-objects" carrying slavery and colonialism [documented in W1]. Lynn Garafola ("The Travesty Dancer in Nineteenth-Century Ballet", *DRJ*, 1985) reads Franz *en travesti* as male roles turned into female display [probable].
- **How long was Franz a woman in Paris?** "Well into the 1950s" (Bru Zane Mediabase); "until after World War II" (Wikipedia); "up until WWII" (Jefferson Performing Arts Society study guide) [documented: each]. The end date is **contested**.
- **How many performances before the war?** Eighteen, the last on 31 August 1870 (Wikipedia on Bozzacchi; Gramilano; Museum of Music History); "only twelve times" (JPAS) [documented: each]. Eighteen has the better sourcing.
- **Myths of "firsts".** A study guide calls it "the first ballet to incorporate … national dances" and "the first ballet to use automata" [documented: JPAS]. National dances were standard at the Opéra from the 1830s (Elssler's cachucha, 1836) [probable], so the first claim is false; the second is unproven. Use "earliest known" or drop it.

### Facts worth telling
1. Swanilda was first meant for Adèle Grantzow; when she fell ill, Delibes was asked to look for a dancer in Italy and came back empty-handed [documented: Wikipedia on Bozzacchi; Gramilano, 2020].
2. Bozzacchi made her stage debut in the role, before Napoleon III and Empress Eugénie [documented: Gramilano].
3. Unpaid during the siege, she grew weak from hunger, caught smallpox and died on her seventeenth birthday [documented: Petipa Society; Wikipedia].
4. Delibes played the organ at her funeral, improvising a march on *Coppélia* melodies [documented: Museum of Music History].
5. Saint-Léon collapsed after the performance of 1 September 1870 and died the next day [documented: Museum of Music History].

### Set it beside
- `petrushka-1911`: the doll is the one with a soul, and the showman, not the lover, is mocked.
- `nutcracker-1892`: Hoffmann again; the doll becomes a prince rather than being exposed as a machine.
- `triadic-ballet-1922`: Schlemmer's Bauhaus figurines make every dancer a mechanism, by design rather than as a joke.
- `giselle-1841`: the Romantic plot in reverse: here the woman sees through the illusion and saves the man by cleverness, not death.

## La Sylphide (1832)
`id: sylphide-1832` · W file: W1 §4

### Design and art
W1 has the white skirt and gas light; four more concrete points. **The skirt**: a long, vaporous petticoat of muslin that puffs out a dress of white crêpe, falling below the knee [documented: INA, "La Sylphide ressuscitée par Pierre Lacotte"; Chroniques de Danse, 2017]. **The wings**: small butterfly wings at the back, the Sylph's only sign of not being human [documented: INA]. **The tartan**: James in a kilt of his clan's colours, red and yellow, so that Act I is warm colour against Act II's white [documented: INA describing the Lacotte staging, which follows Lami]. **The light**: gas, whose "cerulean radiance, once perfected by the addition of colored glass media", suited the white act [documented: EBSCO Research Starters]. Gautier later described what followed: designers were asked only for "romantic forests" and "pretty German moonlight", there was "a great abuse of white gauze, tulle and tarlatan", and "white was almost the only colour adopted" [documented: Gautier quoted by INA, our translation]. So one costume and one light became the period's look. Ciceri's set maquettes survive at the Bibliothèque de l'Opéra; Lacotte's 1972 restaging rebuilt the sets from them and the costumes (made by Michel Fresnay) from Lami's drawings and fabric descriptions in the Archives nationales [documented: Lacotte quoted in George Carvalho, PhD thesis, Universidade de São Paulo, 2019].

### Readings and disputes
- **Who designed the tutu?** Britannica: "Eugène Lami designed a muslin dress" that "became the new uniform of the classical dancer" [documented]. EBSCO: "Charles Ciceri, working in collaboration with painter Eugène Lami, designed" it [documented]. W1 flags the attribution as contested; the two reference works disagree in so many words.
- **Myth: Taglioni was the first on pointe.** Britannica now says "one of the first" [documented]. Amalia Brugnoli "had worked on pointe" earlier (NYPL, *500 Years of Italian Dance*), and E. A. Théleur's *Letters on Dancing* (1831) already shows women posed on their toes in soft shoes [documented: NYPL; San Francisco Classical Voice, 2026]. What changed was the *meaning*: pointe as character, not trick [documented: SFCV]. Taglioni wore soft satin slippers darned at the tip, not blocked shoes [documented: SFCV].
- **The sylph as ideal woman or as male fantasy.** Ivor Guest (*The Romantic Ballet in Paris*, 1966) reads the work as the birth of the Romantic ideal [probable]; Lynn Garafola (ed., *Rethinking the Sylph*, 1997) and Sally Banes (*Dancing Women*, 1998) read the sylph as an image male desire constructs and then destroys [probable].
- **Reconstruction or new work?** Lacotte's version is "entirely new" in steps and "criticised by some … as inauthentic" [documented: Wikipedia, citation flagged]; Lacotte himself spoke of a ballet that "would be reborn" [documented as quoted, Carvalho 2019].

### Facts worth telling
1. Taglioni danced the role at the Paris Opéra exclusively for six years, to 1838 [documented: Madrid 1842 article, *Vremennik*, 2023].
2. The ballet reached London (July 1832), St Petersburg (1837) and Milan (1841) with Taglioni herself [documented: Chroniques de Danse].
3. Petipa revived Taglioni's version in St Petersburg in 1892, with extra music by Drigo; a Drigo variation from it is now danced in *Paquita* [documented: Wikipedia].
4. Ghislaine Thesmar was made an étoile of the Paris Opéra after two performances of Lacotte's Sylphide [documented: Carvalho 2019].
5. INA's own notice dates the premiere "12 mai 1832"; the date is 12 March [documented: INA; Britannica]. Even archives slip.

### Set it beside
- `sylphide-bournonville-1836`: the Danish remake, now "the" *Sylphide*, made because Paris charged too much for the score.
- `les-sylphides-1909`: a different, plotless work; the confusion of the two titles is itself a lesson in naming.
- `giselle-1841`: the white act turned round: the spirit forgives instead of dying of the man's gift.
- `triadic-ballet-1922`: costume again as the whole concept, but hard geometric shells against soft muslin.

## L'Oiseau de feu / Жар-птица / The Firebird (1910)
`id: firebird-1910` · W file: W2 §2

*Design and art: W2 §2 already covers Golovin's garden, Bakst's Firebird and the Goncharova 1926 redesign in detail; not repeated.*

### Readings and disputes
- **Who made the music's rules?** Fokine, in a 1910 interview, said the ballet was made "hand in hand … we wrote every bar together" [documented: quoted from *S.-Peterburgskiye teatral'nïye novosti*, 10 Sept 1910, in "Forced Freedom", inLibra open-access chapter]. Stravinsky later wrote that Fokine made the choreography "section by section, as the music was handed to him" [documented as quoted: EBSCO Research Starters], which puts the composer first. NYCB notes he would "decidedly avoid" Fokine's method afterwards [documented in W2]. Two authors tell the same collaboration with the lead reversed.
- **Did Liadov fail, or never accept?** The much-repeated story is that Liadov was too slow. A current summary says there is "no evidence … that he ever accepted the commission to begin with" [documented: sin80, after Wikipedia]. A further version has Diaghilev also try Glazunov and Nikolai Sokolov before Stravinsky [documented: Utah Symphony programme, 2026; Houston Symphony]. Stravinsky's own line: when Diaghilev phoned to say "Go ahead", he had "already" begun [documented as quoted: Utah Symphony]. **Contested.**
- **How Russian is it?** Richard Taruskin ("Russian Folk Melodies in *The Rite of Spring*", *JAMS* 33/3, 1980; *Stravinsky and the Russian Traditions*, 1996) shows Stravinsky drew on Rimsky-Korsakov's *One Hundred Russian Folk Songs* (1877) more than any other source for *Firebird* and *Petrushka* [documented: Taruskin 1980]. Stravinsky later downplayed such debts, "not wanting to appear indebted" to Russia or the past [documented: Benjamin Pesetsky, programme essay, 2022]. A small contradiction inside the record: Stravinsky acknowledged three folk melodies in *Firebird*; Russian scholars had identified only two [documented: Taruskin 1980, n.].
- **A modern ballet or an export souvenir?** Lynn Garafola (*Diaghilev's Ballets Russes*, 1989) reads it as Russianness designed for Paris [probable]; the Mariinsky's own programme calls it "an export Russian fairy tale" [documented in W2].

### Facts worth telling
1. Pavlova is reported to have said, "I shall never dance to such nonsense!" [documented as quoted from Joni Lynn Steshko, PhD, UCLA, 2000, in "Forced Freedom"].
2. Fokine danced the Firebird "without a turnout and without any preparations", arms opening "like wings … in complete contradiction of all ballet arm-positions" [documented: Fokine, *Memoirs*, quoted in "Forced Freedom"].
3. The Paris Opéra's machinery could not fly a dancer, so pointe had to make the bird seem to float [documented: "Forced Freedom"].
4. The score is dedicated to Andrei Rimsky-Korsakov, the teacher's son, who was with Stravinsky while he wrote it [documented as quoted: Utah Symphony].
5. A fee of "100 rubles" for the score is given by Oregon Ballet Theatre's historian [documented as OBT's claim, 2011]; no primary source was seen, so treat the figure as **unverified**.

### Set it beside
- `swan-lake-1877-1895`: another bird-woman, but bound by a curse, not free; Ivan frees the princesses, Siegfried fails Odette.
- `rite-of-spring-1913`: three years later the same composer uses folk sources not for a fairy tale but for a sacrifice, with the magic removed.
- `red-poppy-1927`: the Soviet state's own "national" ballet for export, made with the methods the Ballets Russes had invented abroad.

## Petrouchka / Петрушка / Petrushka (1911)
`id: petrushka-1911` · W file: W2 §3

*Design and art: W2 §3 covers Benois's fair, cells and curtain. One addition: the street-theatre Petrushka wore a red dress, red hat and often a red nose; Benois kept the red only for cuffs, ruff edge, hat and trousers, and gave the Moor's cell fruit, flowers and animals against Petrushka's stars and devil [documented: Interlude, 2021].*

### Readings and disputes
- **Whose libretto, and whose money?** The Library of Congress credits "Igor Stravinsky and Alexandre Benois" [documented]; IRCAM's catalogue credits "Alexandre Benois" alone [documented]; the Boston Symphony notes that Benois appears on the title page as co-author [documented]. As co-author Benois held a legal claim to one sixth of the performance rights in the music, which Stravinsky resented and contested (André Boucourechliev, *Stravinsky*, 1987, as cited in a doctoral thesis on CORE) [documented as cited]. The credit dispute was a royalty dispute.
- **Why the 1947 revision?** It "is usually claimed" that Stravinsky reorchestrated it to re-copyright the score and collect royalties; the BSO's note adds that he also restored the piano he had "forgotten" after the second tableau [documented: BSO]. Both motives can be true; teach as **argued**.
- **A Russian work or a Paris work?** An *Artforum* essay ("The Russianness of *Petrouchka*") reads the Moor–Petrushka fight through Slavic epic, where the *Arap* is interchangeable with dragons and slays the hero with a sword [documented]; Catriona Kelly (*Petrushka: The Russian Carnival Puppet Theatre*, 1990) roots it in the fairground puppet show [probable]. Russian critics in 1911 found the score "too gaudy, coarse" and full of "street melodies", though Nikolai Myaskovsky heard "life itself" [documented: Sofia Philharmonic programme].
- **The Moor in blackface.** Wendy Perron (*Dance Magazine*, 14 July 2020) finds no critic objecting before ABT's 2005 revival; a 2015 Sarasota Ballet dress-rehearsal photograph caused a "firestorm" [documented]. The coconut-worship business was Benois's idea, from memories of blackface puppets [documented: Christie's lot essay; Perron]. Isabelle Fokine, guardian of the choreography, proposed replacing him with a "Warrior" based on a Cossack doll, and called "blue face" "not sufficient" [documented: Perron 2020].

### Facts worth telling
1. Benois publicly criticised Fokine's crowd scenes in *Rech'* (1911), then retracted in a letter titled "Criticism of Collaborators" [documented: thesis on CORE, citing Wiley 1981].
2. At the first orchestra rehearsal the players refused to play music "in discord with the laws of harmony" [documented: Nijinska, *Early Memoirs*, quoted by LoC].
3. Stravinsky said he was "more proud of these last pages than of anything else in the score" [documented: LA Phil].
4. The fair score quotes two waltzes by Josef Lanner as well as the French song [documented: Walsh 1988, as cited in the CORE thesis].
5. A 1936 book by Prince Peter Lieven called it "the greatest achievement of the Ballets-Russes" [documented: Christie's].

### Set it beside
- `coppelia-1870`: the doll as a joke on the deluded lover; here the doll is the only one who suffers.
- `the-cage-1951`: Robbins, who danced Petrushka in 1942, later made another closed world in which a female group kills its males.
- `triadic-ballet-1922`: the human body made into a puppet deliberately, as a Bauhaus experiment rather than as pathos.

## Le Sacre du printemps / Весна священная / The Rite of Spring (1913)
`id: rite-of-spring-1913` · W file: W2 §5

*Design and art: W2 §5 covers Roerich's palette, Talashkino sources and the 1987 Archer revival designs; not repeated.*

### Readings and disputes
- **Was there a riot?** The legend: fistfights, a hatpin, forty people taken out by police at the interval, a duel next day [documented as the legend's content: Madison Mainwaring, *Lapham's Quarterly*, 2014]. Against it: Esteban Buch (EHESS) went to the Paris prefecture archives and found the relevant file missing; his "hunch" is that a couple of routine policemen "witnessed the scene without doing much" [documented: Ivan Hewett, BBC News, 29 May 2013]. Katie Buzard (Illinois Public Media, 2025) finds no evidence of physical fights or police, notes five curtain calls, and says the word "riot" was attached only from the 1920s, partly to sell the work to American audiences [documented]. The next-day press complains chiefly of noise and stamping: Gaston de Pawlowski in *Comœdia* had to cup his ears "in the middle of an indescribable racket" [documented: Buzard]. Teach as **contested**: very loud, possibly violent, certainly mythologised; Buch's phrase is that the riot "acquires greater importance as time goes on" [documented: BBC].
- **What did they boo?** Alfredo Casella and M.-D. Calvocoressi thought the protest was at Nijinsky's choreography, not the score [documented: LibreTexts]; Taruskin agrees "it was not Stravinsky's music that did the shocking" [documented in W2]; Monteux blamed two factions in the audience attacking each other and the orchestra [documented as recalled: uDiscover Music]. Stravinsky said the storm broke when the curtain rose on the "knock-kneed and long-braided Lolitas jumping up and down" [documented: BBC].
- **Did Diaghilev want a scandal?** Lydia Sokolova: "He knew there was going to be trouble" [documented: BBC]; his press announcement promised "impassioned debate" [documented: BBC]. Stephen Walsh notes the dress rehearsal passed "without a murmur" [documented: BBC].
- **Hodson's reconstruction.** Hodson claimed about 85 per cent of the choreography recovered, the rest by "educated guess" [documented: Mainwaring 2014]; Mainwaring finds it lacks "the tense, compressed energy" of the 1913 reports [documented]; Robert Craft disputed its claims [probable, W2].

### Facts worth telling
1. Saint-Saëns did not storm out: Stravinsky said he was not there [documented: LibreTexts].
2. Stravinsky also denied Cocteau's tale of a tearful Diaghilev reciting Pushkin in the Bois de Boulogne after the show [documented: LibreTexts].
3. Astruc paid Diaghilev 25,000 francs per performance; the premiere took 35,000 francs in tickets at doubled prices [documented: LibreTexts].
4. The same evening also gave *Les Sylphides*, *Le Spectre de la rose* and the *Polovtsian Dances* [documented: LibreTexts].
5. Rehearsal counts disagree: about 120 dance rehearsals (Stravinsky's estimate) or "over 130 dance calls", with 17 orchestral sessions [documented: Past Due History, 2026; Buzard 2025].

### Set it beside
- `giselle-1841`: a maiden dances to death in both; in 1841 the dead dance men to death for revenge, in 1913 the community kills the maiden for renewal.
- `sacre-bausch-1975`: Pina Bausch's version on a floor of peat, where the sacrifice is read as violence of men on women.
- `les-noces-1923`: the same composer's peasant ritual ten years on; the bride is consumed by the community without a death.
- `green-table-1932`: Jooss's Dance of Death, where the ritual killing is the state's war, not the tribe's spring.

## L'Après-midi d'un faune / Afternoon of a Faun (1912)
`id: apres-midi-faune-1912` · W file: W2 §4

*Design and art: W2 §4 covers Bakst's set, the nymphs' pleated tunics and the faun's piebald body-stocking; not repeated.*

### Readings and disputes
- **Which *Faune* is Nijinsky's?** Ann Hutchinson Guest, in conversation with Alastair Macaulay (2017), holds that what she and Claudia Jeschke produce from the 1915 score "is the ballet as Nijinsky wanted it", and that memory-based versions should not be billed "Choreography by Nijinsky" [documented]. She also concedes "he wrote the score in 1915. How much it may have departed from the 1912 original, no one can say" [documented]. The memory lineage runs through Rambert's company, which took it in 1931 as staged by Leon Woizikovsky and Lydia Sokolova [documented: Rambert performance database]. Nijinsky himself, in 1916, objected that Massine's performances used his name while not dancing it "as I devised" [documented as quoted, Macaulay 2017]. So there is a score that may not be 1912, and a tradition that may not be Nijinsky.
- **Was the notation shaped by Dalcroze?** A French teaching dossier (Réseau Canopé, 2007) raises the hypothesis of a "retroactive" Dalcroze influence on the notation, since Nijinsky met the Hellerau school in 1912 and wrote the score only in 1915 [documented as a hypothesis]. Nijinska says he had fixed the choreography by 1911 [documented as reported, same]. **Argued.**
- **Scandal by design?** Some accounts say matrons fainted in the boxes, with "suggestions" that Diaghilev paid them [documented as rumour: The Rehearsal Studio blog, 2020]; Diaghilev did order an immediate repeat performance [documented: Cheryl Huddleston, PhD, University of Georgia, 2010]. Hanna Järvinen (*Dance Research* 27/1, 2009) argues the scandal came because the work refused the expected erotic spectacle, not because it supplied it [documented in W2]. Two readings: provocation managed, or expectation betrayed.
- **Who danced on 29 May?** W2 records Nijinska as cast but pregnant and not dancing; Huddleston (2010) lists her in the premiere cast and the *Giornale della Danza* (2024) names her among the nymphs [documented: each]. **Contested.**

### Facts worth telling
1. Guest says the biggest difference between the score and memory versions is in the footwork: Nijinsky's very high arch shows in the number and detail of walking steps [documented: Huddleston 2010, from interview].
2. Rehearsal counts vary: "a novantina" (about ninety) of rehearsals, or "over 100 hours" [documented: *Giornale della Danza* 2024; Guest in Macaulay 2017].
3. Nijinsky's notation lay unread for nearly forty years after his death because no one could decode his adapted Stepanov system [documented: Google Books record, *Nijinsky's Faune Restored*; Canopé].
4. Cyril Beaumont said the work becomes "meaningless" when given without "the essential nymphs", as sometimes happened [documented as quoted: Wikipedia].
5. Sharon Eyal's *Faunes* (Paris Opéra) mixes male and female dancers in the faun's material [documented: *Giornale della Danza*].

### Set it beside
- `afternoon-faun-robbins-1953`: the same music in a ballet studio before a mirror; the gaze moves from nymph to audience.
- `apollon-musagete-1928`: another "Greek" ballet, but neoclassical and turned-out, where Nijinsky's was archaic and turned-in.
- `les-sylphides-1909`: Fokine's white reverie three years earlier, the very lightness *Faune* refuses.
- `rite-of-spring-1913`: the next Nijinsky grammar: from flat profile to turned-in mass; from one faun to a tribe.

## Schéhérazade / Scheherazade (1910)
`id: scheherazade-1910` · W file: W2 §1

*Design and art: W2 §1 covers Bakst's palette and room. Two additions: the stage crew at the premiere judged the translucent costumes obscene, which Bakst said made him ashamed; and a front curtain with layered hunting and terrace scenes was painted by Valentin Serov [documented: Centre national du costume et de la scène, "Shéhérazade" collection page]. Bakst's green-and-blue combination was taken up "by everyone from Matisse to Cartier's jewellery designers" [documented: Marina Frolova-Walker, Gresham College lecture, 2019].*

### Readings and disputes
- **Who wrote it?** W2 gives Bakst (credited) against Benois (claimed). The record splits three ways: Frolova-Walker says "the plot was invented by Fokine and Bakst" [documented: Gresham 2019]; the CNCS says Benois was "probably" behind the adaptation but the livret is "signed by Léon Bakst and Michel Fokine" [documented]; Benois's *Reminiscences* (1941) claim it for him [probable]. **Contested.**
- **What colour was the Golden Slave?** A Van Cleef & Arpels dance-history page says Nijinsky wore "a dark plum paint blackface" [documented]; the CNCS says his body was made up "grey-blue" (*gris-bleu*) with gold jewellery [documented]; Sandie Bourne cites Ramsay Burt (1995) for "blackface" and Kevin Kopelson (1998) for a "mulatto slave" [documented: Bourne, Mobballet paper]. Hanna Järvinen ("Ballets Russes and Blackface", *DRJ* 52.3, 2020) argues that canonising such works as "masterpieces" "amounts to complicity with racism" [documented: abstract]. Read against the source: in the *Nights* frame tale the queen's lover is a Black cook; the ballet makes him a gilded slave, "expurgated … with some racial shuffling" (Artem Lozynsky, "Orientalism and the Ballets Russes", *Situations* 1, 2007) [documented].
- **Orientalism as ideology, or as a modernist tool?** Following Said, Garafola (*Crafting the Ballets Russes*, Morgan Library, 2024) writes that "racist representation pervades the works of the Ballets Russes", with the Golden Slave "feminized and emasculated" [documented as quoted in Pauline Grégoire, MA thesis, Concordia, 2025]. A 2024 Korean study (KERD) argues the "wild East" was the Ballets Russes' means to a new modern aesthetic, distinct from Romantic Orientalism [documented: abstract]. Grégoire adds a third view: unlike the Romantic sylph, Zobeide is a woman shown as "desirous, sexual, and active" [documented].
- **Ballet led fashion, or fashion led ballet?** The Met says Poiret was "spurred on by the success" of *Schéhérazade* to launch harem trousers (1911) and lampshade tunics (1913) [documented: Met, "Paul Poiret"]; Poiret himself later denied owing his Orientalism to Bakst [probable: Poiret, *En habillant l'époque*, 1930; not seen at source in this pass].

### Facts worth telling
1. Rimsky-Korsakov's widow protested against "this unauthorized and anti-artistic" use of the score [documented: Frolova-Walker 2019].
2. The ballet was never staged in Russia in its time, yet caused a scandal there for its free handling of the music [documented: CNCS].
3. Bakst urged that the Shah's men "cut everyone to pieces … above all their Negro lovers" [documented as quoted from Grigoriev: Lozynsky 2007].
4. Fokine swapped pointe shoes for demi-pointes like Eastern slippers and set arm and chest movement for seated dancers [documented: Van Cleef & Arpels Dance Reflections].
5. Ravel let the craze for the Orient shape the last dance of *Daphnis et Chloé* (1912) [documented: Frolova-Walker 2019].

### Set it beside
- `bayadere-1877`: the imperial Orient, white and classical in its ideal act; here the Orient is all body and colour, with no white act at all.
- `sylphide-1832`: Lozynsky's pairing: the ballerina who rises on her toes (soul of the West) against the harem dancer who sinks to the floor (body of the East).
- `train-bleu-1924`: fashion and ballet trading places again, but now the source is the Riviera and the designer is Chanel.

## Apollon musagète / Apollo (1928)
`id: apollon-musagete-1928` · W file: W2 §9

### Design and art
W2 has Bauchant and Chanel; one correction to the "white ballet" image. Bauchant's "set" was an existing oil painting (now Wadsworth Atheneum) whose rocky ridge became the scenery, "hardly … a set design in the traditional sense"; another painting was copied for the front cloth [documented: Jane Pritchard, V&A blog, 2010]. Lifar's 1928 costume was a **red and gold** tunic, belt and cloak, not white; a Calliope costume sold at Chenil Galleries in 1973 was described as a "lilac pleated chiffon bodice, full skirt of silver organza" [documented: Pritchard 2010]. So the *ballet blanc* Stravinsky imagined was not what Paris saw in 1928; it arrived through Chanel's 1929 recostuming and, finally, through NYCB's practice clothes [argued]. Sasha's London photographs of 1928 record the first version of the Muses' costumes [documented: Pritchard].

### Readings and disputes
- **Restore the birth, or keep the cut?** Balanchine cut the birth prologue in 1979 and ended on the Muses' "peacock" fan of arabesques instead of the climb to Parnassus [documented: Wikipedia; sin80]. NYCB dances the cut version; Suzanne Farrell restored the birth for her company (2001), as did Arthur Mitchell and Iain Webb (Sarasota, 2015) [documented: Wikipedia]. Penny Askew notes "much debate among ballet lovers about which ending is better" [documented, 2020]. An essay in the *Massachusetts Review* ("Of Foundations and Evolutions: *Apollo* and Impersonality", 18 Jan 2024; author not recorded in this pass) argues the cuts "caught up with" Kirstein's 1947 claim for a pure new classicism; the ballet looks "more chastised" without narrative [documented]. The maker's last version against the version that made the reputation.
- **Lifar's ballet or Balanchine's?** André Levinson objected to Balanchine's use of Lifar, whom Diaghilev "imposed"; Kirstein's writing is "anti-Lifarian", partly because of Lifar's wartime collaboration [documented: *Massachusetts Review* 2024]. A foundation story that needed its first star removed [argued].
- **Who saw what first?** Bolm's Washington version omitted the birth scene; Paris opened with it [documented: *The New Yorker*, "News from the Muses", 11 Sept 1978]. Stravinsky "took no interest" in the US staging [documented: Wikipedia]. So "the" *Apollo* was in its first form an American ballet without a prologue.
- **Polyhymnia: mime or rhetoric?** The Balanchine Foundation catalogue has her "finger to lips" representing mime; Wikipedia's list calls her the muse of rhetoric [documented: both].

### Facts worth telling
1. The Coolidge commission paid Stravinsky $1,000 [documented: LA Phil programme note].
2. Calliope's variation has a couplet of Boileau's alexandrines written over the score, matched by the violins' metre [documented: LA Phil].
3. Night-time neon in Piccadilly "was said to have inspired" the opening and closing of Apollo's hands [documented: Pritchard, V&A blog].
4. The touching fingers of Terpsichore and Apollo quote Michelangelo's *Creation of Adam* [documented: Pritchard; Askew].
5. Diaghilev called the score "extraordinarily calm … somehow music not of this world" [documented as quoted: Nancy Reynolds, *Routledge Encyclopedia of Modernism*, 2016].

### Set it beside
- `apres-midi-faune-1912`: an earlier "Greek" ballet in flat archaic profile; Apollo is Greek through French classicism and turned-out line.
- `agon-1957`: the third of the Stravinsky–Balanchine "Greek" works, where the gods were dropped altogether.
- `ballet-de-la-nuit-1653`: a king costumed as Apollo the sun; in 1928 Apollo is not a king but a student who learns restraint.
- `symphony-in-c-1947`: the "white" Balanchine ballet that the 1928 costumes did not yet deliver.

## Agon (1957)
`id: agon-1957` · W file: W2 §11

*Design and art: W2 §11 covers the practice clothes and Porcher's lighting. One addition: an *Arts* (MDPI) article, "The Balanchine Dilemma: 'So-Called Abstraction' and the Rhetoric of Circumvention in Black-and-White Ballets" (2020), notes that the duet's leotards in converse tones and reversed leg colours produce a tessellated effect, and that Kirstein called it "near nudity" [documented].*

### Readings and disputes
- **Was skin colour part of the choreography?** Arthur Mitchell said so for decades: "There was a definite use of the skin tones … so that even the placing of the hands or the arms provided a color structure integrated into the choreographic one" (Mitchell in Mason, 1991) [documented as quoted: MDPI 2020]; "Agon doesn't look the same if you see two white people doing it" [documented: NEA, *American Artscape*, 2016]. Balanchine, interviewed by John Gruen in 1971, denied being "political" [documented as cited: Columbia University Libraries, *Arthur Mitchell: Harlem's Ballet Trailblazer*]. Alastair Macaulay (2021) holds that the colour placing was "partly for musical reasons", where "music's black and white notes are mixed", and that the racial element was "underplayed in critical discussion" in 1957 [documented]. Three readings: intended politics, musical structure, or both.
- **Abstraction or "so-called abstraction"?** The MDPI article asks why, if the contrast is integral, it did not survive in mono-racial recastings, and whether the Black role came to be seen as "extraneous" [documented]. Brenda Dixon Gottschild (*Digging the Africanist Presence in American Performance*, 1996, ch. 5) reads "jazz-Africanist inflected elements" in both score and steps [documented as quoted: Columbia exhibition]; she calls Black dancers in *Agon* "the fulfillment of Mr. B's subconscious sub-rosa subtext" [documented: thINKingDANCE interview, 2013].
- **Who said what about Faubus?** In 1958 Balanchine rearranged a televised *Nutcracker* so Mitchell partnered Diana Adams. Macaulay quotes him telling Mitchell, "This is for Governor Faubus!"; the Columbia exhibition gives "I hope Governor Faubus is watching", overheard [documented: both]. Same event, two wordings.

### Facts worth telling
1. Mitchell was not allowed to dance the duet on national television until 1966 [documented: thINKingDANCE, editor's note].
2. When producers objected to "the black guy", Balanchine said, "If Mitchell doesn't dance, New York City Ballet doesn't dance" [documented: Mitchell in NEA 2016].
3. The pas de deux was the first section Balanchine choreographed; Mitchell was still listed in the corps de ballet [documented: Columbia exhibition].
4. Balanchine called it the "IBM ballet", "as though controlled by an electronic brain" [documented: Columbia exhibition].
5. Balanchine's 1933 letter imagined a company of "eight Caucasian and eight colored dancers" [documented as quoted: *Los Angeles Times*, 2010].

### Set it beside
- `apollon-musagete-1928`: the first Stravinsky–Balanchine "Greek" ballet; there the men's black tights were replaced to make it all-white, here black and white is the system [documented: MDPI 2020].
- `ballet-de-la-nuit-1653`: court dance as the source of royal display; in *Agon* the court-dance titles are pretexts, the "cubist still life" of Denby's phrase.
- `creole-giselle-1984`: Mitchell's own company, staging the Romantic canon on Black bodies in a story of colour and class.
- `in-the-middle-1987`: Forsythe's practice-clothes ballet thirty years on, pushing the same off-balance classical vocabulary further.

## Les Noces / Свадебка / The Wedding (1923)
`id: les-noces-1923` · W file: W2 §7

*Design and art: W2 §7 covers the move from colour to brown and white. Two additions: Goncharova assigned meanings to her final palette, "pale ash blue, for serenity and hope; gray, the color of ashes and the past; yellow ochre, suggesting anxiety", with a brown frame "recalling the earth" [documented: The Dance Enthusiast, report of the Works & Process symposium, Guggenheim, 2023]; and the men's tops were simplified into Russian or Ukrainian peasant shirts after an interim "almost a blue blouse" stage [documented: Lynn Garafola, NYPL Doc Chat 46, 2022].*

### Readings and disputes
- **Who killed the colour?** Three accounts. Nijinska's: she saw Goncharova's sketches, "magnificent colours, theatrical and sumptuously Russian", said they were for "a Russian opera", and argued with Diaghilev "for a whole year" (to John Martin, *New York Times*, 1936) [documented: Garafola, Royal Academy of Dance *Dance Gazette*, 2022; NYPL Doc Chat]. Marina Frolova-Walker's: "the idea of black-and-white sets and costumes came from Mikhail Larionov", Goncharova's husband, after the spare scoring was fixed [documented: Gresham College, "Playing with the Past", 2020]. Goncharova's own: she moved through a suburban version and pastels before the final one [documented: Frolova-Walker quoting her]. **Contested**; the choreographer, the designer's partner and the music each claim the cause.
- **Nijinska against Diaghilev, or against Stravinsky?** Garafola has Diaghilev threaten to replace her until he saw Meyerhold's touring company [documented as reported: Dance Enthusiast]. The same report argues the real quarrel was with Stravinsky, who wanted dancers in folk costume beside musicians in evening dress, "a divertissement of the masquerade type" [documented]; IRCAM's note confirms he wanted "all my instrumental apparatus" visible "beside the actors" [documented]. In 1923 the four pianos stood on stage partly because they would not fit the Gaîté-Lyrique pit [documented: Garafola, NYPL].
- **Neoclassical or abstract-spiritual?** Garafola (*La Nijinska*, 2022) places it in Nijinska's neoclassicism; the Dance Enthusiast reviewer calls that a "queer notion" and reads the triangles as Kandinskian "spiritual striving", citing Nijinska's wish (1963) "to express the mystery felt by a boy and girl on their wedding day" [documented: Dance Enthusiast 2023]. A third reading, protofeminist: the bride does not look happy, and marriage reads as "the woman's obligation" [documented as an argued essay: *Musical Geography*, imagined letter from Stravinsky].

### Facts worth telling
1. The bride's braids are about eight feet long; the women wind them round her neck "as if strangling her" [documented: Garafola, *Dance Gazette* 2022].
2. Nijinska ignored the sung text almost entirely: its nightingales, rings and swans "find no echo" in the steps [documented: Garafola 2022].
3. Stravinsky said he had "never either seen or heard" such peasant festivities [documented: IRCAM programme note, our translation].
4. The Russian title *Svadebka* is a homely diminutive for "wedding" [documented: Frolova-Walker 2020].
5. Rehearsals ran six days a week from March 1923; on matinee days the second rehearsal was from 9 to 11 at night [documented: Garafola 2022].

### Set it beside
- `rite-of-spring-1913`: the same composer's pagan ritual, ending in death; *Noces* is its Christian, bell-like sequel, ending at a bedroom door [documented for "bell-like": Frolova-Walker].
- `les-biches-1924`: Nijinska's next ballet, a chic house party where gender and desire are ambiguous, not a village rite.
- `white-haired-girl-matsuyama-1955`: a peasant woman's story told by a mass ensemble, with the collective as liberator rather than consumer.
- `giselle-1841`: the bride who never marries; in *Noces* she marries and is lost all the same.

## Parade (1917)
`id: parade-1917` · W file: W2 §6

*Design and art: W2 §6 covers the curtain, the Managers and the costumes. Two additions: the curtain is 10.50 × 16.40 m, painted in glue-based paint (*peinture à la colle*) on canvas, laid flat in a studio at the Buttes-Chaumont between late April and early May 1917 [documented: Centre Pompidou record AM 3365 P; Pompidou+ magazine, 2022]; it is Picasso's largest known painting [documented: *New York Times* ArtsBeat, 2012]. Its winged mare suckling a foal, harlequin, guitarist and dancer return to the Blue and Rose periods, while the costumes speak Cubist [documented: Pompidou+].*

### Readings and disputes
- **Did Picasso paint the curtain alone?** Cocteau wrote on the day of the premiere that those who saw Picasso "paint alone" the curtain at the Buttes-Chaumont remain amazed, "walking on the immense canvas" [documented: Cocteau, *L'Excelsior*, 18 May 1917, quoted by the Centre Pompidou]. The Pompidou itself says the curtain's history "is not as simple as the version Cocteau gives" [documented]; Balla is named as a helper [documented: Wikipedia]; other assistants are disputed (scope 04). Werner Spies argues the composition is based on a tavern scene by the little-known Achille Vianelli in Naples's San Martino museum [documented as Spies's argument: Roderick Conway Morris, 1998]. Myth of the lone genius against the workshop record.
- **What was the scandal about?** Wikipedia says much of the hostility targeted Picasso's Cubism, with cries of "sale boche" (dirty Hun) [documented]; Cocteau later said the "Battle of *Parade*" was fiercer because it coincided with Verdun [documented as Cocteau's 1953 claim: Kunstplaza]. Jacinthe Harbec ("1917. *Parade*, l'avènement du cubisme sur scène", *Nouvelle histoire de la musique en France*, 2023) quotes critics who found it a "chaotic assemblage of nonsense" [documented]. Christine Reynolds (PhD, Liverpool, 2005) reads "réaliste" as a serious aesthetic claim rather than a joke [documented in W2]. Two readings of one subtitle.
- **The libel case: who was arrested, and for how long?** Satie sent the critic Jean Poueigh a postcard: "you are an arse, an arse without music" [documented: Wikipedia; *BBC Music Magazine*/classical-music.com, 2025]. Most accounts have *Cocteau* arrested for shouting "arse" in court or slapping Poueigh's lawyer [documented: Wikipedia; classical-music.com; Interlude, 2020]; one has the police arrest *Satie* [documented: Kunstplaza, 2021]. The sentence is "a week in prison" (Interlude) or "eight days" (classical-music.com, Harbec), with 100 francs fine and 1,000 francs damages; it was suspended on condition of five years' good conduct [documented: each]. **Contested** in its details.

### Facts worth telling
1. Poueigh had congratulated Satie backstage before writing that "rattles" and "typewriters" could not supply the wit his music lacked [documented: Interlude; Harbec].
2. Satie told the Princesse de Polignac he would not give "one cent" of the damages to the critic and asked to spend her loan on living expenses [documented: Interlude].
3. Picasso met the Ballets Russes dancer Olga Khokhlova in Rome while working on *Parade*; they married on 12 July 1918 with Cocteau, Max Jacob and Apollinaire as witnesses [documented: Musée Picasso press kit, 2017]. She is described both as a "prima ballerina" and as a "third-rate but beautiful chorus dancer" [documented as the contradiction: Kunstplaza].
4. It was the company's first Paris performance since the war began [documented: Pompidou+].
5. Satie's piano score was finished on 9 January 1917, the orchestration only on 8 May, ten days before the premiere [documented: Harbec, after Orledge 1990].

### Set it beside
- `train-bleu-1924`: Cocteau's later "present tense" ballet; leisure and fashion instead of fairground and wartime noise.
- `petrushka-1911`: another fairground booth, but there the show is all inside the booth; in *Parade* the show never begins.
- `skating-rink-1922`: the rival Ballets Suédois with Léger's designs, taking the urban popular spectacle further.
- `triadic-ballet-1922`: costume as moving architecture, like Picasso's Managers, but as an abstract programme, not a satire.

## Ballet royal de la Nuit / Royal Ballet of the Night (1653)
`id: ballet-de-la-nuit-1653` · W file: W1 §2

*Design and art: W1 §2 covers the Gissey drawings and the Rising Sun costume. One addition: the BnF drawing shows the king "sparkling with gold and precious stones" in his solar costume, under a diadem topped with tall many-coloured plumes [documented: Académie de Montpellier teaching dossier, J.-M. Sanchez, 2022]; Torelli's machines made the clouds part as he appeared [documented: same].*

### Readings and disputes
- **Who designed the costumes?** The BnF's *Essentiels* page credits "costumes d'Henri Gissey" [documented]. Gabriella Asaro (*L'Histoire par l'image*, 2013) writes that "nothing allows" the costume designer to be identified and that the attribution of the Rising Sun gouache to Gissey "has been denied" (*démentie*); the drawing only "announces" his style [documented]. The BnF itself labels the sheet "Maître du Ballet royal de la nuit", an anonymous hand [documented]. **Contested**: the most reproduced image in court ballet has no secure author.
- **Who organised it?** "Sieur Clément", former intendant of the duc de Nemours (BnF; Asaro), against Louis Hesselin, *surintendant des plaisirs* (Waddesdon catalogue) [documented: W1 and both].
- **Was the "Sun King" born here?** A popular account says the nickname "was born" when Louis danced Apollo in 1653 [documented: Interlude, 2013]; the Nancy Opéra's programme says "the Sun King is born" [documented]. Historians treat the sun as one emblem among several until it became the king's personal device at the 1662 Carrousel [probable: Peter Burke, *The Fabrication of Louis XIV*, 1992]. Myth of origin against a slower process.
- **Political rite or pleasure?** Mazarin's "tool of promotion of royal power" (Sébastien Daucé, Caen dossier, 2020) [documented] against the reading that court ballet was also simply carnival entertainment, with comic and low entrées (Georgia Cowart, *The Triumph of Pleasure*, 2008, argues pleasure itself could carry dissent) [probable].

### Facts worth telling
1. The king's age: "14" (BnF) or "15" (Daucé, Caen) [documented: both]; born 5 September 1638, he was fourteen [documented in W1].
2. His roles: five (an Hour, an Ardent, a Curious one, a Furious one, the Rising Sun: Montpellier dossier) or six (adding a Game: BnF; Swiss National Museum blog, 2023) [documented: each].
3. Aurora announces him: "Le Soleil qui me suit c'est le jeune LOUIS" [documented: Asaro 2013].
4. Lully danced in it, his earliest known ballet appearance; a popular account wrongly credits him with the music [documented: BnF; Interlude].
5. Daucé rebuilt the music from the single surviving first-violin part; Francesca Lattuada's 2017 Caen staging cast a Black dancer as Louis [documented: musicologie.org, 2017; Actu-Juridique, 2020].

### Set it beside
- `balet-comique-1581`: the earlier court rite, where the king is the audience at the end of the hall; here he dances the sun himself.
- `apollon-musagete-1928`: Apollo again, as a youth learning restraint rather than a king rising in gold.
- `sleeping-beauty-1890`: a Russian court's nostalgia for Louis XIV, with a fairy tale in place of the king.
- `red-detachment-1964`: a later state's use of dance for legitimacy; the sun returns as Mao's red light.

## Balet comique de la Royne / The Queen's Comic Ballet (1581)
`id: balet-comique-1581` · W file: W1 §1

*Design and art: W1 §1 covers the hall plan and machines. One addition: Circe's garden on the raised stage had orange and lemon trees and trellises, with a castle of two crenellated towers behind; opposite were Pan's grove, a gilded vault and "a great cloud of luminous stars" [documented: Histoires de Paris, 2019, after Ludovic Celler, 1868]. Jacques Patin, the king's painter, drew and engraved twenty-seven subjects for the printed book [documented: Opéra Baroque].*

### Readings and disputes
- **Whose ballet: Louise's or Catherine's?** The title and livret say the Queen, Louise de Lorraine, commanded it [documented in W1]. Britannica places it "at the French court of Catherine de Médicis" [documented]; Opéra Baroque says Catherine gave Beaujoyeulx the charge [documented]; Alastair Macaulay (2021) calls it "a vast masquerade to disguise a sham": officially Louise's, "really" administered by Catherine, and staged for the arranged marriage of Henri III's favourite [documented]. A credit dispute over the patron, not the artist.
- **Healing rite or cover story?** Frances Yates (*The French Academies of the Sixteenth Century*, 1947) reads it as a magical-political rite of harmony for a France torn by religious war [probable, W1]. Macaulay reads it as propaganda for a court troubled by rumours about the king and his *mignons* [documented: Macaulay 2021]. A footnote by Beatriz Cotello (*Circe*, 12, 2008) adds that Catherine and her son had not been strangers to the St Bartholomew massacre less than ten years before [documented].
- **Where, and how long?** Most sources: the great hall of the Petit-Bourbon [documented: Wikipedia; Opéra Baroque]. Pierre de L'Estoile's journal says "the queen gave her feast at the Louvre, which ended with a ballet of Circe" [documented as quoted: Opéra Baroque]. A teaching blog gives "either the Louvre's Great Salle or … the Salle de Bourbon" and a length of 10 p.m. to 3 a.m. [documented: BAA Dance History, 2007]; W1 has about 3:30 a.m. [documented in W1].
- **Which Beaulieu?** Fétis named the composer "Lambert de Beaulieu", probably following an error in a letter of Rudolf II; he is now identified with the bass singer Girard de Beaulieu, who sang Glaucus with his wife, the soprano Violante Doria [documented: Wikipedia].
- **First ballet, or first opera?** Britannica: "considered the first ballet" [documented]. Opéra Baroque argues it could as well "symbolise the birth of opera", twenty years before Florence [documented]. Use "earliest known printed ballet de cour".

### Facts worth telling
1. Crowds of up to 10,000 people are said to have come to see the hall's decorations [documented as a claim: Histoires de Paris, after Celler 1868].
2. Britannica's cost of 3,600,000 gold francs is given "without clues" to its modern value [documented: Cotello 2008, n. 9].
3. The bridegroom, the duc de Joyeuse, aged twenty-one, had lost seven teeth and part of his jaw to a musket shot the year before [documented: Opéra Baroque].
4. Beaujoyeulx boasted of having pleased "in one well-proportioned body the eye, the ear and the understanding" [documented as quoted: Opéra Baroque, our translation].
5. Circe was played by "la damoiselle de Sainte-Mesme" [documented: Opéra Baroque].

### Set it beside
- `ballet-de-la-nuit-1653`: seventy years on the king no longer watches from the dais; he dances the sun.
- `medee-et-jason-1763`: the court rite of words, song and dance against Noverre's wordless *ballet d'action*.
- `red-detachment-1964`: another state spectacle in which a divided country is shown reconciled under one ruler.
- `sleeping-beauty-1890`: the imperial Russian echo of French court festivity, with the processions turned into fairy tales.

## Médée et Jason / Medea and Jason (1763)
`id: medee-et-jason-1763` · W file: W1 §3

### Design and art
W1 is about the theory; the look is barely recorded. Stuttgart scenery was by Giovan Battista Colomba and costumes by Louis-René Boquet [documented: Klassika, Rodolphe work page; Boquet probable for the first name]. The most important costume decision was a subtraction: Gaetano Vestris danced Jason without the customary mask, so his face could act [documented: Klassika; consistent with Noverre's "quittez ces masques froids", W1]. The 1763 printed scenario lists allegorical roles danced as persons, Fire, Iron, Poison, Vengeance, Hatred and Jealousy, each needing a readable costume [documented: Library of Congress, *Didon abandonnée* libretto, Stuttgart 1763, pp. 75–89]. The London 1781 revival had scenery and costumes by Michael Novosielski [documented: V&A print record E.2836-1962]. Modern stagings rebuilt the look from Boquet's drawings: Ivo Cramer's for the Ballet du Rhin (1992), designed by Dominique Delouche [documented: *Nouvel Obs*, 2012]. The best-known image, the Bartolozzi aquatint after Nathaniel Dance (1781), is a satire on tragic poses, not a record [documented: V&A; Moira Goff, *Dance in History*, 2018].

### Readings and disputes
- **Who invented the *ballet d'action*?** Noverre claimed it. Gasparo Angiolini named his teacher Franz Hilverding, or himself, as the true inventor; the Wienbibliothek's curator (Reinhard Buchberger, 2025) says the feud "can hardly be decided" today and points to a parallel genesis "in the air" of the Enlightenment [documented]. Britannica traces the genre "at least to 1717" with John Weaver's *The Loves of Mars and Venus* [documented]; the *Nouvel Obs* (2012) says Noverre "was not quite a precursor" but the theorist [documented]. **Contested**, with three national claims.
- **Who owned the choreography?** Vestris, the first Jason, staged it in Paris four months later, in June 1763; the APGRD, after Ivor Guest (*The Ballet of the Enlightenment*, 1996, app. B), notes it "is suggested he 'stole' Noverre's choreography and passed [it] off as his own" [documented]. In London (1781) the V&A says he "revived [it] as his own work" [documented]. An early case of a dancer as carrier, and taker, of a work.
- **Pantomime or spectacle?** Modern commentary stresses expressive mime as Noverre's innovation; Moira Goff (2018) shows that the 1780 scenario and the London productions are full of fire, storms and flying chariots, and that a 1790 Royal Circus version sold it as a "Beautiful Spectacle" [documented]. Iris Julia Bührle reads it as a translation of Corneille into wordless drama [documented in W1].

### Facts worth telling
1. It premiered on 11 February 1763, the birthday of Duke Carl Eugen of Württemberg, who paid for Noverre's company [documented: Klassika].
2. It was an interlude in an opera, about 25 minutes long [documented: *Nouvel Obs*]; in Stuttgart it followed Act I of Jommelli's *Didone abbandonata* [documented: LoC libretto record; composer probable].
3. Madame Noverre danced the children's Governess; the two children were "les petits Delaître" [documented: LoC].
4. The 1790 Royal Circus playbill claimed "original Music composed by GLUCK"; the score is by Jean-Joseph Rodolphe [documented: Goff 2018; Wienbibliothek].
5. Nothing of Noverre's choreography survives except the scenario, notes on some gestures and testimonies; Vestris's own notes are said to lie in the Bibliothèque de l'Opéra [documented as claimed: *Nouvel Obs*].

### Set it beside
- `balet-comique-1581`: the court ballet of sung verse and allegory that Noverre's wordless drama replaced.
- `giselle-1841`: a heroine destroyed by betrayal; the mad scene is Romantic ballet's version of Medea's fury turned inward.
- `spartacus-1968`: another tragic *ballet d'action* on a classical subject, with mime replaced by heroic dance.
- `fille-mal-gardee-1789`: Dauberval, Noverre's pupil, takes the action ballet into a farmyard comedy.

## Le Train Bleu / The Blue Train (1924)
`id: train-bleu-1924` · W file: W2 §8

*Design and art: W2 §8 covers Laurens's beach, Chanel's knitwear and the Picasso front cloth in detail. Additions: Cocteau's unpublished manuscript (Feb–Mar 1924) asks for two raised bathing cabins numbered 1 and 2 with hatches for faces and arms, a diving board into an unseen sea, and a straw hat on an invisible thread [documented: *Cahiers Jean Cocteau*, 2021]; the front cloth weighs about 350 kg [documented: V&A O171462].*

### Readings and disputes
- **Whose ballet: Cocteau's or Nijinska's?** The V&A says Nijinska "was not entirely happy" with Cocteau, who "tried to dominate the creative process and add details"; after "a disastrous dress rehearsal" it was "reworked and tightened" [documented: V&A Sasha photograph S.302-2017]. Lynn Garafola (*La Nijinska*, 2022) describes her "mulish determination to ignore the high life details knitted into Cocteau's imagined ballet", which the *Nation* review reads as politically motivated [documented: *The Nation*, 2022]. A Brazilian study (*Revista Brasileira de Ciências do Esporte* 30/3, 2009) frames the tension as gendered power, "a woman and a man in command, who should prevail?" [documented]. Satire of the rich (Cocteau) or a sport-and-new-woman ballet (Nijinska)?
- **Did Picasso design it?** The same Brazilian article credits "scenery by Pablo Picasso and Henri Laurens" [documented]; the Paris Opéra's *Picasso and Dance* exhibition says his involvement was "limited to just a stage curtain, or instructions for making one" [documented]; the V&A says the cloth was attached to the ballet only in London in November 1924 [documented in W2]. The famous image is the least integral part.
- **Costume as fashion or as dancewear?** The Thyssen says Chanel's sportswear caused "practical problems as they were not specifically intended for dancing" [documented]; Sokolova: "my woollen costume was impossible to grip" when Woizikovsky threw and caught her [documented: V&A Perlouse costume O1115812]. Beaumont found them "uninteresting" [documented in W2]; the V&A curator notes the gigolos' navy knit resembles the "vest and pants look" of 1990s choreographers [documented: V&A O1115811].

### Facts worth telling
1. Sokolova's costume is cyclamen-pink hand-knitted wool with black and white bands; the chorus wore machine knits [documented: V&A].
2. André Messager, the operetta composer, conducted the premiere [documented: *Cahiers Jean Cocteau*].
3. Cocteau's draft marks the overture "genre Beethoven" [documented: *Cahiers Jean Cocteau*].
4. Sasha's London photograph shows the "uncomfortable bathing shoes" chosen with the costume [documented: V&A S.302-2017].
5. Nijinska had already made a *Sports and Touring Ballet Revue* (1922) for her own company [documented: RBCE 2009].

### Set it beside
- `parade-1917`: Cocteau's first ballet of the present tense: fairground and war instead of beach and Olympics.
- `skating-rink-1922`: the Ballets Suédois' sport ballet, with Léger's designs; sport as mass rhythm rather than chic flirtation.
- `les-noces-1923`: Nijinska a year earlier, stripping colour from a peasant wedding; here she strips luxury from the Riviera.
- `jeux-1913`: Nijinsky's tennis ballet; the brother's erotic game of three becomes the sister's comedy of types.

## Красный мак / The Red Poppy (1927)
`id: red-poppy-1927` · W file: W2 §10a

*Design and art: W2 notes Kurilko as designer; his designs are at the Bakhrushin. Not found in this pass: a description of the 1927 palette. One detail from the score: Glière marked the opening "lifeless China", set to gongs [documented: China Channel, *LARB*, 2017].*

### Readings and disputes
- **"The first Soviet ballet": fact or myth?** Soviet tradition repeats the title [probable, W2]. An article on the Academy of Music site ("The Quart Leap: The Invention of Soviet Ballet and Glière's *The Red Poppy*", 2022) treats its history as "a myth of the miraculous attainment of an 'authentic-Soviet ballet'", and argues its authors chose "a radically conservative method", Petipa's form with a new flag [documented: abstract]. Lopukhov's experiments of the early 1920s are the counter-claim [probable].
- **Who had the idea?** Kurilko said he saw one *Pravda* item about a Soviet ship detained in China; in truth "he conflated two unrelated news stories into one" [documented: China Channel 2017]. W2 records the rival contest story (Glière). Kurilko's own 1952 memoir of the ballet's origin is catalogued in the Russian State Catalogue [documented: reference list, "The Quart Leap"]; written after his Stalin Prize, it is a late source.
- **Liberation or condescension?** For the Bolshoi it was a story of Soviet heroes freeing China; China Channel reads it as a Soviet vision "in which … the Chinese were downtrodden victims in need of saving" [documented]. When Mao was in Moscow in 1949–50 he declined to see it and sent Chen Boda and others [documented: China Channel]; the 1957 retitling as *The Red Flower* and the cut pigtail followed Chinese objections [documented in W2].

### Facts worth telling
1. It was given across the USSR some 3,000 times before fading in the 1930s [documented as claimed: China Channel].
2. The 1949 Bolshoi revival also marked Stalin's seventieth birthday [documented: China Channel].
3. The ending rains red poppies onto the stage as a vision of liberation [documented: China Channel].
4. The Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo's 1943 version made the villain Japanese, to suit the wartime alliance [documented in W2].
5. The printed libretto of 1929 runs to three acts, eight scenes "with apotheosis" [documented: "The Quart Leap" references].

### Set it beside
- `bayadere-1877`: the imperial exotic multi-act ballet whose structure it kept, with an opium dream again.
- `red-detachment-1964`: the Chinese answer, a revolutionary ballet made by Chinese makers in which the Soviets do not appear.
- `scheherazade-1910`: the Orient sold to Paris for pleasure; here the Orient is sold to Moscow for politics.

## Пламя Парижа / The Flames of Paris (1932)
`id: flames-of-paris-1932` · W file: W2 §10b

*Design and art: Vladimir Dmitriev designed sets and costumes and co-wrote the libretto [documented: People's World, 2014]; W2 has little more. Ratmansky's 2008 sets by Ilya Utkin and Evgeny Monakhov were "inspired by contemporary engravings", with costumes by Elena Markovskaya [documented: Marc Haegeman, *Dance Magazine*, 2008; Bolshoi credits].*

### Readings and disputes
- **Stalin's favourite?** It is "allegedly a favorite of Stalin's" (Haegeman 2008) or "purported" to be (Eric A. Gordon, *People's World*, 2014) [documented: both]; Ismene Brown says Vainonen's ensembles "whipped up the packed audiences and Josef Stalin himself" [documented: *The Arts Desk*, 2013]. No primary record of Stalin's attendance was seen in this pass. **Probable**, widely repeated.
- **How much survives?** "All but about twenty-five minutes of the choreography was lost" (*The New Yorker*, 2018) [documented]; Ratmansky said there is a film, part of which he found "too late" [documented: Brown 2013]. Mikhail Messerer's 2013 Mikhailovsky version was rebuilt "from memory, photographs and film" of Vainonen's 1947 revision [documented: People's World]. So two "authentic" versions exist, both partly invented.
- **Who starred?** W2 gives Dudinskaya as Mireille in 1932; *The Arts Desk* says the 1932 creation starred Galina Ulanova, "utterly bewildered by her role as the actress Mireille" [documented: both]. Ulanova may have taken the role later; **contested** as phrased.
- **Ratmansky's reversal.** Not a reconstruction but "a dialogue with Vainonen" (Ratmansky, in Haegeman 2008) [documented]; Jann Parry compares it to Bourne's *Swan Lake*, a commentary with a tragic end [documented: DanceTabs, 2013]. A Moscow critic told him his past and the Bolshoi's "could never meet" [documented: Brown 2013].

### Facts worth telling
1. Cyril Beaumont's synopsis of the original runs to eight pages [documented: Brown 2013].
2. Asafiev's score also lifts court music by Lully and Grétry for the aristocrats [documented: Gordon 2014].
3. Ratmansky cut four acts to two and added Adeline, an aristocrat guillotined before her friends [documented: Haegeman 2008].
4. Ratmansky admired Vainonen's folk phrases: "Simple and great. You can't imagine anything else" [documented: Brown 2013].
5. It took 82 years to reach the United States, with the Mikhailovsky in 2014 [documented: Gordon 2014].

### Set it beside
- `swan-lake-bourne-1995`: Parry's comparison; a remake that comments on its source and darkens the end.
- `spartacus-1968`: the later Soviet heroic ballet, where the collective hero becomes one muscular man.
- `medee-et-jason-1763`: the *ballet d'action* principle that every dance is motivated, pushed here into a drama theatre's method.

## 红色娘子军 / The Red Detachment of Women (1964)
`id: red-detachment-1964` · W file: W3 §1

*Design and art: W3 §1 covers the look. One addition: the costumes are military and the props "overwhelmingly martial: pistols, rifles, knives, machetes" [documented: Antonia Finnane, *The Conversation*, 16 Feb 2017]; the image the West remembers is "arabesques and pirouettes performed en pointe with rifles cocked" [documented: Alex Colville, *The China Project*, 2021].*

### Readings and disputes
- **Whose ballet?** When Nixon asked Jiang Qing in 1972 for the names of the writer, composer and director, she said it was "created by the masses" [documented: Kristine Harris, "Re-makes/Re-models: *The Red Detachment of Women* between Stage and Screen", *Opera Quarterly* 26.2–3, 2010]. Harris notes Jiang had "so publicly insinuated herself" into it that hers was the name most linked to it [documented]. A Springer reference entry (*Contextual Dictionary of Chinese Cultural Knowledge*, 2026) says instead that its creation "was proposed by Premier Zhou Enlai in 1963" and done "under the direct supervision of Premier Zhou Enlai" [documented: preview]. The credited collective, the patron Jiang and the patron Zhou: three authorships, each politically useful in its decade. **Contested.**
- **Why so many guns?** Finnane argues the arsenal reflects not the 1930s but 1964: split with Moscow, war with India, no US recognition, and a premiere "a week before China's first successful atomic test" [documented: *The Conversation* 2017]. An argued reading of design as geopolitics.
- **Did ballet crush Chinese dance?** Citing Emily Wilcox (*Revolutionary Bodies*, 2019), Colville writes that "at the whim of one woman" ballet "had suffocated a national dance culture", and quotes Wang Kefen and Long Yinpei on a stage where "ballet was the single blossoming flower" [documented: *The China Project* 2021]. Wilcox's book argues the model ballets were an exception within a longer socialist dance tradition built on Chinese forms [probable].
- **Revival as celebration or wound?** The 2017 Melbourne performances drew demonstrators from part of the Chinese-Australian community [documented: Finnane, *Asia Times* version, 2017].

### Facts worth telling
1. Nixon called it a "musical" [documented: Harris 2010].
2. The original cast underwent military training to handle the weapons convincingly [documented: *New York Times*, 2 July 2015].
3. The heroine Wu Qionghua was renamed Qinghua during the Cultural Revolution [documented: Finnane 2017].
4. Gough Whitlam saw it in 1971 and in 1973 chose *The White-Haired Girl* instead [documented: Finnane 2017].
5. It is a focal point of Act II of John Adams and Alice Goodman's opera *Nixon in China* (1987) [documented: Harris 2010].
