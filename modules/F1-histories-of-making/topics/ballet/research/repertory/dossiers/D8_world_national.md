## La Coronela / The Colonel's Wife (1940)
`id: la-coronela-1940` · threads: T08, T14, T18, T24 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 23 November 1940, Palacio de Bellas Artes, Mexico City, Ballet de Bellas Artes (repeated 26 and 30 November, per the programme cited by Tortajada 2004) [documented]. Choreography: Waldeen (Waldeen von Falkenstein Brooke, 1913–1993), US-born dancer and teacher [documented, INBA digital]. Music: Silvestre Revueltas, composed for piano and left unfinished at his death; fourth episode composed by Blas Galindo; orchestration by Candelario Huízar; conductor Eduardo Hernández Moncada [documented, Biblioteca Digital Silvestre Revueltas, UNAM]. Scenario: Waldeen, Gabriel Fernández Ledesma and Seki Sano, "inspired by twelve engravings by José Guadalupe Posada"; spoken-choir verse by Efraín Huerta [documented, UNAM]. Sets and costumes: Gabriel Fernández Ledesma; masks: Germán Cueto; stage and dramatic direction (spoken choirs): Seki Sano [documented, UNAM]. One popular history credits the scenery to the Taller de Gráfica Popular (Luis Arenal, Leopoldo Méndez, Pablo O'Higgins) [contested, see Readings]. Four episodes, about 19 minutes of Revueltas's music [documented, UNAM]. The version seen today: concert performances and recordings of the 1957–62 Limantour/Hernández Moncada score; the dance survives through a 1986 reconstruction (UTEC/INBA video) [documented, Reynoso 2012].

### In one line
A modern-dance ballet that turns Posada's skeleton prints of the old dictatorship into a revolution led by a woman, the Coronela.

### How it came to be
In 1939 Celestino Gorostiza, head of the Department of Fine Arts, named Waldeen artistic director of the state's new Ballet de Bellas Artes [documented, Ramos Villalobos thesis, UNAM 2010]. She had trained in Russian ballet with Theodore Kosloff in Los Angeles and then in German expressive dance [documented, Dallal, Material de Lectura UNAM; Reynoso 2012]. Her dancers were young students of the National School of Dance: Guillermina Bravo, Lourdes Campos, Amalia Hernández and Josefina Lavalle among them [documented, UNAM 2010 thesis]. The company set itself a programme: a dance "national in spirit and form, but universal in its reach" [documented, 1940 programme quoted by Tortajada 2004].

The project for a Posada ballet was signed on 7 June 1940 [documented, es.wikipedia citing the contract]. Waldeen invited Revueltas by letter. He composed at the piano from 11 July to 5 October 1940, playing each character's theme for Waldeen, who tried it in the studio the next day [documented, Contreras Soto in *Silvestre Revueltas en escena y en pantalla*, INBA; Lavalle quoted on es.wikipedia]. He was paid 700 pesos [documented, es.wikipedia]. Revueltas died in October 1940, aged forty, with three episodes written and none orchestrated [documented, UNAM; UNM 2013]. Blas Galindo wrote the "Last Judgement" and Huízar orchestrated the whole in weeks; Galindo wrote to a colleague on 20 November, three days before the opening [documented, Contreras Soto citing the letter].

### Roots and reference points
- **Posada's prints.** José Guadalupe Posada (1852–1913) made broadsheet engravings for the Mexico City printer Vanegas Arroyo, including the *calaveras* (skeletons dressed as the living) [probable]. In the 1920s and 1930s the muralists and the print collective around Leopoldo Méndez had made Posada the ancestor of a people's art [argued, es.wikipedia calls him "precursor of pictorial nationalism"]. The ballet takes "twelve engravings" as its scenario [documented, UNAM].
- **"La Coronela".** The title figure is a *soldadera*, the women who fought and followed the armies of 1910–20 [probable]. Don Ferruco is a stock figure of the dandy in Posada's broadsheets [probable].
- **Workers' theatre.** Seki Sano, a Japanese director exiled from Japan and the USSR, brought "spoken choirs, pantomime and other resources of modern stage technique unknown in Mexico" [documented, 1940 programme quoted by Contreras Soto]. The spoken choir came from the left-wing workers' theatres of New York and Berlin [probable, Tortajada cites Prickett 1990 on the New Theatre movement].
- **Earlier Mexican nationalist dance.** Nellie and Gloria Campobello's *Ballet Simbólico 30-30* (1931) had already staged the Revolution [documented, Reynoso 2012; SciELO review]. Anna Pavlova's *Fantasía mexicana* (1919), with its *china poblana*, was the exotic version that Mexican dance wanted to replace [argued, Reynoso 2012].

### The world it built
Four tableaux, not a story [documented, Lugo Viñas 2020]:
1. *Damitas de aquellos tiempos* (Young Ladies of Those Times): the society of Porfirio Díaz's dictatorship, in waltz and quadrille rhythms.
2. *Danza de los desheredados* (Dance of the Disinherited): the injustice suffered by peasant men and women, and their revolt, with Huerta's spoken chorus.
3. *La pesadilla de don Ferruco* (Don Ferruco's Nightmare): a party that turns sour; the Coronela appears as "the incarnation of the revolutionary spirit".
4. *Juicio final* (Last Judgement): the "social sinners" are sent to hell and the Coronela triumphs [documented, Tortajada 2004].

The movement mixed ballet's leg extensions, *attitudes* and *développés* with steps from Mexican traditional dances and with abstract modern movement [documented, Reynoso 2012, from the 1986 reconstruction]. The music, in the concert arrangement, sets a steady waltz against a Mexican song that slowly takes over, then gives the Coronela a military theme with trumpet calls [documented, Naxos notes quoted by UNM 2013]. Because Revueltas composed theme by theme for each character, the score works like a cast list [argued, after Lavalle].

### Design and art
Fernández Ledesma, a painter and printmaker who had worked on *Forma* magazine and on popular-art exhibitions, researched the period for the costumes [probable; Lugo Viñas credits him with "artistic and historical research", documented]. The peasant women wore a plain blouse and a long, wide dark-blue skirt [documented for the 1986 reconstruction, Reynoso]. Germán Cueto, a sculptor of the Estridentista avant-garde, made masks, so that some figures appeared as Posada's skulls in three dimensions [probable]. Germán Horacio's rehearsal drawings were printed in the magazine *Hoy* in November 1940 [documented, Reynoso fig. 18]. The look was a black-and-white print brought to life: the broadsheet, not the painted mural, was the model [argued].

### Premiere and reception
The program of 23 November 1940 also included Waldeen's *Seis danzas clásicas*, *Danza de las fuerzas nuevas* and *Procesional* [documented, Tortajada 2004]. The press greeted "a new dance": one headline read "La Coronela by Waldeen triumphed at Bellas Artes" (Luis Bruno Ruiz, *Excélsior*) [documented, UNAM bibliography]. Critics Jesús Bal y Gay and Sonia Verbitzki reviewed it in *El Universal* in December 1940 [documented, UNAM]. The work opened in the last days of Lázaro Cárdenas's presidency; with the new government the group left the state and became the Ballet del Teatro de las Artes, touring US universities in 1941 [documented, INBA page on Guillermina Bravo]. Audience figures and costs: not found in this pass.

### Versions and lineage
- 1940: Huízar/Galindo orchestration, lost after the premiere run [documented, UNAM].
- 1949: revived; Pablo Moncayo apparently reconstructed the music [documented as "apparently", UNAM].
- 1957–62: conductor José Yves Limantour asked Hernández Moncada for a new orchestration and completed it with music from Revueltas's film scores *Vámonos con Pancho Villa* and *Los de abajo*; revived at Bellas Artes in 1962. This is the score used for later revivals and recordings [documented, UNAM; Lugo Viñas].
- 1986: reconstruction filmed by UTEC with INBA [documented, Reynoso].
- 2001: documentary *La Coronela (1940): Punto de partida* (Lavalle, Cobo, Roca; 50 min) [documented, INBA digital].
No dance notation is known: not found in this pass.

### Readings and disputes
- **A founding work.** Josefina Lavalle (*En busca de la danza moderna mexicana*) and the INBA documentary treat it as the "watershed" that began Mexican nationalist modern dance [documented, INBA digital abstract]. Jose Luis Reynoso (*Choreographing Politics, Dancing Modernity*, UCLA PhD, 2012) reads it as both point of departure and point of culmination, an "indigenized, mestizo universalism" that claimed Mexico's revolution as a model for any people [documented].
- **A woman's revolution.** Margarita Tortajada (*Mujeres de danza combativa*, 1998; "Casa del Tiempo", 2004) argues it "recovered woman as the centre of history" [documented, quoted by Lugo Viñas]. William Beezley (*Cultural Nationalism and Ethnic Music in Latin America*, 2018) calls it a "musical analysis of the revolution" with an early feminist view of women [documented, Chamosa review 2021].
- **Imported or native?** A critical history (SciELO, *Revista Brasileira de Estudos da Presença*) questions the story that Mexican modern dance "sprang" from two US women (Waldeen and Anna Sokolow) arriving in 1939, pointing to the Campobellos and the state school [documented]. Both readings agree the dancers were Mexican-trained.
- **Contradictions.** Sets: Fernández Ledesma (UNAM catalogue, programme) or the Taller de Gráfica Popular (Lugo Viñas 2020) [contested]. Revival year: 1957 (orchestration) or 1962 (stage) [both documented, different events]. The concert title is translated both "The Colonel's Wife" (register) and "The Lady Colonel" (Naxos); the Spanish means a woman who is herself a colonel [argued: "The Lady Colonel" is closer].

### Facts worth telling
- Revueltas wrote the music in under three months and died before orchestrating a note [documented, Contreras Soto].
- He was paid 700 pesos [documented, es.wikipedia].
- The orchestration used on the opening night was lost straight after it [documented, UNAM].
- The score heard today contains music from two Revolution films [documented, UNAM].
- A Japanese theatre director, Seki Sano, staged the spoken choirs [documented].
- Its first dancers included Amalia Hernández, later founder of the Ballet Folklórico de México [documented, UNAM 2010].
- The scenario was registered with the Ministry of Education on 22 February 1941 [documented, Archivo General de la Nación via UNAM].

### Set it beside
- `red-poppy-1927`: Moscow's revolution ballet uses classical technique and an exotic China; Mexico makes its revolution from its own prints.
- `white-haired-girl-matsuyama-1955`: another revolution of the poor told through a woman, made by a small group against the mainstream.
- `rodeo-1942`: two years later another woman builds a national dance from folk material in the US, but around a romance, not a revolt.
- `frida-lopez-ochoa-2020`: Mexico's visual art again as ballet scenario, eighty years on.

### What a maker can take from it
- A world can be built from one artist's images: twelve prints gave characters, costume and tone. Choose a visual source and let it set the rules.
- Compose with the dancers in the room: Revueltas wrote a theme a day and Waldeen tested it the next, so music and character grew together.
- Plan for loss. The score, the orchestration and the steps were each lost or rebuilt; a world that matters needs its own archive.

### Beyond the stage
The concert suite is recorded (for example by Naxos) and played with projected Posada prints (University of New Mexico, 2013) [documented]. Posada's Catrina skeleton became a global image through Diego Rivera's mural and later film, a separate path from the ballet [probable].
