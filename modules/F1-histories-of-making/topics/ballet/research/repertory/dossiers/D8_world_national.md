## La Coronela / The Colonel's Wife (1940)
`id: la-coronela-1940` · threads: T08, T14, T18, T24 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 23 November 1940, Palacio de Bellas Artes, Mexico City, Ballet de Bellas Artes (repeated 26 and 30 November, per the programme cited by Tortajada 2004) [documented]. Choreography: Waldeen (Waldeen von Falkenstein Brooke, 1913–1993), US-born dancer and teacher [documented, INBA digital]. Music: Silvestre Revueltas, composed for piano and left unfinished at his death; fourth episode composed by Blas Galindo; orchestration by Candelario Huízar; conductor Eduardo Hernández Moncada [documented, Biblioteca Digital Silvestre Revueltas, UNAM]. Scenario: Waldeen, Gabriel Fernández Ledesma and Seki Sano, "inspired by twelve engravings by José Guadalupe Posada"; spoken-choir verse by Efraín Huerta [documented, UNAM]. Sets and costumes: Gabriel Fernández Ledesma; masks: Germán Cueto; stage and dramatic direction (spoken choirs): Seki Sano [documented, UNAM]. One popular history credits the scenery to the Taller de Gráfica Popular (Luis Arenal, Leopoldo Méndez, Pablo O'Higgins) [contested, see Readings]. Four episodes, about 19 minutes of Revueltas's music [documented, UNAM]. The version seen today: concert performances and recordings of the 1957–62 Limantour/Hernández Moncada score; the dance survives through a 1986 reconstruction (UTEC/INBA video) [documented, Reynoso 2012].

### In one line
A modern-dance ballet that turns Posada's skeleton prints of the old dictatorship into a revolution led by a woman, the Coronela.

### How it came to be
In 1939 Celestino Gorostiza, head of the Department of Fine Arts, named Waldeen artistic director of the state's new Ballet de Bellas Artes [documented, Ramos Villalobos thesis, UNAM 2010]. She had trained in Russian ballet with Theodore Kosloff in Los Angeles and then in German expressive dance [documented, Dallal, Material de Lectura UNAM; Reynoso 2012]. Her dancers were young students of the National School of Dance: Guillermina Bravo, Lourdes Campos, Amalia Hernández and Josefina Lavalle among them [documented, UNAM 2010 thesis]. The company set itself a programme: a dance "national in spirit and form, but universal in its reach" [documented, 1940 programme quoted by Tortajada 2004].

The project for a Posada ballet was signed on 7 June 1940 [documented, es.wikipedia citing the contract]. Waldeen invited Revueltas by letter. He composed at the piano from 11 July to 5 October 1940, playing each character's theme for Waldeen, who tried it in the studio the next day [documented, Contreras Soto in *Silvestre Revueltas en escena y en pantalla*, INBA; Lavalle quoted on es.wikipedia]. He was paid 700 pesos [documented, es.wikipedia]. Revueltas died in October 1940, aged forty, with three episodes written and none orchestrated [documented, UNAM; UNM 2013]. Blas Galindo wrote the "Last Judgement" and Huízar orchestrated the whole in weeks; Galindo wrote to a colleague on 20 November, three days before the opening [documented, Contreras Soto citing the letter].

### Roots and reference points
- **Posada's prints.** José Guadalupe Posada (1852–1913) made broadsheet engravings for the Mexico City printer Vanegas Arroyo, including the *calaveras* (skeletons dressed as the living) [probable]. In the 1920s and 1930s the muralists and the print collective around Leopoldo Méndez had made Posada the ancestor of a people's art [argued, es.wikipedia calls him "precursor of pictorial nationalism"]. The ballet takes "twelve engravings" as its scenario [documented, UNAM].
- **"La Coronela".** The title figure is a *soldadera*, the women who fought and followed the armies of 1910–20 [probable]. Don Ferruco is a stock figure of the dandy in Posada's broadsheets [probable].
- **Workers' theatre.** Seki Sano, a Japanese director exiled from Japan and the USSR, brought "spoken choirs, pantomime and other resources of modern stage technique unknown in Mexico" [documented, 1940 programme quoted by Contreras Soto]. The spoken choir came from the left-wing workers' theatres of New York and Berlin [probable, Tortajada cites Prickett 1990 on the New Theatre movement].
- **Earlier Mexican nationalist dance.** Nellie and Gloria Campobello's *Ballet Simbólico 30-30* (1931) had already staged the Revolution [documented, Reynoso 2012; SciELO review]. Anna Pavlova's *Fantasía mexicana* (1919), with its *china poblana*, was the exotic version that Mexican dance wanted to replace [argued, Reynoso 2012].

### The world it built
Four tableaux, not a story [documented, Lugo Viñas 2020]:
1. *Damitas de aquellos tiempos* (Young Ladies of Those Times): the society of Porfirio Díaz's dictatorship, in waltz and quadrille rhythms.
2. *Danza de los desheredados* (Dance of the Disinherited): the injustice suffered by peasant men and women, and their revolt, with Huerta's spoken chorus.
3. *La pesadilla de don Ferruco* (Don Ferruco's Nightmare): a party that turns sour; the Coronela appears as "the incarnation of the revolutionary spirit".
4. *Juicio final* (Last Judgement): the "social sinners" are sent to hell and the Coronela triumphs [documented, Tortajada 2004].

The movement mixed ballet's leg extensions, *attitudes* and *développés* with steps from Mexican traditional dances and with abstract modern movement [documented, Reynoso 2012, from the 1986 reconstruction]. The music, in the concert arrangement, sets a steady waltz against a Mexican song that slowly takes over, then gives the Coronela a military theme with trumpet calls [documented, Naxos notes quoted by UNM 2013]. Because Revueltas composed theme by theme for each character, the score works like a cast list [argued, after Lavalle].

### Design and art
Fernández Ledesma, a painter and printmaker who had worked on *Forma* magazine and on popular-art exhibitions, researched the period for the costumes [probable; Lugo Viñas credits him with "artistic and historical research", documented]. The peasant women wore a plain blouse and a long, wide dark-blue skirt [documented for the 1986 reconstruction, Reynoso]. Germán Cueto, a sculptor of the Estridentista avant-garde, made masks, so that some figures appeared as Posada's skulls in three dimensions [probable]. Germán Horacio's rehearsal drawings were printed in the magazine *Hoy* in November 1940 [documented, Reynoso fig. 18]. The look was a black-and-white print brought to life: the broadsheet, not the painted mural, was the model [argued].

### Premiere and reception
The program of 23 November 1940 also included Waldeen's *Seis danzas clásicas*, *Danza de las fuerzas nuevas* and *Procesional* [documented, Tortajada 2004]. The press greeted "a new dance": one headline read "La Coronela by Waldeen triumphed at Bellas Artes" (Luis Bruno Ruiz, *Excélsior*) [documented, UNAM bibliography]. Critics Jesús Bal y Gay and Sonia Verbitzki reviewed it in *El Universal* in December 1940 [documented, UNAM]. The work opened in the last days of Lázaro Cárdenas's presidency; with the new government the group left the state and became the Ballet del Teatro de las Artes, touring US universities in 1941 [documented, INBA page on Guillermina Bravo]. Audience figures and costs: not found in this pass.

### Versions and lineage
- 1940: Huízar/Galindo orchestration, lost after the premiere run [documented, UNAM].
- 1949: revived; Pablo Moncayo apparently reconstructed the music [documented as "apparently", UNAM].
- 1957–62: conductor José Yves Limantour asked Hernández Moncada for a new orchestration and completed it with music from Revueltas's film scores *Vámonos con Pancho Villa* and *Los de abajo*; revived at Bellas Artes in 1962. This is the score used for later revivals and recordings [documented, UNAM; Lugo Viñas].
- 1986: reconstruction filmed by UTEC with INBA [documented, Reynoso].
- 2001: documentary *La Coronela (1940): Punto de partida* (Lavalle, Cobo, Roca; 50 min) [documented, INBA digital].
No dance notation is known: not found in this pass.

### Readings and disputes
- **A founding work.** Josefina Lavalle (*En busca de la danza moderna mexicana*) and the INBA documentary treat it as the "watershed" that began Mexican nationalist modern dance [documented, INBA digital abstract]. Jose Luis Reynoso (*Choreographing Politics, Dancing Modernity*, UCLA PhD, 2012) reads it as both point of departure and point of culmination, an "indigenized, mestizo universalism" that claimed Mexico's revolution as a model for any people [documented].
- **A woman's revolution.** Margarita Tortajada (*Mujeres de danza combativa*, 1998; "Casa del Tiempo", 2004) argues it "recovered woman as the centre of history" [documented, quoted by Lugo Viñas]. William Beezley (*Cultural Nationalism and Ethnic Music in Latin America*, 2018) calls it a "musical analysis of the revolution" with an early feminist view of women [documented, Chamosa review 2021].
- **Imported or native?** A critical history (SciELO, *Revista Brasileira de Estudos da Presença*) questions the story that Mexican modern dance "sprang" from two US women (Waldeen and Anna Sokolow) arriving in 1939, pointing to the Campobellos and the state school [documented]. Both readings agree the dancers were Mexican-trained.
- **Contradictions.** Sets: Fernández Ledesma (UNAM catalogue, programme) or the Taller de Gráfica Popular (Lugo Viñas 2020) [contested]. Revival year: 1957 (orchestration) or 1962 (stage) [both documented, different events]. The concert title is translated both "The Colonel's Wife" (register) and "The Lady Colonel" (Naxos); the Spanish means a woman who is herself a colonel [argued: "The Lady Colonel" is closer].

### Facts worth telling
- Revueltas wrote the music in under three months and died before orchestrating a note [documented, Contreras Soto].
- He was paid 700 pesos [documented, es.wikipedia].
- The orchestration used on the opening night was lost straight after it [documented, UNAM].
- The score heard today contains music from two Revolution films [documented, UNAM].
- A Japanese theatre director, Seki Sano, staged the spoken choirs [documented].
- Its first dancers included Amalia Hernández, later founder of the Ballet Folklórico de México [documented, UNAM 2010].
- The scenario was registered with the Ministry of Education on 22 February 1941 [documented, Archivo General de la Nación via UNAM].

### Set it beside
- `red-poppy-1927`: Moscow's revolution ballet uses classical technique and an exotic China; Mexico makes its revolution from its own prints.
- `white-haired-girl-matsuyama-1955`: another revolution of the poor told through a woman, made by a small group against the mainstream.
- `rodeo-1942`: two years later another woman builds a national dance from folk material in the US, but around a romance, not a revolt.
- `frida-lopez-ochoa-2020`: Mexico's visual art again as ballet scenario, eighty years on.

### What a maker can take from it
- A world can be built from one artist's images: twelve prints gave characters, costume and tone. Choose a visual source and let it set the rules.
- Compose with the dancers in the room: Revueltas wrote a theme a day and Waldeen tested it the next, so music and character grew together.
- Plan for loss. The score, the orchestration and the steps were each lost or rebuilt; a world that matters needs its own archive.

### Beyond the stage
The concert suite is recorded (for example by Naxos) and played with projected Posada prints (University of New Mexico, 2013) [documented]. Posada's Catrina skeleton became a global image through Diego Rivera's mural and later film, a separate path from the ballet [probable].

## Estancia / Estancia (1952)
`id: estancia-1952` · threads: T14, T07, T11 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 19 August 1952, Teatro Colón, Buenos Aires, Cuerpo de Baile Estable del Teatro Colón; conductor Juan Emilio Martini [documented, IRCAM; Signorelli 2014 citing the programme]. Choreography: Michel Borowski (also spelled Borovsky), Warsaw-trained former Ballets Russes dancer, Argentine citizen from 1935 [documented, Signorelli 2014]. Music and scenario: Alberto Ginastera, Op. 8, composed 1941, with recited and sung lines from José Hernández's poem *Martín Fierro* (1872) for solo baritone [documented, IRCAM; Library of Congress]. Sets and costumes: Dante Ortolani [documented, Signorelli 2014]. One act, five tableaux, about 30 to 35 minutes [documented, IRCAM; NYCB]. Leads: Esmeralda Agoglia (the country girl), Enrique Lommi (the city boy) [documented]. The version most often seen today: Christopher Wheeldon's for New York City Ballet (29 May 2010), sets by Santiago Calatrava [documented, NYCB]; the music is heard far more often as the four-dance concert suite, Op. 8a [documented, Britannica].

### In one line
One day on an Argentine ranch, from dawn to dawn, in which a city boy earns a country girl and ends dancing the gauchos' malambo.

### How it came to be
In 1941 the US government's Office of Inter-American Affairs, run by Nelson Rockefeller, paid for American Ballet Caravan to tour South America as wartime goodwill against fascist influence [documented, NYCB 2020]. Its director Lincoln Kirstein saw Ginastera's first ballet *Panambí* in Buenos Aires and commissioned a second, on rural Argentine life, for George Balanchine to choreograph [documented, NYCB repertory page]. Ginastera was twenty-five [documented, Library of Congress blog]. Horacio Butler, an Argentine painter, drew designs: an open landscape with a windmill, and a "herd cut in silhouette on wheels" [documented, NYCB 2020, from the MoMA exhibition "Lincoln Kirstein's Modern"].

Then the company folded. Its men, Kirstein among them, went to war, and the score went into the Library of Congress in 1944 with Kirstein's other Latin American commissions [documented, LoC]. Ginastera drew four dances into a suite, first played at the Colón in 1942 or on 12 May 1943 [contested; see Readings]; it made his name [documented, LoC]. The Colón finally staged the whole ballet eleven years after it was written, choreographed by its own ballet master, on a mixed bill with *Les Sylphides* and Act I of *Swan Lake* danced by the visiting British ballerina Alicia Markova [documented, Signorelli 2014].

### Roots and reference points
- **Martín Fierro.** Hernández's verse epic of a gaucho press-ganged into the frontier army became Argentina's national poem; Ginastera set its lines for a baritone and used it for mood, not plot [documented, LA Phil notes; IRCAM].
- **The Pampa.** Ginastera wrote that crossing the Pampa flooded him with impressions, "now joyful, now melancholy", changing over a day; hence a ballet shaped by the clock [documented, quoted by NYCB and LA Phil].
- **Folk dance.** The malambo is a competitive solo foot-tapping dance for men; the musicologist Gerard Béhague described its "fast and constant movement" in 6/8 [documented, quoted by OFCM; Béhague, *Music in Latin America*, 1979].
- **US models.** Kirstein had commissioned Aaron Copland's *Billy the Kid* (1938) for the same company; *Estancia* was planned as its Argentine cousin [documented, LoC; LA Phil]. Copland met Ginastera on his own 1941 tour and called him the "white hope" of Argentine music [documented, NYCB 2020, from Copland's diary].
- **Ginastera's "objective nationalism".** The composer's own term for his early period of direct folk quotation [documented, LoC].

### The world it built
Five tableaux: Dawn (introduction, little dance); Morning (Wheat Dance, the Land Workers, the Ranch Hands and entrance of the little horses, the Townsfolk); Afternoon (*Triste pampeano*, *La doma* or horse-breaking, Idyll at Twilight); Night (a nocturne); Dawn (scene and Final Dance, Malambo) [documented, en.wikipedia list; LoC]. The story is slight: the girl thinks the city boy soft beside the gauchos; he wins her by taming a horse, or by outdancing them [contested in summaries; NYCB says the horse, Britannica the dance]. Borowski's choreography, the *La Nación* critic wrote, stylised "our vernacular dances" through "the resources of classical dance" [documented, 20 August 1952, quoted by Signorelli]. Lommi, the lead, found that a hundred-piece orchestra drowned his heel-work, so he faked the *zapateo* and invented knee-drops, landing on both knees [documented, Lommi quoted in Manso 2008]. The women's corps added a pointe-tapping *repiqueteo* against the men's stamping in the finale [documented, Manso 2008].

### Design and art
Ortolani's designs were literal and pictorial. In the Wheat Dance the women were dressed as ears of wheat, "all golden"; in *La doma* dancers played the horses; the city family arrived in a sulky (a light horse-cart) that rolled on like a rail car [documented, Georgina Ginastera interviewed by Signorelli 2011]. María del Carmen Pérez danced one of the horses [documented, photograph in Heinrich and Alvaro, *Ballet en la Argentina*, 1960]. Butler's unused 1941 designs and Calatrava's 2010 painted drops share the same idea: an open sky over flat grass, with herds as moving silhouettes [argued; NYCB 2020 notes the likeness]. Calatrava also made a front curtain abstracting a stampeding herd [documented, McClure blog 2016].

### Premiere and reception
The opening fell less than a month after Eva Perón's death; the programmes carried a mourning badge [documented, Signorelli 2014]. *Crítica* headlined its review "La vida rural en puntas de pie" ("Rural life on pointe") [documented]. *La Razón* praised a choreography that "follows faithfully" the music and showed off Agoglia and Lommi; the magazine *Sintonía* called Lommi's "a magnificent creation" [documented, quoted by Signorelli]. Ginastera's daughter remembered "the bravos, the flowers thrown to the dancers" [documented, Signorelli interview]. Box office: not found in this pass.

### Versions and lineage
- 1941: Balanchine planned; never made [documented].
- 1942/43: concert suite, Op. 8a [documented].
- 1952: Borowski, Teatro Colón; no film or notation survives [documented, Signorelli: "not preserved on film nor in writing"].
- 1966: Oscar Araiz, Teatro Colón, for the 150th anniversary of independence; dropped from the repertory when the military government banned Araiz's works over his *Miraculous Mandarin*; revived 2001 at La Plata [documented, *La Nación* 24 May 2001].
- 2010: a new version at Teatro Argentino de La Plata for the Bicentenary; the choreographer said only the 1952 staging had used the complete score [documented, *Página/12* 25 May 2010].
- 2010: Wheeldon, NYCB, 29 May; Tiler Peck, Tyler Angle, Andrew Veyette as the Wild Horse; costumes Carlos Campos, lighting Mark Stanley [documented, NYCB]. This is the version in repertory today because it is the one a major company keeps reviving (2016, 2020) [documented for revivals].

### Readings and disputes
- **The gaucho as the nation.** Mariana Signorelli ("Análisis indiciario del ballet *Estancia*", *AdVersuS* 27, 2014) argues that the gaucho stood for "the national", an identity that Perón's government deliberately reinforced, so a Colón ballet on the Pampa in 1952 was also state myth [documented].
- **A goodwill product.** The NYCB history (Sutton 2020) and the Library of Congress (Cait Miller, "Goodwill and Ballet", 2019) read it as a piece of US cultural diplomacy, an Argentine *Billy the Kid* paid for by Rockefeller's office [documented].
- **Wheeldon's remake.** Mary Cargill (*DanceView Times*, 30 May 2010) found it "excessively literal", horses "in horsey suits"; *Dance Magazine* (Wendy Perron, 2010) likened it to *Rodeo* and to "an operetta"; enthusiasts heard five minutes of applause [documented].
- **Contradictions.** Suite premiere: 1942 under Ferruccio Calusio (Signorelli) or 12 May 1943 (OFCM, LoC) [contested]. A 2001 *La Nación* article says Borowski staged it in 1943 [contested; every other source says 1952]. The hero's winning feat: horse or dance [contested].

### Facts worth telling
- It waited eleven years, and an ocean, for its premiere [documented].
- The score was paid for by the US government's wartime propaganda office [documented, NYCB 2020].
- The lead faked the malambo's stamping because the orchestra drowned it, and invented knee-drops instead [documented, Manso 2008].
- Women danced as golden ears of wheat; dancers played the horses [documented, G. Ginastera].
- Alicia Markova danced *Swan Lake* on the same night [documented].
- The 1966 version vanished because a military government banned its choreographer's other works [documented, *La Nación* 2001].
- Every folk-dance troupe in Argentina, one choreographer said, has staged the final malambo [documented, *Página/12* 2010, as his claim].

### Set it beside
- `rodeo-1942`: the US ranch ballet: the tomboy changes to win the man; here the city man changes to win the woman.
- `billy-the-kid-1938`: the same patron's earlier frontier ballet, the model Kirstein had in mind.
- `la-coronela-1940`: Mexico's national dance of the same years rejects ballet; Buenos Aires puts the gaucho on pointe.
- `cesmebasi-1965`: another national ballet built as a day in a rural place, ending at night.

### What a maker can take from it
- A day is a structure: dawn to dawn gives order, light cues and an ending without a complicated plot.
- Folk material changes when its sound changes. On a big stage the malambo's footwork vanished, and the dancer found a visual equivalent. Translate an effect, not a step.
- The patron shapes the brief. A goodwill commission made a "typical" Argentina; ask who pays for the picture of a place.

### Beyond the stage
The Malambo is a staple of youth orchestras worldwide, notably the Simón Bolívar orchestra under Gustavo Dudamel [probable]; LA Phil played the complete ballet in 2022 and 2024 [documented]. Naxos recorded the complete score with *Panambí* (8.557582, 2006) [documented].

## 鱼美人 / The Fish Beauty (1959)
`id: fish-beauty-1959` · threads: T03, T01, T06, T14 · confidence of the header: documented (venue contested)

**Header.** Premiere 1959, Beijing, for the tenth anniversary of the People's Republic, by the Beijing Dance School (北京舞蹈学校) with the Central Conservatory of Music; the work was the graduation project of the school's second choreography training class (第二届编导训练班) [documented, HNU Arts Centre; Wang Yuhe on Wu Zuqiang; Wilcox 2018 citing the programme]. Venue: Tianqiao Theatre (天桥剧场) per HNU, but the lead dancer recalls the Nationalities Cultural Palace (民族文化宫) [contested]. Chief choreographer: Pyotr Gusev (彼·安·古雪夫), Soviet ballet master [documented]. Choreographers: the class collectively, led by Li Chenglian (栗承廉, Act I), Wang Shiqi (王世琦, Act II), Li Chengxiang (李承祥, Act III) [documented, HNU]. Music: Wu Zuqiang (吴祖强) and Du Mingxin (杜鸣心) [documented]. Scenario: Li Chengxiang and others, from Li's student étude, fixed in mid-January 1959 [documented, HNU]. Designers: not found in this pass. Leads: Chen Ailian (陈爱莲) and Chen Zemei as the Fish Beauty; Wang Gengyao (王赓尧) and Sun Zhengting as the Hunter; Chen Mingqi as the Mountain Demon [documented, CCTV dance channel]. Structure: three acts with prologue and epilogue; sources count the scenes as four, five or eight [contested]. Seen today: the 1979 Central Ballet of China pointe version and the 1994 Beijing Dance Academy remake [documented, Yu Ping 2020; PAR 1994].

### In one line
A sea-spirit falls in love with a hunter and, with the help of a ginseng elder, the two defeat the Mountain Demon who wants her.

### How it came to be
From 1954 the new Beijing Dance School invited Soviet teachers; Viktor Tsaplin taught the first choreography class (whose graduation work was *The Magic Lotus Lantern*, 1957), and Pyotr Gusev the second [documented, CCTV]. In 1957 the school split into a European (ballet) programme and a national dance-drama programme [documented, Wilcox 2018]. Gusev, between 1957 and 1959, rehearsed *Swan Lake* with the ballet students and planned a new Chinese work with the choreographers [documented, Yu Ping 2020].

In 1958 he noticed Li Chengxiang's short étude *The Fish Beauty* and saw that it could grow into a full ballet [documented, HNU]. His stated aim was an "experiment": to build "a classical-ballet system in which Chinese classical dance and Western ballet complement each other" [documented, quoted by Yu Ping]. He gave each act to a different student, a method of "dividing the work and gathering the best" [documented, CCTV]. Chen Ailian was cast because she could dance both; Dai Ailian (戴爱莲), the school's director, coached her pointe work, and she watched fish in park ponds [documented, Chen Ailian, *Guangming Daily* 2016]. It opened as one of four new dance dramas for the 1959 anniversary [documented, Yu Ping].

### Roots and reference points
- **Folk motifs.** The plot is "loosely based on a Chinese folk legend, heavily adjusted" to ballet devices [documented, Wilcox 2018]. Chinese sources call it a "myth" without naming one tale [documented, HNU]; the exact folk source: not found in this pass.
- **Swan Lake.** Chinese critics have called it "China's *Swan Lake*" [documented, Wilcox, *Asian Theatre Journal*]; the Coral Dance has been compared to the cygnets' dance [documented, Wilcox 2011]. Yu Ping (former vice-president of the Beijing Dance Academy) rejects the jibe that it is a gourd-ladle "copied from *Swan Lake*" [documented, Yu Ping 2020].
- **Soviet drama-ballet.** Gusev, of the Leningrad school, brought the *drambalet* model: a clear story, character themes in the music, set dances that can be lifted out [probable].
- **Chinese classical dance (古典舞).** Built in the 1950s from opera (xiqu) movement and martial arts; the work used its "twist, lean, round, curve" body against ballet's "open, stretched, straight, lifted" [documented, CFLAC 2022]. Yi minority folk dance appears in two scenes [documented, Wilcox 2011 citing Yu 2004].

### The world it built
Three realms, three movement rules [documented, HNU]. Act I, "Under the Sea", uses mainly ballet: Pearl Dance, Waterweed Dance, the Coral Dance for six women in red bodices with golden long nails, and "Twenty-four Fish Beauties". Act II, "The Human World", is a wedding with *yangge* steps, handkerchiefs and a sedan chair, drawn from Chinese folk dance. Act III, "The Demon's Cave", returns to ballet and fighting: the demon conjures a Snake Woman to seduce the hunter, then fire-demons [documented, sin80 synopsis]. The Ginseng Elders, small and whiskered with ginseng flowers on their heads, dance *yangge* steps and boxing moves [documented, sin80]. The overture states three themes: the Hunter, the Fish Beauty (lyric), the Demon (brass) [documented, sin80]. Wu and Du, both Moscow Conservatory graduates, split the acts; Wu wrote the Hunter–Fish Beauty duet, the 24 Fish Beauties and the Straw-Hat Flower Dance [documented, Wang Yuhe].

### Design and art
Designers' names: not found in this pass. What survives as description: waterweed girls in long green skirts swaying like weed in water; coral girls in red tights with coral headdresses and gold fingernails; the demon's invisibility cloak; a bridge from which the wedding party descends [documented, sin80]. Stage photographs dated 1959 are in the Beijing Dance Academy Archives [documented, Wilcox 2018 n.46]. The 1994 remake was criticised for "garish" sets and costumes [documented, Lin Yatin, *PAR* 1994]. No 1959 recording is available electronically [documented, Wilcox 2011].

### Premiere and reception
It ran for more than a month to full houses, and leaders received the company [documented, HNU, company-side claim]. Chen Ailian remembers about a thousand spectators, Liu Shaoqi among them [documented, *Guangming Daily* 2016]. But the work "alarmed China's dance critics" because it ignored the genre division; a debate ran through *Wudao* (*Dance*) nos. 1–3 of 1960, and in 1964 Wang Shiqi published a self-critique titled "Criticise foreign dogma" [documented, Wilcox 2018]. Wilcox argues the outcome "reaffirmed genre boundaries and subordinated ballet to Chinese dance" [documented]. Wilcox's count: the only newly created work using ballet technique in China between 1950 and 1964 [documented, Wilcox, Butterfly Lovers talk].

### Versions and lineage
- 1959: premiere [documented].
- 1963: restaged [documented, Wilcox 2011].
- 1979: Central Ballet of China revival "really put the Fish Beauty on pointe" [documented, Yu Ping 2020].
- 1994: Beijing Dance Academy, about three-quarters newly choreographed, for the school's fortieth anniversary at the Poly Theatre; sponsors were short, and the school considered selling the rights [documented, Lin Yatin, *PAR* 1994]. HNU calls this the reawakening after "nearly forty years" [documented].
- Excerpts such as the Snake Dance tour as concert items [documented, sin80]. The original was named a "twentieth-century classic" [documented, CCTV].
Its composers then wrote *The Red Detachment of Women* (1964); the Fish Beauty's success, writes Wang Yuhe, prompted the call for a ballet on contemporary Chinese subjects [documented].

### Readings and disputes
- **The founding fusion.** Yu Ping ("新时代中国芭蕾的'C'位思考", *Wenyi bao*, 3 Feb 2020) reads it as Chinese ballet's "original intention", crossing the river by feeling the stones [documented]. Jin Hao (BDA) lists its gains: narrative structure, less mime, a new vocabulary [documented, quoted by HNU].
- **The genre scandal.** Emily Wilcox (*Revolutionary Bodies*, 2018, ch. 2–3, open access) reads it as a work that broke the 1957 split and was disciplined for it; she translates the title *Lady of the Sea* [documented].
- **Copy or synthesis.** The "Swan Lake copy" charge (quoted and rejected by Yu Ping) against the "new system" reading (Li Chengxiang, *Wudao* 1997 no. 6) [documented both]. The 1994 Taiwanese reviewer Lin Yatin found Chinese vocabulary "stiff" inside a Russian frame, "*Sleeping Beauty* with a fish" [documented, paraphrase].
- **Contradictions.** Venue (Tianqiao vs Nationalities Cultural Palace); scene count (8 per HNU, 4 per Wang Yuhe, 5 per CFLAC); Chen Ailian is called both "national dancer" and ballet lead [contested].

### Facts worth telling
- The whole ballet grew from one student's classroom sketch [documented, HNU].
- Three students each choreographed one act [documented].
- The lead was a former street orphan from Shanghai [documented, *Guangming Daily* 2016].
- She studied fish in park ponds to find the role [documented].
- Gusev said ballet joined with Chinese dance would be "first in the world" [documented, quoted by HNU].
- In 1994 the school nearly sold the rights to pay for the revival [documented, *PAR*].
- Same composers, five years later: *The Red Detachment of Women* [documented].

### Set it beside
- `swan-lake-1877-1895`: the lake-maiden template; here the creature-woman is rescued into a village wedding.
- `shurale-1950`: a Tatar bird-maiden and forest demon, the Soviet "national ballet" model Gusev knew.
- `red-detachment-1964`: the same composers and school, the opposite subject: fantasy gives way to revolution.
- `shim-chung-1986`: another Asian heroine who enters the sea king's realm and returns.

### What a maker can take from it
- Give each realm its own movement rule (ballet under the sea, folk dance among people), so the audience reads the world from the body.
- A small sketch can carry a big world; test an idea at étude size first.
- Fusion is political. Mixing two systems can be read as theft or as invention, so write down why you mix.

### Beyond the stage
The Snake Dance and Coral Dance circulate as gala and competition pieces [probable]. A symphonic suite from the score is performed [documented, sin80 lists "symphonic ballet suite"].

## Çeşmebaşı / At the Fountain (1965)
`id: cesmebasi-1965` · threads: T14, T21, T04, T05 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere Friday 19 February 1965, Büyük Tiyatro, Ankara, State Theatre ballet company (later Turkish State Opera and Ballet), billed "A Turkish fantasy"; the composer conducted [documented, *Akis* 26 Feb 1965; tr.wikipedia]. Choreography: Ninette de Valois, founder of the Royal Ballet and of the Turkish state ballet school (1947) [documented, Başar 2022; DergiPark 2022]. Music: Ferit Tüzün (1929–1977), reworked from his *Anadolu Süiti* (Anatolian Suite), with a soprano *türkü* (folk song) sung by Serap Sezer [documented]. Title suggested by the theatre historian Metin And [documented]. Scenario: none fixed; "it has no tangible subject, it is rather a fantasy" (programme quoted by *Akis*) [documented]. Sets: Refik Eren and Hüseyin Mumcu; costumes: Hale Eren [documented, tr.wikipedia; Başar citing Nutku 1965]. One act, six sections [documented]. The version seen today: revivals of de Valois's choreography by state companies, and Uğur Seyrek's new version (Antalya, 2 August 2014), toured with *Bolero* [documented].

### In one line
A day at an Anatolian village fountain, from water-fetching girls to the shadow-puppet clowns Karagöz and Hacivat, made by an English founder as a model for a Turkish ballet.

### How it came to be
De Valois had come to Istanbul in 1947 at the invitation of the education minister Hasan Âli Yücel, with British Council backing, to found a ballet school; the first dancers graduated in 1957 and danced a complete *Coppélia* in 1961 [documented, Başar 2022; DergiPark 2022]. She wanted, by the 1964–65 season, a ballet to a Turkish composer's music "as an example for Turkish choreographers" [documented, tr.wikipedia]. Metin And helped her choose; she met the composer Sabahattin Kalender, then chose Tüzün's *Anatolian Suite*, a five-part score that had won second prize in Yapı Kredi Bank's 1954 competition and had been played in Munich in 1958 [documented, tr.wikipedia].

She heard a recording and asked for changes; And translated between them and later called Tüzün's hesitation "laziness". Deniz Başar reads it instead as a composer given "no negotiation space" over his own score [documented, Başar 2022]. The twins Rezzan and Ümran Ürey, dancers trained in Anatolian dance, helped with the folk sections; Erhan Ergüler, grandson of the Karagöz master Hayali Küçük Ali, inspired the shadow-puppet scene [documented].

### Roots and reference points
- **Petrushka (1911).** To British colleagues de Valois called the ballet "my bastard *Petrouchka*" (Glasstone 2012), a fairground of popular entertainers; Başar notes she did not share this model with her Turkish team [documented, Başar 2022].
- **Karagöz and Hacivat.** The Ottoman shadow-puppet pair (the blunt commoner and the pompous scholar) belong to Istanbul's coffee-house nights. Metin And and others objected to putting them in a village; they gave way "out of respect" [documented, Başar citing Günsür/Yüceil 2007].
- **Folk dance.** The *horon* of the Black Sea coast, 7/8 and 9/8 *aksak* ("limping") rhythms, a dance tune and a *türkü* [documented, *Akis*; tr.wikipedia].
- **Âşık Garip.** The pas de deux is a dream: a minstrel with a *bağlama* (long-necked lute) dances with his imagined beloved, after the folk romance of Âşık Garip [documented, tr.wikipedia].
- **Metin And's theory.** His *Turkish Village Dances* (1964) set out how a national ballet should use folk material [documented, Başar].

### The world it built
Six sections: a quiet introduction, a lively *horon*, a slow pas de deux, a fast dance tune, a bar section, a final dance and a soprano's *türkü* [documented, *Akis* from the programme]. A recent synopsis: young men tease girls fetching water; vendor girls pass; the village beauty, seeing a minstrel, dreams of an ideal couple from folk tales until villagers wake her; a troupe of Roma, acrobats, Karagöz and Hacivat and masked twins arrive; at night the girl is left alone with "the Karagöz and Hacivat of her dreams" [documented, DergiPark 2025]. The choreography used "clipped, hard, limping-rhythm steps rarely seen in classical ballets" [documented, tr.wikipedia]. De Valois used the Ürey twins' likeness to build a mirror-symmetrical dance [documented]. Karagöz and Hacivat moved sideways to the front "without losing their two-dimensionality", and at the end their shadows were thrown on the backcloth [documented, Nutku, *Milliyet*, 2 March 1965, quoted by Başar].

### Design and art
The set by Refik Eren and Hüseyin Mumcu showed a village square with its public fountain, the centre of every Turkish village [documented, photograph in de Valois 1977, reproduced in Başar]. Hale Eren's costumes dressed the girls with water jugs; the 2014 remake swapped jugs for buckets and added daisies for love and respect [documented, tr.wikipedia]. The Karagöz figures copied the flat, jointed, painted-leather look of shadow puppets; Glasstone and his wife bought a pair of Küçük Ali's real puppets [documented, Glasstone 2015 quoted by Başar]. The cartoonist Bedri Koraman drew the 1965 leads from a performance photograph [documented, Bursa festival archive].

### Premiere and reception
Engin Akaoğlu danced Âşık Garip, Meriç Sümen the village girl, the Ürey twins the masked pair; Ergüler was Karagöz [documented]. The finale was encored, which *Akis* said had no precedent in the short history of Turkish opera and ballet [documented, *Akis*]. Özdemir Nutku called it a hopeful trial: music and dance were not "organically" in dialogue, but folk steps could merge with "methodological dance"; he proposed expressionist dance rather than classical ballet as the better partner [documented, Nutku 1965 via Başar]. Tüzün gave a supper for the cast attended by de Valois and the British and Soviet ambassadors [documented, *Akis*].

### Versions and lineage
- 1965: Ankara premiere, Istanbul tour [documented].
- 1967: first foreign tour of Turkish ballet (Bulgaria); shown to Soviet premier Kosygin (1967) and President de Gaulle (1968), for whom dancers waved French tricolour scarves [documented, Başar citing Glasstone 2015]. Also Pakistan, Algeria, Tunisia, Egypt; Hong Kong Asian Arts Festival 1981 [documented, Başar].
- 1982: first production of the İzmir State Ballet; 1993: revived by İstanbul State Ballet under Richard Glasstone [documented, Başar].
- Turkish dance notators trained in England (Suna Eden Şenel, Yüksel Çapanoğlu And) recorded Ankara's ballets on paper [documented, KTB e-book]; whether *Çeşmebaşı* was notated: not found in this pass.
- 2014: Uğur Seyrek's modern version, Antalya State Opera and Ballet, no Karagöz, the fountain as today's "media" square [documented].

### Readings and disputes
- **The founding national ballet.** Turkish histories (DergiPark 2022, "The Establishment of Turkish Ballet and First Original Works") call it "a truly Turkish ballet" and the turning point after which Turkish choreographers such as Sait Sökmen (*Çark*, 1968) followed [documented].
- **An outsider's Turkey.** Deniz Başar ("Çeşmebaşı: Negotiating the Official Turkish Identity in Ballet", transcript, 2022, open access) argues it "patchworked" Istanbul and village images into state-approved Turkishness for export, under "the colonial hegemony of the outsider and the bureaucratic control of the insider" [documented]. Metin And (1976) and Özdemir Nutku also judged de Valois's approach an outsider's [documented, per Başar].
- **De Valois's own view.** In the programme she wrote that the ballet makes no claim to master village steps: "the real Turkish ballet will be born soon", from Turkish choreographers trained internationally [documented, quoted on thehouseseat by Seyrek].
- **Contradictions.** Premiere year 1964 (Başar; Mutlucan) against 19 February 1965 (*Akis*, a week after) [contested; *Akis* is the nearer witness]. Hacivat danced by Taner Akakçe (tr.wikipedia) or Dudley Tomlinson (photo caption in Yüceil 2007) [contested; perhaps different casts]. The register lists de Valois as "probable"; she is now documented as choreographer.

### Facts worth telling
- The finale was danced twice on the first night because the audience would not stop clapping [documented, *Akis*].
- De Valois privately called it "my bastard *Petrouchka*" [documented, Glasstone via Başar].
- Karagöz was danced by the grandson of a real Karagöz master [documented].
- Twin dancers argued with de Valois in rehearsal: "not like that Madame, like this" [documented, Rezzan Ürey interview, Günsür].
- It was danced for de Gaulle with French flags in the finale [documented].
- The music had first been a prize-winning concert suite for a bank's anniversary [documented].

### Set it beside
- `petrushka-1911`: the fairground with puppets; Ankara takes the model, drops the tragedy and keeps the crowd.
- `el-leila-el-kebira-2001`: Cairo's moulid of puppets turned dancers, made from inside by an Egyptian choreographer.
- `estancia-1952`: another national ballet as a day in a rural place, also made by a foreign-born ballet master.
- `la-coronela-1940`: a US-born woman founds a national dance abroad, but for a left-wing state project.

### What a maker can take from it
- Know your hidden model. De Valois built on *Petrushka* but did not tell her team, who could not then judge or answer it. Share your references with collaborators.
- Moving a figure moves its meaning: Karagöz from an Istanbul café to a village square is a different character. Check where your borrowed elements belong.
- Flat things can dance flat: keeping the puppets two-dimensional on stage was the scene's best idea.

### Beyond the stage
The documentary *Dancing Across the Bosphorus* (Levent Kumrulu, 2010) covers the founding generation [documented, cited by Başar]. Ferit Tüzün's suite is played in concert [probable].

## Raka / Raka (1967)
`id: raka-1967` · threads: T23, T18, T16, T15 · confidence of the header: documented (premiere month contested)

**Header.** Premiere 26 August 1967, the Aula (University of Pretoria), Pretoria, PACT Ballet (Performing Arts Council of the Transvaal), conductor Quayle [documented, Steyn 2008; *Ons Erfdeel* confirms "August" in Pretoria]; Steyn's own introduction says "November 1967" [contested]. Choreography and scenario: Frank Staff (1918–1971), South African-born, formerly of Ballet Rambert [documented for role, Steyn; biography probable]. Music: Graham Newcater (b. 1941), a twelve-tone composer, commissioned 1966; about 45 minutes [documented, Steyn]. Source: N. P. van Wyk Louw's Afrikaans verse epic *Raka* (1941, about 850 lines) [documented]. Sets and costumes: Raimond Schoop [documented]. Six scenes [documented, 1967 programme quoted by Steyn]. Leads: Juan Sanchez (Raka), Ken Yeatman (Koki), Veronica Paeper (the Woman) [documented]. The version seen today: Paeper's recreations of Staff's choreography (George 2001; Cape Town City Ballet at KKNK 2004 and 2010) and the 1968 film [documented, ESAT].

### In one line
A half-human creature enters an African village; the women and children are drawn to him, only the warrior Koki resists, and Raka kills him and takes the kraal.

### How it came to be
PACT, one of four state-funded provincial arts councils founded in 1962–63, wanted a major South African work [probable]. The commission came in 1966. A PACT official, De Villiers, took charge; she recruited Staff and Schoop, then struggled to find a composer until the SABC conductor Edgar Cree recommended the young Newcater [documented, Steyn 2008]. Newcater had no English translation of the poem; Antony Dawes's first complete translation appeared only in 1968 [documented, Steyn]. Staff drafted the scene framework that Newcater composed to [documented].

Steyn points out two oddities. The commission came during a public feud in which Prime Minister H. F. Verwoerd accused Van Wyk Louw of being unpatriotic. And the most iconic Afrikaans poem went to a young English-speaking composer rather than an Afrikaner [documented, Steyn 2008, argued there]. Staff wrote a new role, the Woman, for Paeper, whom he had married the year before [documented].

### Roots and reference points
- **The poem.** Louw cast it like a Greek tragedy. Raka is "the ape-man, destructive voluptuary and intruder"; Koki, lone defender of the tribe's spiritual inheritance, is killed [documented, *Ons Erfdeel* 1969, paraphrased]. Critics read it as the Second World War's release of the "primal man" (Dekker), as Western cultural pessimism after Ortega y Gasset, or even with Raka as Hitler (rejected by Kannemeyer) [documented, *Acta Theologica* 2019]. Louw refused to fix one meaning [documented, Van Coller and Van Jaarsveld 2010].
- **Earlier art on the poem.** Alexis Preller's *Raka in word and paint* (1956); Stefans Grové's *Elegy* (1948); Peter Klatzow's *Orchestral Piece* (1966); a 1950s Amsterdam ballet to *The Rite of Spring* [documented, ESAT; Steyn].
- **The Rite.** A chosen victim and a community's rite: the register thread T16 [argued].
- **Music.** Newcater's twelve-tone writing came from Alban Berg's Violin Concerto [documented, Steyn].

### The world it built
Six scenes: the kraal, awakening; the river ("the women saw him first"); a clearing; the kraal; the jungle; the kraal [documented, programme 1967]. The ballet's biggest change is scene four: the Woman first watches "the dark beast", then pledges herself to Koki, doubts, and dances "a moving pas de deux" with him before the two males fight, a love triangle the poem does not have [documented, Van Coller and Van Jaarsveld 2010]. Staff "does not render it as Bantu dance or legend", applying ballet principles, with flat-foot dancing for weight [documented, *Ons Erfdeel*]. The music gives the evil forces "shrill dissonances in the brass" and Koki the steady timpani [documented, *Ons Erfdeel*]. The suite (1973) names the sections: Awakening of the Village, Koki and the Children, By the River, Koki and the Woman, Raka's Dance, Funeral and Destruction [documented, UP composers page].

### Design and art
Schoop, a designer at PACT, designed set and costumes [documented]. The register notes that the costumes drew on Ndebele ("Mapogger") motifs [register note citing *Ons Erfdeel* 1969; not re-seen in the excerpt read in this pass]. If so, the bold geometric house-painting and beadwork of Ndebele women in the Transvaal were borrowed for a mythical, unnamed tribe [argued]. Colours, materials and photographs: not found in this pass.

### Premiere and reception
*Ons Erfdeel* called it "a surprising breakthrough in our ballet art"; Paeper's Woman was "particularly expressive", Sanchez danced Raka "with astonishing virtuosity" [documented]. PACT toured it to Welkom (20–23 March 1968) and Bloemfontein (27–30 March 1968) [documented, Steyn]. The film (1968), shot with five cameras in one day, was called the earliest known South African full-length ballet film and was distributed by Twentieth Century Fox [documented, *Ons Erfdeel*; ESAT]. Its royalties paid for Newcater's house [documented, Steyn]. Audiences: PACT's main theatres then served white audiences under apartheid's separate-amenities laws [probable; the audience rule for these dates not found in this pass].

### Versions and lineage
- 1967–68: PACT premiere and tour; film directed by Alf Travers (Steyn ch. 3; *Ons Erfdeel*) or Sven Persson (Steyn ch. 1) [contested].
- 1985: PACT revival, State Theatre Pretoria (26–28 Sept) and Johannesburg Civic (9–12 Oct); 1992: Pretoria, 4–8 July [documented, Steyn].
- 2001: George Arts Festival, with Christo Davids [documented, ESAT].
- 2004, 2010: Cape Town City Ballet, Paeper recreating Staff, KKNK and Artscape; leads Coert Grobbelaar and Robin van Wyk in 2004; the Cape Town company used Adam Small's translation in preparation [documented].
No notation found; Paeper's memory and the film are the record [argued].

### Readings and disputes
- **A universal good-and-evil fable.** The 1969 review and the 2004 leads read it as timeless: "the moral questions and elemental passion are timeless" (Greeff 2004) [documented, quoted by Van Coller and Van Jaarsveld].
- **An apartheid allegory.** Amoré Steyn (*Graham Newcater's* Raka *in its literary, musical and historical context*, MMus, Pretoria, 2008) argues the programme and film narration dwell on Raka's "darkness" and blame the women's lust for the tribe's fall; under the Immorality Act "it is hard not to see this as a metaphor for interracial sex" [documented]. She adds that Staff, a state employee, likely shaped it to please "the powers-that-be" [documented, argued there].
- **The poem itself.** Older readings set Koki/Raka as culture/nature, good/evil; recent ones see an Other who is "different rather than evil" and criticise a "colonialist import" (Matildie Thom Wium, *Tydskrif vir Geesteswetenskappe* 53/2, 2013) [documented]. Michael Chapman notes Louw made Koki "coloured, not white", perhaps to avoid a European/African split [documented, quoted 2010].
- **Who is missing.** A white company, an Afrikaans poem, an English-speaking composer and a designer drawing on Ndebele art staged an African village with no Black artist on the team found in this pass [argued; team list documented].
- **Contradictions.** August vs November 1967; film director Travers vs Persson; ESAT dates the Paeper recreations 1999/2004 on one page and 2001/2004/2010 on another [contested].

### Facts worth telling
- The composer was in his mid-twenties and could not read the poem in English, because no translation existed yet [documented].
- The role of the Woman, absent from the poem, was written for the choreographer's new wife [documented].
- The film was shot in a single day with five cameras [documented, *Ons Erfdeel*].
- Royalties from a Hollywood studio's distribution bought the composer his house [documented].
- Staff and Newcater's next ballet, *Rain Queen*, was said to be cursed by the real Rain Queen; Staff died within months [documented as story, UP composers page].
- The poem was later broadcast in Zulu, Sotho and Xhosa radio translations (1989) [documented].

### Set it beside
- `creation-du-monde-1923`: Paris's "Africa" made with none of the culture on the team; Pretoria repeats the pattern under law.
- `rites-page-1997`: Bangarra's Aboriginal *Rite*, the counter-case with Indigenous co-authorship.
- `swan-lake-masilo-2010`: forty years on, a Black South African choreographer from Soweto remakes the canon on her own terms.
- `rite-of-spring-1913`: the sacrificial village rite and its "primitive" pulse, the model behind Raka's dance.

### What a maker can take from it
- An allegory takes on the politics of the room where it is staged. A fable of the "dark intruder" means something specific in 1967 Pretoria; test your myth against your moment.
- Adding a romance changes a story's meaning: the invented Woman turned a poem about a community's spiritual decay into one about female desire as danger.
- Ask who is on the team when a culture is shown, and whose patterns are on the costumes.

### Beyond the stage
The 1968 film; Newcater's concert suite (1973); a radiophonic drama (1967); *Raka – die Musical* (1988); Koos Kombuis's thriller *Raka die roman* (2005) [documented, ESAT].

**Header.** Premiere 8 February 1980, Cultural Center of the Philippines (CCP), Manila, CCP Dance Company (renamed Ballet Philippines) [documented, Philstar 2012]. Direction and choreography: Alice Reyes, co-founder of the company (with Eddie Elejar) [documented]. Music: Ryan Cayabyab, aged 26 [documented, *Vogue PH* 2023]. Libretto and lyrics in Filipino: Bienvenido Lumbera; English translation (later surtitles): Rolando Tinio [documented]. Sets and costumes: Salvador "Badong" Bernal [documented]. Lighting: not named for 1980 in sources seen; Barbara Tan-Tiongco is credited for technical and lighting direction in 2023 [documented, Theater Fans Manila]. About two hours with interval [documented, 2023]. Original cast: singers Basil Valdez (Rama) and Kuh Ledesma (Sita); dancers Nonoy Froilan (Rama), Effie Nañas and Ester Rimpos (Sita), Robert Medina (Ravana) [documented, Philstar 2012]. Seen today: the 2023 Alice Reyes Dance Philippines staging with the original score and Bernal's designs [documented].

### In one line
The Ramayana as a Filipino "rock opera-ballet", each character played at once by a singer and a dancer.

### How it came to be
In 1977 Reyes had made *Tales of the Manuvu*, a rock-ballet on the origins of the Manobo people of Mindanao [documented, *Vogue PH* 2023]. As the company entered its second decade she wanted to "go beyond national themes and explore other Asian sources"; she had read the Ramayana in college [documented]. She choreographed it in 1979 "as an audacious experiment" [documented, ARDP]. She asked Lumbera to distil the epic into a Filipino libretto and the young Cayabyab, from the University of the Philippines, to write the score [documented].

Cayabyab put in "everything I learned in UP": gamelan learned from a visiting Indonesian professor who had shown the *wayang kulit* Ramayana, a version of the Balinese *kecak* monkey chant, the Filipino love song (*kundiman*), Indian melody, soul rock and some Dixieland [documented, Philstar Life 2023; *Vogue PH* 2023]. The CCP itself, opened in 1969, was the flagship of First Lady Imelda Marcos's cultural policy, and 1980 fell within martial law [probable; not stated in the sources read].

### Roots and reference points
- **The Ramayana**, Valmiki's Sanskrit epic: Prince Rama, an incarnation of Vishnu, wins Sita in Mithila, is exiled on the eve of his coronation through his stepmother Kaikeyi's scheme, and defeats the demon king Ravana [documented, Philstar 2023].
- **Southeast Asian Ramayanas.** The Maranao people of Lake Lanao sing their own version, *Maharadia Lawana*, in which the Ravana figure is the hero; Juan R. Francisco recorded it in 1968 [documented, en.wikipedia; Indian Express 2025]. Java's *wayang* and Bali's *kecak* versions shaped the score [documented].
- **Rock opera.** *Jesus Christ Superstar* (1970) and the rock-opera wave were the genre model [probable]. The ballet coincided with the birth of OPM (Original Pilipino Music); the 2023 press calls it "the first OPM music and dance theater masterpiece" [documented, Philstar 2023, a claim].
- **Reyes's own repertory**: *Amada*, *Itim-Asu*, *At a Maranaw Gathering*, *Dugso* [documented, Philstar 2012].

### The world it built
Each major character has a singer and a dancer, colour-matched so the pair reads as one person; singers are drawn into the choreography [documented, GMA review 2012]. Sung numbers include "Magbalik Ka Na, Mahal" ("Come back, my love"), Sita's ballad, which became a Filipino standard [documented]. Lumbera wrote in levels of language: elevated verse for Rama and Sita, 1980s street Tagalog and gay "swardspeak" for the demons; Ravana's sister Soorpanakha sings "Type Kita Rama" ("I fancy you, Rama") [documented, Philstar Life 2023]. Lumbera wanted to "jar the audience" into seeing the epic as "now" and Filipino [documented]. The dance joins classical ballet's lightness with modern steps; the corps moves in sweeping patterns against lyrical duets [documented, Philstar Life]. A fight scene draws on an Asian martial form [documented, GMA, which found it "anti-climactic"].

### Design and art
Reyes described Bernal's 1980 design as a set "that echoed Indian lives and costumes that carried the colors of curry spices and the feel of turquoise" [documented, show notes quoted in *Vogue PH*]. A 2023 writer calls the set "a multilevel Mughal garden", with costumes in "the colors and textures of Jaipur gemstones and Kerala spices" [documented, Philstar Life]. The stage floor is plain white so the jewel-toned costumes and the dancers' silhouettes stand out [documented, GMA 2012]. Arched portals of drapes painted with embroidery-like detail framed the 2023 stage [documented, *Vogue PH*]. Bernal, later National Artist for Theater Design (2003), was famous for turning "limited resources into a richly symbolic stage world" [documented, ARDP]; Reyes joked he had "a very operatic mind and opera means a lot of money" [documented, Philstar 2023].

### Premiere and reception
The 1980 run drew "long queues" outside the theatre [documented as "according to accounts", *Vogue PH*]. It launched Kuh Ledesma's career before her first hit [documented, GMA; Philstar 2012]. Gigi Escalante danced the witch Kooni and the demon Soorpanakha; her daughter Kalila Aguilos sang Sita in 2012 [documented]. Critics' words from 1980: not found in this pass. Box office: not found.

### Versions and lineage
- 1980: premiere; staged twice more before 2012 (dates not found) [documented: 2012 was "the fourth time", *Vogue PH*].
- 2012: Ballet Philippines, CCP, 30 Nov–9 Dec, singers Christian Bautista, OJ Mariano, Karylle, Kalila Aguilos; dancers Richardson Yadao, Katherine Trofeo and others [documented].
- 2020: planned for 20 March to close Ballet Philippines' fiftieth season; cancelled [documented, *Vogue PH*; cause probably the pandemic].
- 2023: Alice Reyes Dance Philippines, Metropolitan Theater then Samsung Performing Arts Theater, 15–24 September; Arman Ferrer and Karylle sing; Orchestra of the Filipino Youth; restaging by Lester Reguindin [documented].
The singers keep "the original from 1980" score (Cayabyab) [documented].

### Readings and disputes
- **A national landmark.** The 2023 press calls it the only work with five National Artists (Reyes, Cayabyab, Lumbera, Bernal, Tinio) and "the Philippines' *Les Misérables*" (singer Arman Ferrer) [documented]. The canonising reading is the company's and the state's [argued].
- **The epic from inside Asia.** The register pairs it with *La Bayadère* under T05: an Asian company retells an Asian epic, against Paris's or St Petersburg's India [argued, CENSUS]. But an Indian epic told in Manila is still an import; the Maranao *Maharadia Lawana*, with its heroic Ravana, was not the version chosen [argued; Francisco documented].
- **Hard to follow.** Nikki Francisco (Theater Fans Manila, 2023) found it "gorgeous, though mystifying", hard for newcomers to grasp [documented]; Amanda Lago (GMA, 2012) praised a "luminous collaboration" [documented].
- **Contradictions.** The 1980 Ravana singer: Leo Valdez is named in 2023 accounts but not in the 2012 cast list [contested]. The title is written "Rama Hari" and "Rama, Hari"; *hari* is Tagalog for "king" [documented for both spellings; meaning probable].

### Facts worth telling
- Every role is played twice at once, by a voice and a body [documented].
- The composer was 26 and called it his first major work [documented].
- A demon sings a come-on in 1980s gay slang [documented].
- Its love ballad became a pop standard outside the theatre [documented].
- A 2020 revival was cancelled; it came back in 2023 under a new company [documented].
- The mother danced a demon in 1980; the daughter sang the heroine in 2012 [documented].

### Set it beside
- `bayadere-1877`: Petipa's India, made in St Petersburg with no Indian artist; Manila's from a shared Southeast Asian Rama tradition.
- `the-kabuki-1986`: the reverse route: a European writes the Asian epic for an Asian company.
- `fish-beauty-1959`: another Asian "fusion" whose movement shifts by realm.
- `el-leila-el-kebira-2001`: a popular, sung source (an operetta) turned into dance in a Global South capital.

### What a maker can take from it
- Split one character into two performers, voice and body, and colour-code them; it solves the problem of words in dance.
- Use registers of language as costume: high verse for heroes, slang for villains, so an ancient story speaks today.
- A small budget can push a design to clarity: a white floor and saturated costumes did more than scenery.

### Beyond the stage
"Magbalik Ka Na, Mahal" lives on as a recorded standard; Kuh Ledesma's career began here [documented]. A cast recording: not found in this pass.

## 祝福 / The New Year's Sacrifice (1981)
`id: new-years-sacrifice-1981` · threads: T18, T24, T15, T07 · confidence of the header: documented (date in detail contested)

**Header.** Premiere 25–27 June 1981, Tianqiao Theatre (天桥剧场), Beijing, Central Ballet of China (中央芭蕾舞团, now National Ballet of China) [documented, NBC repertory page]; chinadance.cn gives 27 June [contested in detail]. Act II was shown first, in 1980 [documented]. Choreography: Jiang Zuhui (蒋祖慧, b. 1934), daughter of the writer Ding Ling, co-choreographer of *The Red Detachment of Women* [documented]. Music: Liu Tingyu (刘廷禹) [documented]. Scenario: Jiang Zuhui, Liu Tingyu, Chen Minfan, Jiang Weihao [documented, chinadance.cn]. Stage design: Zheng Riyang (郑日洋), Jin Taihong, Liang Hongzhou, Peng Yufei, Li Keyu [documented, NBC]; chinadance.cn writes the first name 郑越洋 [contested, probably a typo]. Four acts [documented]. Leads: Yu Leidi (郁蕾娣) as Xianglin's Wife, Wu Zhaoning (武兆宁) as He Laoliu [documented, chinadance.cn]. The version seen today: Act II alone, which "extended and replaced the life of the whole ballet" [documented, chinadance.cn].

**Note on the brief.** The batch list placed this work in Shanghai; the sources place it in Beijing. Shanghai's Lu Xun ballet of the same year was the one-act *A Q* (阿Q, Shanghai Ballet, 1981) [documented, Zou Zhirui 2008].

### In one line
A widow on pointe: Lu Xun's peasant woman, sold into a second marriage, chooses love, loses husband and child, and dies in the snow while the rich celebrate the New Year rite.

### How it came to be
Jiang Zuhui had studied with the Korean dancer Choi Seung-hee in Pyongyang (1949), at the Beijing Dance School, and in the choreography department of the State Institute of Theatre Arts in Moscow (1956–61) [documented, *Zhongguo wenyi pinglun* 2023]. Her mother had been branded a "rightist" in 1957; Jiang was told "your mother's affairs have nothing to do with you" [documented, *Yanhuang chunqiu* oral history 2025]. After the Cultural Revolution, Chinese ballet turned to modern literary classics by Lu Xun, Ba Jin and Cao Yu [documented, Yu Ping 2020; Zou Zhirui 2008]. The ballet was made for the centenary of Lu Xun's birth (1881–1981) [documented, chinadance.cn].

Jiang began, she said, with the Soviet method: the theme and "supreme task", then "typical environment and typical character" [documented, interview 2023]. She went to Shaoxing, Lu Xun's home town, and was oppressed by the stone chastity arches she saw there [documented]. She interviewed Yuan Xuefen (袁雪芬), the Yue-opera actress who had played Xianglin's Wife, and asked why the widow came to accept He Laoliu. "He Laoliu was too kind; he gave her a cup of water" [documented, interview 2023]. Jiang used the detail. Act II won first prize for choreography and performance at the 1980 Ministry of Culture review [documented].

### Roots and reference points
- **Lu Xun, "祝福" (1924).** A narrator returns home at New Year and meets the beggared Xianglin's Wife, whose life the villagers treat as bad luck [probable, standard synopsis]. The title names the year-end rite of offering to the gods, to which a twice-married widow may not lay hands [probable].
- **Stage and screen.** Yue opera (Shanghai) and the 1956 colour film (Xia Yan screenplay) [film probable; Yuan Xuefen's role documented by Jiang].
- **Soviet drama-ballet.** Jiang's training: the realist "typical character" method [documented, her own words].
- **Folk dance.** Shandong *guzi yangge*, Jiaozhou *yangge* and Anhui *huagudeng* (flower-drum lantern) for the wedding crowd; for young Xianglin, "features of Korean dance" and the body style of the Peking-opera *qingyi* (virtuous young woman) role [documented, Zou 2008; Jiang, *Renmin ribao* 2019].

### The world it built
The ballet opens with Xianglin newly widowed, sold, and fleeing at night; it ends at the annual blessing rite as she staggers through heavy snow to her death [documented, Zou 2008]. Act II is the core: the village wedding, all bustle, the guests eager to lift the bride's veil, when Xianglin appears with her arms bound behind her and throws herself against the table corner [documented, chinadance.cn]. The three veil-lifting moments drew knowing laughter every night [documented]. Then the duet: He Laoliu covers her hanging hand; she wakes and knocks away the medicine he boiled; he picks up the bowl in silence; he lets her go; at the door she has nowhere to go and turns back [documented, Jiang 2023]. Jiang called her own aim to show "two kind hearts" coming together, a liberation, not "revenge" [documented]. Her essay "How I choreographed the Act II pas de deux" treats it as both character-building and "ballet nationalisation" [documented, quoted by Yu Ping].

### Design and art
A five-person design team signed the sets and costumes, Li Keyu (李克瑜) among them, a costume designer for the national companies [documented for names; Li's specialism probable]. What the stage showed: a mountain village wedding with a table as the central prop; the bride's red veil; the Lu family's ancestral hall for the rite; falling snow at the end [documented, chinadance.cn and Zou 2008, as scenes]. Colours, materials and photographs: not found in this pass. A 1980 rehearsal photograph shows Jiang with Wang Caijun and Wang Pingping [documented, *Zhongguo wenyi pinglun* 2023, caption].

### Premiere and reception
A critic told Jiang, "you have written out what Lu Xun wrote only between the lines" [documented, Jiang 2023]. Chinadance.cn calls it the earliest psychological ballet in post-Cultural-Revolution China, opening "a new page" [documented, argued there]. Act II toured the United States twice, the USSR, Britain, Germany, the Netherlands, Belgium, Switzerland, Austria, the Philippines and Taiwan; a Chinese woman working at the White House said watching it she "felt her heart crying" [documented, Jiang 2023]. In 1987 Jiang staged Act II for Ballet Philippines [documented]. In 1994–95 Act II was nominated as a "classic" of twentieth-century Chinese dance [documented].

### Versions and lineage
- 1980: Act II at the Ministry of Culture review [documented].
- 1981: full four-act premiere [documented].
- Later: Act II tours alone; the full ballet "has not been performed for a long time" (2012) [documented, chinadance.cn].
- 1987: Act II for Ballet Philippines, Manila [documented].
Notation or full film: not found in this pass.

### Readings and disputes
- **A psychological ballet.** Chinadance.cn (2012) and the dance-theory world call it "psychological ballet" (心理芭蕾), in line with an international trend [documented].
- **A realist ballet.** Jiang rejects the label: "they say it is a psychological drama, but I did not write it as one"; theorists rank psychology above realism, and she does not [documented, Jiang 2023]. The two readings are about the same duet.
- **Too pretty for Lu Xun.** The ballet's Xianglin "is not much like the aged, dying" woman in Lu Xun, but a graceful figure on pointe [documented, chinadance.cn]; the same writer argues that abroad she still read as "very Chinese" [documented].
- **From model works to literature.** Zou Zhirui ("改革开放30年中国芭蕾舞剧的繁荣", *Zhongguo yishu bao*, 2008) and Yu Ping (2020) place it with *Lin Daiyu* (1982) as the period's representative works [documented].
- **Contradictions.** 25–27 vs 27 June; Zheng Riyang vs Zheng Yueyang; Act II dated 1980 while the full work is 1981 [contested or explained].

### Facts worth telling
- Its choreographer's mother, Ding Ling, chose ballet for her after seeing *The Fountain of Bakhchisarai* in the USSR [documented, oral history].
- One act outlived the whole ballet [documented].
- The key gesture came from an opera star's answer: a cup of water [documented].
- The wedding scene got laughs before the bride's suicide attempt [documented].
- Lu Xun's centenary produced two Lu Xun ballets in one year, in Beijing and Shanghai [documented].
- Its Act II was taught to the Filipino company that made *Rama, Hari* [documented].

### Set it beside
- `red-detachment-1964`: the same choreographer; Qionghua is "a rough girl" who explodes outward, Xianglin a woman who holds everything in [documented, Jiang's contrast].
- `giselle-1841`: the village girl destroyed by the social order, here without a supernatural second act.
- `raise-the-red-lantern-2001`: the same company, twenty years later, the patriarchal house as spectacle.
- `mayerling-1978`: the psychological duet as the engine of a literary ballet, in London three years earlier.

### What a maker can take from it
- Find the one small gesture that turns a character (a cup of water, a covered hand); it carries more than a scene of mime.
- Build the strongest unit first. Act II was made, tested and toured before the whole, and it is the part that survived.
- Comedy before catastrophe: the laughing wedding makes the bound bride hit harder.

### Beyond the stage
Lu Xun's story is taught in Chinese schools [probable]; the 1956 film and the Yue opera are its better-known adaptations [probable]. No film of the ballet found in this pass.

## ザ・カブキ / The Kabuki (1986)
`id: the-kabuki-1986` · threads: T05, T18, T13, T14, T16 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 16 April 1986, Tokyo Bunka Kaikan (東京文化会館), Tokyo Ballet (東京バレエ団) [documented, NBS performance record]. Direction and choreography: Maurice Béjart [documented]. Music: Mayuzumi Toshirō (黛敏郎), orchestra, recorded *gidayū* narration, kabuki off-stage (*geza*) music, electronic sound, and the last movement of his *Nirvana Symphony* (涅槃交響曲, 1958) [documented, ja.wikipedia; NBS 2024]. Scenario: condensed by Mayuzumi from the eleven-act puppet and kabuki play *Kanadehon Chūshingura* (仮名手本忠臣蔵, 1748) into "less than one manuscript page" [documented, NBS 2013]. Sets and costumes: Nuno Côrte-Real [documented]. Lighting: Takazawa Tatsuo (高沢立生); sound: Ichikawa Fumitake (市川文武) [documented]. Kabuki movement coach: Hanayagi Yoshijirō (花柳芳次郎, later Hanayagi Juō 花柳壽應) [documented]. Producer: Sasaki Tadatsugu (佐々木忠次) [documented]. Two acts, a prologue and nine scenes, about two hours [documented]. Seen today: the Tokyo Ballet's own staging, its signature work: 213 performances by June 2025, 132 abroad, in 15 countries and 28 cities [documented, Chacott 2025, company figures].

### In one line
A young man in present-day Tokyo takes up a sword, falls into the eighteenth-century play of the 47 *rōnin*, leads their revenge as Yuranosuke, and dies with them in ritual suicide.

### How it came to be
The Tokyo Ballet had asked Béjart for a work for its twentieth anniversary in 1984 [documented, NBS 2013]. In autumn 1983 he agreed and named both the subject, *Chūshingura*, and the composer, Mayuzumi, whose opera on Mishima Yukio's *Kinkakuji* he had heard; he admired Mishima [documented, sound designer Ichikawa, NBS 2013]. Mayuzumi's first reaction, Ichikawa recalls: "*Chūshingura*, of all kabuki plays, the one that is all men" [documented].

Béjart told Mayuzumi "the overall structure is yours". The *gidayū* chanting was recorded first in autumn 1985, then the orchestra, with Béjart present; the tape reached him at the end of December [documented]. He rehearsed in January and March 1986, choreographing fast and changing everything, including Côrte-Real's drawings [documented]. He cut the Yamashina scene for the apocryphal "Farewell in the Snow at Nanbuzaka" and swapped an orchestral number for *geza* music [documented]. The ending changed last: the young man was to return to modern Tokyo, but at a run-through of the mass *seppuku* Béjart wept and ended there [documented, ja.wikipedia citing Sasaki].

### Roots and reference points
- ***Kanadehon Chūshingura*** (Takeda Izumo II, Miyoshi Shōraku, Namiki Senryū, 1748), the most performed play of the puppet and kabuki stage, about the 1701–03 Akō vendetta set in a fourteenth-century disguise [probable]. Characters: Yuranosuke (for Ōishi Kuranosuke), Lord Enya Hangan (Asano), Kō no Moronao (Kira), Lady Kaoyo, Kanpei and Okaru [documented, ja.wikipedia].
- **Béjart's Japan.** Mishima's writing; Buddhism; Béjart saw *seppuku* as "a hymn to the great void" [documented, Béjart creation notes 1986, quoted ja.wikipedia]. He made Kaoyo's dances from his image of Kannon, and enlarged the spy Bannai into an Iago [documented, ja.wikipedia].
- **Shakespeare.** Hangan's ghost appears like Hamlet's father [documented, Unno Satoshi, NBS 2024].
- **Earlier Japanese-Western crossings**: the Matsuyama Ballet's Chinese subject (`white-haired-girl-matsuyama-1955`) is the opposite route [argued, W3 §11].

### The world it built
Prologue, "Present-day Tokyo"; 1, The Helmet; 2, Okaru and Kanpei; 3, The Pine Corridor (Moronao provokes Hangan); 4, Hangan's *seppuku*; 5, Surrender of the castle; 6, Yamazaki Road; 7, The Ichiriki teahouse; 8, Farewell in the snow; 9, The night attack [documented, Chacott 2012]. Act I ends with Yuranosuke's solo of about seven and a half minutes [documented, Chacott 2025]. The movement keeps ballet's pointe work, jumps and turns but takes in kabuki: *suriashi* (sliding feet), deep open-knee stances, the *Genroku mie* pose, the stylised *roppō* walk for the villain Sadakurō, and flexed-foot *attitudes*; Moronao taunts Hangan with *fouetté* turns on a flexed foot [documented, Unno 2024]. Present and past are doubled: a modern Okaru and Kanpei dance beside the historical pair [documented]. The music changes with the realm: light electronic sound and rock for Tokyo, *gidayū* and *shamisen* for the play, orchestra for the big solos, and Buddhist chant from the *Nirvana Symphony* for the death [documented].

### Design and art
Côrte-Real dressed dancers in sweatshirts under *kataginu* shoulder-wings and leotards under trailing *uchikake* robes, so they look Japanese but can still dance [documented, Unno 2024]. Kabuki devices: black-robed stagehands (*kurogo*) who dress the dancers in view, the black-orange-green striped curtain, the *furiotoshi* curtain that drops in a flash; Béjart also asked for a thin curtain "painted with blood" [documented, NBS 2013]. A curtain carries the 47 *iroha* syllables, standing for the 47 *rōnin*; Béjart set them in rows of six, not the usual seven, leaving one blank square, which the technical director reads as "what will you write there?" [documented, NBS 2013]. The prologue's youths were first in jeans, then all in white; Béjart wanted the proscenium "filled with TVs", like Akihabara [documented]. Lighting rule: white for the prologue, even shadowless "kabuki lighting" for the *gidayū* scenes, ballet lighting as the drama rises [documented, Takazawa].

### Premiere and reception
The premiere's Yuranosuke was Eric Vu-An of the Paris Opéra Ballet, alternating with Natsuyama (夏山周久) [documented, ja.wikipedia]; the leading role was given first to a Frenchman of Vietnamese descent [argued as notable]. In *Le Monde*, François Weyergans called the score "Hollywood-style music" that would help when it was televised [documented, quoted ja.wikipedia]. The 1986 European tour took it to Covent Garden, La Scala, the Deutsche Oper Berlin and the Paris Opéra: 33 performances in nine countries [documented, NBS]. The Paris success is said to have opened the Opéra to visiting companies [documented as "it is said", NBS 2024].

### Versions and lineage
One production, kept by its owner company; casting passes from Yuranosuke to Yuranosuke (Takagishi Naoki 高岸直樹 danced it 64 times in 15 countries; Tsukamoto Dan 柄本弾 became the fifth in 2010) [documented]. It was performed at Béjart's memorial in Lausanne (2008), for the 300th anniversary of the vendetta (2002), at the Paris Opéra again (2012), and at the New National Theatre's Opera Palace (2025), where the prologue now includes electric scooters and screens showing the Skytree [documented]. A suite (*Kabuki Suite*) exists [documented]. Excerpts of the score on CD (Nippon Columbia, 1988) [documented].

### Readings and disputes
- **A gift to Japan.** The company and its critics call it "a wonderful gift from Béjart to Japan" that "taught us about a Japan we did not know" [documented, NBS 2010] and the company's "greatest classic" [documented, Ichikawa 2013]. Unno Satoshi (NBS 2024) stresses that Japanese artists co-made it [documented].
- **A Western Japan for export.** Its figures show more performances abroad than at home [documented]. The Paris critic Sanko Hiroshi (Chacott 2012) heard French audiences drawn by the "exotic elements" and puzzled by the plot; he found Béjart's yoga-based vocabulary and the kabuki gestures not fully joined [documented]. The register reads it as "the exotic-other thread from the other side" [argued].
- **Loyalty and death.** The company frames the theme as universal "loyalty" [documented, NBS 2004]. The ending, a glorified mass *seppuku* to Buddhist chant, with Mishima as inspiration (Mishima died by *seppuku* in 1970), carries a nationalist charge [argued]; Mayuzumi was himself a prominent conservative activist [probable].
- **Contradictions.** Premiere date 16 April (NBS) vs "6 April" (Chacott 2012) [contested; NBS is the company's record]. Tour counts vary across NBS pages (31 or 33 in 1986) [contested; suites counted differently].

### Facts worth telling
- Béjart chose the most masculine play in kabuki, to the composer's surprise [documented].
- The music was recorded before a single step was made [documented].
- The ending changed because Béjart cried at a rehearsal [documented].
- One square of the 47-syllable curtain is left blank on purpose [documented, as reading].
- The first Yuranosuke was a Paris Opéra dancer [documented].
- It has been performed more often abroad than in Japan [documented].
- Monitors in the 2025 prologue switch all at once to the national flag as the sword is drawn [documented, Chacott 2025].

### Set it beside
- `white-haired-girl-matsuyama-1955`: a Japanese company takes a Chinese story; here a European takes a Japanese one.
- `bayadere-1877`: the Orientalist route the register pairs it against: but this "other" was made with the culture on the team.
- `rama-hari-1980`: an Asian epic adapted inside Asia, with a singer for each dancer, where Béjart has the chanter on tape.
- `rite-of-spring-1913`: the group's ritual death as climax.

### What a maker can take from it
- Use a frame to let an outsider in: the modern youth who falls into the play is the audience's guide, and Béjart's own position.
- Borrow devices, not just looks: the dropped curtain, visible stagehands and wooden clappers change scenes faster than any set.
- Leave a blank: one empty square on a 47-letter curtain asks the audience to finish the meaning.

## 심청 / Shim Chung (1986)
`id: shim-chung-1986` · threads: T14, T16, T01, T06, T24 · confidence of the header: documented

**Header.** Premiere 21 September 1986, National Theater of Korea (main hall), Seoul, Universal Ballet, a special invited work of the Seoul Asian Games arts festival [documented, Universal Ballet; Encyclopedia of Korean Culture]. Choreography: Adrienne Dellas, the company's American founding artistic director; later choreography by Roy Tobias, Oleg Vinogradov and Yoo Byung-hun (Bingxian Liu) [documented]. Music: Kevin Barber Pickard; the 1986 score was shared among three composers, including Choi Dong-sun, and unified under Pickard in 1988 [documented, Pickard 1987; *Korea Herald* 2026]. Libretto: Park Yong-gu (박용구), dance critic [documented]. Sets: Kim Myung-ho, Hong Yong-han; costumes: Sylvia Taalsohn (Act I), Oleg Vinogradov (Act II), Oh Han-gyul (Act III); lighting Kang Nak-cheon; video Choi Hyo-min [documented, current credits]. Three acts, four scenes, 2 hours 15 minutes [documented]. Title role created by Moon Hoon-sook (Julia H. Moon) [documented]. Seen today: the company's continually revised production (2001 Vinogradov revision; 2010–11 video and design update), revived for its fortieth anniversary in April–May 2026 [documented].

### In one line
A daughter sells herself as a sea sacrifice for 300 bags of rice so that her blind father may see, and returns from the Dragon King's palace on a lotus to become queen.

### How it came to be
Universal Ballet was founded in 1984 by the Korean Cultural Foundation, the arts body of the Unification Church; its chairman was Pak Bo-hi, Moon Hoon-sook's father, and Moon was the church founder's daughter-in-law [documented for the foundation, Encyclopedia of Korean Culture; family ties probable from public record]. Dellas had come to Korea in 1976 to teach ballet at Sunhwa Arts School and "built dancers from the ground up" [documented, *Herald Business* 2026; *Korea Herald* 2026]. "If you are going to go abroad, you need your own colours," she would say [documented, *Korea Herald* 2026].

She found the tale in an English-language children's book and saw in its *hyo* (filial piety) a value the West lacks [documented, *Herald Business* 2026]. Moon says the question was "Why not Korean culture?", like *La Bayadère* for India or *Don Quixote* for Spain [documented]. Pickard, director of the church's Performing Arts Department in New York, wrote in 1987 that "Father himself outlined the theme of this ballet in a logo: *Hyo*", meaning the Rev. Moon Sun-myung, and that the score took about three months [documented, Pickard, tparents.org]. Moon calls the work "a strategic plan" to show Korean culture to foreign visitors at the Asian Games [documented].

### Roots and reference points
- **Simcheong-jeon and the pansori *Simcheong-ga*.** The Joseon-era tale and its sung version, one of the five surviving *pansori* epics: Shim Chung, sold to sailors for 300 sacks of rice offered to a temple, is thrown into the Indangsu sea; the Dragon King sends her back in a lotus; she becomes queen and holds a feast for the blind, where her father regains his sight [probable for pansori history; story documented, Universal Ballet].
- **Petipa's form.** Moon: Dellas built it "exactly in the form of Petipa's classical ballets, like *Swan Lake* or *The Sleeping Beauty*"; "an analogue work", not technically new [documented, *Chum Webzine* interview, 2017].
- **Korean court and folk arts.** Mask dance (*talchum*) ensembles in Act III; court ceremonial robes (*daeryebok*) for the bride-selection scene, choreographed by Moon herself, who trained in Korean dance [documented, Seoul Shinmun 2012; *Herald Business* 2026].
- **Cut from the source**: the scheming widow Ppaengdeok, a comic villain of the pansori, is absent [documented, *Herald Business* 2026].

### The world it built
Act I compresses birth, the mother's death, childhood, the bargain and the voyage; twelve sailors fill the stage with *grand jetés* in a storm; after a pas de deux with the captain, Shim Chung throws herself off the ship's side [documented, Seoul Shinmun 2012; *The Leader* 2019]. Act II is the underwater palace: sea creatures in glittering scales and spikes, the Dragon King's duet with Shim Chung, a waltz solo; she refuses his proposal and hides in a lotus [documented, *The Leader* 2019]. Act III is the Joseon court: she arrives in a lotus palanquin under painted *dancheong* eaves, the king proposes, a moonlit court pas de deux, then the feast for the blind and the reunion [documented]. All movement is "rigorously based on classical ballet steps", dressed in ballet-cut *hanbok* [documented, company Korean page]. The three acts, Moon says, are "spectacle, drama, elegance" [documented].

### Design and art
The 2000s Act II tights were sheer silver scales, so thin, one dancer laughed, that "we were busy covering ourselves" [documented, Kang Mi-sun, *Herald Business* 2026]. Paris spectators in 2012 compared Act II to "a Botticelli painting" [documented, Seoul Shinmun]. In the storm the great sails on both sides hang from battens that rise and fall like waves; since 2010 a projected film shows Shim Chung sinking through water at the start of Act II [documented]. Act III uses the multicoloured *dancheong* of palace roofs and the bride's ceremonial robe [documented]. A 3-metre leap from the ship's side caused an accident in 1988: when the two lead dancers refused to jump without a net, Pak Bo-hi jumped himself, broke his glasses and cracked a rib; a safety net followed [documented, Moon, *Chum Webzine* 2017].

### Premiere and reception
The company's 1986 season had 28 Seoul performances and three on tour [documented, company record]. The work was shown again for the 1988 Seoul Olympics [documented]. The first foreign performance was in 1987 (company Korean page) or at New York City Center in 1989 (Moon, 2017) [contested]. In 2001 it became the earliest Korean ballet at the Kennedy Center, Lincoln Center and the Los Angeles Music Center; Jennifer Dunning of the *New York Times* wrote that it touched the audience "in an age when dance's basic humanity is being lost" [documented in Korean translation, Encyclopedia of Korean Culture; English original not found in this pass]. The 2012 Paris run at the Palais des Congrès sold at 94.3 per cent occupancy [documented, Seoul Shinmun, company figure]. By 2016: more than 200 performances in some 40 cities in 12 to 15 countries [documented, company figures vary].

### Versions and lineage
- 1986: Dellas premiere; 1988: score unified under Pickard [documented].
- 2001: Vinogradov (former Kirov director) revises scenes and dances "to move foreign audiences used to classical ballet"; adds the bride-selection scene; redesigns Act II costumes [documented, Encyclopedia of Korean Culture; *Herald Business*].
- 2007: a ballet-musical version for families [documented].
- 2010–11: underwater video; worn sets and costumes renewed before a world tour (Moscow, Paris, Oman's Royal Opera House Muscat, Japan, Taiwan, Singapore, North America) [documented].
- 2016: thirtieth anniversary, five former Shim Chungs as the older narrator [documented, *Korea Times*].
- 2026: fortieth anniversary revival [documented, *Korea Herald*].
A 2004 DVD exists (Spectrum DVD) [documented].

### Readings and disputes
- **A Korean classic.** Shim Jung-min (Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, 2014) calls it a creative ballet "harmonising the beauty of East and West" [documented]. The 2017 Seoul Arts Center grand prize confirmed its canonical status [documented].
- **A Petipa ballet in hanbok.** Moon herself grants that it is "not groundbreaking"; the national content sits inside a nineteenth-century Russian frame, revised by a Russian director for Western audiences [documented, her words; reading argued].
- **Patronage and message.** Pickard's account makes the church founder the author of its theme of filial piety; the register asks that the patronage be shown on the card [documented, Pickard 1987; register note]. Whether *hyo* is a Korean national value or also a religious one depends on the reader [argued].
- **A daughter's sacrifice.** Seen beside T24 (women against the patriarch), the story rewards a daughter who gives her life for her father; the company presents it as devotion, not oppression [argued; company framing documented].
- **Contradictions.** First tour 1987 vs 1989; 12 vs 15 countries; three composers vs two named [contested].

### Facts worth telling
- The idea came from an English children's book [documented].
- It was paid for by the Unification Church's cultural foundation, and the church founder's daughter-in-law danced the lead [foundation documented; family tie probable].
- Her father jumped off the 3-metre ship to prove it safe and cracked a rib [documented, Moon].
- The scheming stepmother of the Korean original was cut [documented].
- Five Shim Chungs danced at once at the thirtieth anniversary [documented].
- It reached the Middle East at the opening festival of Muscat's Royal Opera House [documented, company].

### Set it beside
- `fish-beauty-1959`: an Asian heroine passes through an undersea court; Beijing's under Soviet guidance, Seoul's under American and later Russian.
- `bayadere-1877`: the model Moon names: Petipa's "Indian" ballet, here answered with Korea's own story.
- `sylphide-1832`: the two worlds, crossed; but Shim Chung returns and marries.
- `rite-of-spring-1913`: the chosen girl given to save the group, here rewarded by heaven.

### What a maker can take from it
- Pick one value and build every scene to show it; *hyo* gave a clear spine that audiences understood without the language.
- Plan to revise. The work stayed alive because each director, designer and decade changed it.
- Know the frame you borrow: a Petipa structure makes a story legible abroad, but it can also make it look like everyone else's.

### Beyond the stage
