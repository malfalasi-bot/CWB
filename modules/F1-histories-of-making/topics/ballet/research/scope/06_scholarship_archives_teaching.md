# Ballet scope 06: the scholarly base, archives, open sources, and how ballet is taught

Angle: (a) the canon of ballet scholarship and which of it is open access; (b) the archives that hold ballet's record and which publish open material; (c) open-image sources and the "Degas problem"; (d) how ballet history is taught in dance, theatre-design and design-history courses; (e) sizing the whole for a foundation-year design student.

Status key: **[D]** documented (seen at source during this session), **[P]** probable (reliable secondary, not checked at primary), **[C]** contested, **[A]** argued (an author's interpretation, attributed, or mine where marked). OA = open access.

## 0. In one paragraph

Ballet's scholarly base is large, mostly behind paywalls, and centred on four cities (Paris, St Petersburg, London, New York). What a foundation design student can read for free is smaller but good: the primary treatises (Feuillet 1700, Noverre 1760, Blasis 1828) are fully open at the Library of Congress and archive.org; there is a short, sharp open teaching text (Järvinen, Uniarts Helsinki); a handful of open-access monographs and articles (Wilcox 2018; Leon 2022; Collins and Jarvis 2016; several *Dance Research Journal* articles since 2022); and free curatorial essays (Met, V&A, Harvard). Open images are plentiful for Degas and the Romantic lithograph, and scarce for the people who made the shoes, skirts and scenery. That imbalance is itself the best teaching material this angle offers. Licences are a trap: Gallica, the V&A and the Royal Ballet and Opera are not open-licensed; the Met, NGA, NYPL, LoC and Harvard (for public-domain items) are.

## 2. The concepts

| Concept | Who argues what, where |
|---|---|
| **Ballet as ethnic dance** | Kealiinohomoku (1969/70) argues ballet is an ethnic dance like any other; Durr (JASHM, from a 1985 NYU thesis) accepts it and specifies an "aristocratic" ethnicity of European nobility [D]. UC Santa Cruz THEA 166 builds a whole course on the claim [D]. [A] For F1: the claim opens the web and stops ballet being the unmarked norm. |
| **Ballet as plural, not unified** | Järvinen: "not a unified form of dance in terms of technique, aesthetics or even its audiences"; its place in dance education is "a legacy of European colonialism" [D]. Kant: ballet is "much loved but little studied" [D]. |
| **Canon formation** | Genné, "Creating a Canon, Creating the 'Classics' in Twentieth-Century British Ballet", *Dance Research* (2000) [D title]; Fisher on *Nutcracker* as "cultural icon" (Companion ch. 21) [D]. Homans organises history by national style and closes with "The Masters Are Dead and Gone", a decline argument [P; C]. |
| **The ephemeral and the record** | The DNB says its scores "kept dances from disappearing" [D, 990 narrative]. Against it: no notation fixes a dance; reconstructions from Stepanov scores are themselves new works [A; C]. Aberdeen's MU4085 asks about "artistic confection and authenticity" [D]. |
| **Authorship of a ballet** | Aberdeen lists each ballet as composer/choreographer/designer [D]; the Met's Speelberg shows "Bakst" designs circulated as workshop copies by assistants using transfer paper [D]; Feuillet published a notation commissioned from Beauchamps [D LoC essay; dispute C]. |
| **Costume and choreography co-dependent** | Collins & Jarvis (2016): the two "co-dependent, each prompting the other to develop and evolve" [D]. Chazin-Bennahum (2005): ballet and street fashion exchange, and ballet shapes women's body image [D]. |
| **Ballet as tastemaker** | Davis (2010): Ballets Russes as "a laboratory" for fashion, interiors, advertising [D]. Bolton & Koda: Poiret's Orientalist "fantasies of the Orient" obscured his technical and marketing work [D]. Who influenced whom (Poiret or Bakst) is contested [C]. |
| **Choreography beyond bodies** | Leon (2022): choreography "transcending its sole association with dancing, moving, human bodies", court ballet to Forsythe's choreographic objects [D]. Useful bridge for design students: choreography as arrangement of objects and space. |
| **The pointe shoe as invented tradition** | Lawry (2022): pointe shoes are "morphological" and constantly reinvented; makers, dancers and choreographers "commodified and mystified" them, linking pain to value; the dispute over Gaynor Minden is about exclusivity [D]. |
| **Orientalism, self-exoticism, racialised objects** | Davidson RUS 293: "self-exoticizing trends of the early Ballets Russes" [D]; Mandradjieff (2024): *Coppélia*'s automata "the Negro", "the Moor", "the Chinaman" and their restagings [D]. |
| **Sinicisation and revolutionary ballet** | Zheng Yangwen (Companion ch. 22) "ballet's sinicisation" [D]; Wilcox (2018) contextualises revolutionary ballet "from within" [D]; Cui (2025) the post-1980s return of the tutu'd ideal under state cosmopolitanism [D]. |
| **Gender: "ballet is a woman"** | The Companion titles Part III "Romantic ballet: ballet is a woman" [D] (a phrase associated with Balanchine [P]). Garafola (1985) on women dancing men's roles [D]. Kant's Penn course asks "When and why do women become the heroines of ballet narratives?" [D]. |
| **The painter's view of the dancer** | Lipton (1986): class and sex in Degas's dancers [D]; McCauley (1989) calls the proof "shaky" [D]; Kendall & DeVonyar (2011): Degas, photography and film [D]. [A] For F1 the key point is not Degas's morals but that his images, being open, stand in for the art form. |

## 3. People and makers: named and unnamed (from this angle)

**Named in the record.** Treatise authors and notators: Raoul-Auger Feuillet, Pierre Beauchamps, John Weaver, Jean-Georges Noverre, Carlo Blasis, Vladimir Stepanov, Nikolai Sergeyev, Rudolf Laban, Ann Hutchinson Guest, Rudolf Benesh. Designers: Paul Lormier (*Giselle* 1841), Léon Bakst, Alexandre Benois, Natalia Goncharova, Roger Furse, Ann Church. Collectors and archive-founders: Aleksei Bakhrushin (1894), Jerome Robbins (film gift 1965), Mona Inglesby (sold the Sergeyev papers), Elizabeth Aldrich (LoC curator), Theresa Ruth Howard (MoBBallet, founded 2015) [D for these]. Scholars: Homans, Kant, Garafola, Scholl, Guest, Au, Kirstein, Chazin-Bennahum, Wiley, Smith, Wilcox.

**Named but treated as subject, not maker.** The dancers in Degas: Rosita Mauri in *L'Étoile* (Orsay, as catalogued on Commons) [D/P]; Marie van Goethem, model for the *Little Dancer* [P]. They are named as sitters, not as workers.

**Not named.** The costume-makers who for the first Ballets Russes season "would scour market stalls for ikat fabrics" [P]; Bakst's workshop assistants who made transfer-paper copies [D]; the copyists of Stepanov scores; the scene-painting and wardrobe staff of the Opéra and the Imperial Theatres; the photographers of company photo archives (often studio names only); Black ballet dancers whose careers MoBBallet's "Roll Call" sets out to restore [D]; the cataloguers whose finding aids decide what is searchable. [A] The archive names the designer on the drawing and the dancer in the photograph; the maker of the object between them appears, if at all, in a wardrobe ledger nobody has digitised.

## 5. How it is taught elsewhere

Seven courses and one textbook pattern were found and read at source [D unless marked]:

1. **UC Santa Cruz THEA 166 "Ballet: A History"** (Edward Warburton). Ballet "as a form of ethnic dance"; a "ballet diaspora" of Russian émigrés to the Americas and Asia; China, Cuba, US. Reads Homans plus two readings on ballet in Cuba and China. Students: quizzes, journal, response paper, midterm, studio performance, oral presentation and a wiki project.
2. **University of Pennsylvania ENGL 250 "19th-Century Romantic Ballet"** (Marion Kant, 2007). Libretti from five national traditions; Heine and Gautier; gender questions. Reads the *Cambridge Companion* plus a packet. Students: present literature, analyse a filmed 19th-c. choreography for gender, write a book review.
3. **Ball State DANC 302 "Dance History 2"** (Jennifer Meckley). Ballet and modern dance "as global entities". Reads Jack Anderson, *Ballet & Modern Dance: A Concise History* (3rd ed. 2018) and Foulkes, *Modern Bodies* (2002). Students: weekly podcasts on the readings, and a staged research paper (proposal, annotated bibliography, outline, draft) using only library-vetted sources.
4. **Swarthmore DANC 002A first-year seminar** (1789–1960): film, text, performance; Petipa, Balanchine, Dunham, Graham; a writing course.
5. **University of Aberdeen MU4085 "The Ballets Russes: Music, Art and Movement"**: ten ballets, each a named collaboration; "confection and authenticity"; gender, sexuality, politics, nationality.
6. **Davidson RUS 293 "Performing Russia: The Ballets Russes"**: Diaghilev as "master curator"; Russianness, self-exoticism, race and gender; readings across dance studies, musicology, art history.
7. **Bennington "Design: The Ballets Russes"** (Charles Schoonmaker, 2015): the only course found taught from the design side: designers Bakst, Benois, Goncharova, Picasso; "Students will do design projects and research projects."

**How design and fashion history frame ballet.** In design and fashion-history courses ballet appears once, as the Ballets Russes in a session on Orientalism and the 1910s: Masaryk University's 20th-century fashion course pairs "Ballets russes, Duncan, Paul Poiret, Mariano Fortuny" under "Passion for the Orient" [D]; the Met's Poiret essay and FIT's *Fashion History Timeline* both tell *Schéhérazade* (1910) as the trigger of a fashion "craze" [D]. Theatre-design history courses (FAU THE 4283; Ca' Foscari FT0441; JAMU Brno; Zagreb ADU) teach period style, theatre architecture and scenography; ballet appears as "Diaghilev's ballets" beside Appia, Craig and the Bauhaus, or not at all [D]. Their standard readings: Brockett, Mitchell & Hardberger, *Making the Scene* (2010); Aronson ed., *Routledge Companion to Scenography* (2017); Aronson, *Fifty Key Theatre Designers* (2024), which includes Ciceri, Bakst and Benois, Exter, Schlemmer, Popova [D]. A broad art-and-design survey such as Pratt's HAD catalogue lists no ballet at all [D]. [A] Pattern: dance courses teach ballet's works and politics; design courses teach the Ballets Russes as a style source. Neither teaches ballet's making (workshops, materials, supply), which is where F1 can add something.

**What they ask students to do**, in order of usefulness for F1: analyse one filmed production for one question (Penn); build a staged research paper with an annotated bibliography (Ball State); one ballet = one collaboration (Aberdeen); design project from a historical design (Bennington); public-facing output (UCSC wiki; Ball State podcast).

## 6. Gaps and traps

1. **"Free to see" is not "free to reuse."** Gallica (NC; commercial paid), V&A (NC, 768 px), Royal Ballet and Opera (ArenaPAL, commercial), Jacob's Pillow (no download) are not open licences [D]. Only the Met, NGA, NYPL (PD items), LoC (most PD items), Harvard (PD reproductions) and Smithsonian (marked items) are reliably open [D].
2. **The Degas problem is a licensing problem as much as an aesthetic one.** Open-access policies release public-domain paintings first; workshop photographs, wardrobe records and 20th-century company photography are in copyright or undigitised. So the open web over-represents the painter's view of the dancer and under-represents the makers [A]. Degas's dancers had names (Mauri, van Goethem) and were workers in an institution; the picture is not the record [P/A].
3. **"First" claims.** Feuillet "first published a system in 1700" (LoC) but the commission was Beauchamps's and the priority was disputed [C]. Homans's book is promoted as "the first cultural history of ballet ever written" [P: blurb as reproduced by Ballet Arizona, https://balletaz.org/5-ballet-books-to-add-to-your-reading-list; C]. Say "earliest known published".
4. **The notation is not the dance.** The Sergeyev scores cover 24 ballets and 24 opera ballets, with gaps; they were unreadable until Stepanov's primer was found [P]; reconstructions from them (1999 Mariinsky *Sleeping Beauty*; Ratmansky) are contested as "authentic" [C].
5. **The "Bakst" drawing may not be by Bakst.** Workshop copies on transfer paper circulated [D, Met]. Attribution on catalogue records should be read with care.
6. **The scholarly base is four-city and mostly paywalled.** Open-access work on ballet outside Europe exists (Wilcox; Cui; DRJ on Masilo) and should be preferred, which also serves the Accessibility thread [A].
7. **Survey framings carry arguments.** Homans's national-style chapters and decline epilogue [P; C]; the Companion's "ballet is a woman" [D]. Teach them as positions.
8. **Design-history courses reduce ballet to Orientalist fashion.** Poiret-versus-Bakst priority is contested [C]; the makers of the costumes (and the market-stall ikat [P]) drop out.
9. **Transliteration.** Sergeyev/Sergeev, Bakhrushin/Bakhrouchine, Stepanov/Stépanov: pick one and give variants, because catalogue searches depend on it.
10. **Gaps not closed this session.** Europeana and Rijksmuseum ballet holdings; Royal Danish Theatre/Bournonville archive online; Bakhrushin reuse terms; whether the JASHM reprint of Kealiinohomoku is in the free back-issue archive (JASHM back issues are OA [D], specific issue not opened); OA status of OpenEdition's Wild catalogue; Japanese ballet history sources (none gathered).

## 7. Size

**This angle on its own** (scholarship, archives, licences, how ballet is taught) deserves about **2 to 3 hours** inside a ballet unit: one taught session on "finding and reusing ballet's record" plus a short independent task. It is method more than content, and it serves every other angle.

**The whole of ballet in one unit (8–12 hours).** A foundation design student can learn: that ballet is a made thing with makers; how its record works (paper, objects, images, licences); two or three objects closely (shoe, tutu, a design drawing); one work re-made; one global strand. Proposed **10 hours as five 2-hour sessions**, following the sibling spine C → A → B and adding the archive as a frame. Each session rests on at least one free reading.

| Session | Question | Core reading (OA marked) | Activity |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Whose ballet? | What do we think ballet looks like, and where did that picture come from? | Järvinen, "A Cultural History of Ballet" **OA**; Kealiinohomoku (1969/70) (via *JASHM* reprint or a reader); Met *Little Dancer* conservation essay **free** | Image census: search two open collections (Met, Commons) for "ballet"; count painter's images against images of making. Write 150 words on what the open web hides. |
| 2. The bench (Spine C) | Who makes the shoe, and why don't we know their names? | Lawry, *Dress* 48.1 (2022) (library); sibling Spine C materials | Object reading of a pointe shoe; attribution statement for a maker's mark. |
| 3. The body and its paper (Spine A) | How was movement written down and taught? | Feuillet 1700 (LoC) **OA**; Noverre 1760, one letter (archive.org) **OA**; Hammond, "The rise of ballet technique and training" (Companion ch. 6) | Read a Feuillet track drawing as a design drawing; compare with a modern technical drawing. |
| 4. Costume as engineering and fashion | How do costume and step shape each other, and how did ballet leave the stage? | Collins & Jarvis (2016) **OA**; Bolton & Koda, Poiret (Met) **free**; Speelberg, Bakst (Met) **free** | Compare a Lormier *Giselle* design (Gallica) with a surviving costume record (V&A); note what the drawing omits that a maker needed. |
| 5. One work re-made (Spine B) and the archive | What survives of a ballet, and who decides? | Fullington interview on Stepanov notation **free**; Wilcox, "A Revolt from Within" (Luminos) **OA** | Write a 300-word museum label for one object with holder, maker (named or "unrecorded"), date, and licence; the unit's assessed output. |

**A module (30–40 hours, 10–12 sessions)** could add: court ballet and Louis XIV's academy; the Paris Opéra as workplace (Garafola 1985; Smith 2000); Imperial Russia and Petipa (Companion ch. 13; Scholl); the Ballets Russes as a design event across fashion and interiors (Davis 2010); Soviet and Chinese revolutionary ballet (Wilcox; Zheng); Cuba (Tomé); America and the Africanist presence (Dixon Gottschild [P]); Britain and canon formation (Genné 2000); the stage machine and lighting (sibling 04); choreography beyond bodies (Leon 2022 **OA**); dance medicine and the body as instrument (JDMS).

**What one page can carry:** the Degas problem as a single argued page, with three open images (a Degas from the Met, a Lormier design from Gallica, a maker's-mark shoe) and one licence note each. **One session:** session 1 above. **One unit:** the five sessions above.

Also archive.org scans from York University Libraries (Toronto), full PDF/EPUB/text download: 1760 ed. https://archive.org/details/lettressurladans00noveuoft ; 1803 St Petersburg ed. vol. 1 https://archive.org/details/lettressurladans01noveuoft and vol. 2 https://archive.org/details/lettressurladans02noveuoft (vol. 3 of 1803 has *Observations sur la construction d'une salle d'opéra*: a theatre-architecture text by a choreographer, useful for a design course) [D]. The English Beaumont translation (Dance Horizons 1975 printing) is on archive.org but access-restricted (lending only): https://archive.org/details/lettersondancing0000nove [D].
- Blasis, *The Code of Terpsichore*, trans. R. Barton (London: Bull, 1828/1830 issue), Google-scanned, full text on archive.org: https://archive.org/stream/codeterpsichore00blasgoog/codeterpsichore00blasgoog_djvu.txt [D]. NYPL catalogue note: the English 1828 edition preceded the full French publication (1830) [D, NYPL record https://www.nypl.org/research/research-catalog/bib/b10487240].
- Feuillet, *Chorégraphie* (Paris, 1700; LoC copy is the 1713 Dezais issue, bound with *Recueil de dances* 1709 incl. Pecour): https://www.loc.gov/item/14002127 [D]. LoC summary: notation indicates foot placement and six basic movements; bar lines match the music; Weaver's English translation 1706 [D]. LoC essay says Beauchamps had the commission but Feuillet "first published a system in 1700" [D; note priority dispute Beauchamps v. Feuillet: contested, Beauchamps sued 1704 (P)].
- LoC collection *An American Ballroom Companion: Dance Instruction Manuals, ca. 1490–1920* (200+ manuals, 75 videos; from Music Division, Rare Book, General Collections): https://www.loc.gov/collections/dance-instruction-manuals-from-1490-to-1920/about-this-collection [D]. Mostly social dance, but includes Feuillet, Weaver, Noverre-adjacent material.
- No Project Gutenberg edition of Noverre, Blasis or Feuillet was found in this pass [P: absence not proved]. archive.org and LoC are the open routes.

### Log 4: archives (second pass)
- NYPL Jerome Robbins Dance Division: founded 1944; calls itself "the largest and most comprehensive archive in the world devoted to the documentation of dance"; >44,000 books, >1 million manuscript items, >25,000 moving-image titles, >4,000 audio recordings, >500 oral histories, footage of >2,600 performances; prints and photographs from the 17th century; moving-image archive began with Jerome Robbins's gift of six cans of film in 1965 [D: https://globallib.nypl.org/about/divisions/jerome-robbins-dance-division]. Digitised sets on NYPL Digital Collections include "Prints Depicting Dance", Denishawn, Cunningham, Baryshnikov archive [D same page]. Thousands of hours of video digitised 2013–14, but much viewable only in the Lincoln Center reading room [D: Hyperallergic 1 Apr 2014, https://hyperallergic.com/new-york-public-library-puts-major-dance-video-archive-online.md]. NYPL Digital Collections flags public-domain items for free download [P: policy since 2016, not re-checked this session].
- V&A Theatre and Performance: example object, Roger Furse costume design for *Adam Zero* (Sadler's Wells Ballet, ROH, 10 April 1946), S.2142-1986, "Given by the British Council" [D: https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O1221188/costume-design-furse-roger]. Image terms on that page: JPG download for non-commercial research, teaching and instruction, non-commercial online use up to 768 px for five years; not an open licence (not CC) [D]. Many T&P items are "view by appointment" (Study Rooms, V&A East Storehouse from 2025 [P]).
- Royal Ballet and Opera Collections (formerly ROH Collections): "the largest theatre archive still housed within its own theatre in the United Kingdom"; admin records, performance photographs, recordings, costumes, wigs, props, designs, playbills from the 18th c.; >13,000 performances in the database; most complete from 1946; online Collections Catalogue and Performance Database at rohcollections.org.uk; image licensing via ArenaPAL (commercial agency, not open) [D: https://www.rbo.org.uk/about/royal-ballet-and-opera-archive-and-collections].
- Harvard Theatre Collection (Houghton Library): founded 1901, "one of the oldest and largest performing arts collections in the world"; theatre, dance and ballet, music, opera, popular entertainment; portraits, stage and costume designs, prints, photographs [D: https://library.harvard.edu/collections/harvard-theatre-collection]. "Russian Theatrical Designs" collection: Ballets Russes designs by Anisfeld, Bakst, Benois, Bilinsky, Doboujinsky, Golovin, Goncharova, Korovine, Larionov, Roerich, Soudeikine, Tchelitchev, Exter; online exhibition *Diaghilev's Ballets Russes 1909–1929*; reading-room access [D: https://library.harvard.edu/collections/russian-theatrical-designs]. Exhibition catalogue: Penelope D. Hull comp., *The Romantic Ballet: Catalogue of an Exhibition, Harvard Theatre Collection* (1966) [D, Google Books record].
- Bakhrushin State Central Theatre Museum, Moscow: founded 1894 by Aleksei Bakhrushin; >1.5 million items [P: Wikipedia, art-katalog]. Online collection at https://collectiononline.gctm.ru/en : "9 collections, 88530 objects" at time of search [D: search snippet]. Reuse licence not established; Russian state museum images are generally not openly licensed [P]. Sanctions and access: current conditions make image licensing from Russian museums impractical for a UK/UAE course [A].
- Jacob's Pillow Dance Interactive: hundreds of performance excerpts filmed at the Pillow from the 1930s, browsable by artist/era/genre, essays and playlists [D: https://www.jacobspillow.org/dance-interactive]. Terms (2011 capture): share via embed only, "downloading or other reproduction in any medium is strictly prohibited"; fair-use counsel Peter Jaszi; full-length videos viewable on site [D: Wayback capture https://web.archive.org/web/20120324054106/http://danceinteractive.jacobspillow.org/about]. Streamable for teaching, not reusable.
- Numeridanse (Maison de la Danse, Lyon): free video platform for dance [D: https://numeridanse.com/en/about/who-we-are]; includes EVDH (European Video Dance Heritage) collection [D]. Streaming, not open licence [P].
- Dance Notation Bureau (New York): 872 Labanotation scores by 302 choreographers incl. ### Log 5: more archives and open sources
- Sergeyev Collection (Harvard Theatre Collection, Houghton): Stepanov-notation scores of the Imperial (Mariinsky) repertory brought out of Russia by Nikolai Sergeyev after 1917; *Swan Lake* notations sold to Harvard 1967, the rest 1969 by Mona Inglesby via dealer Ifan Kyrle Fletcher, for a sum "claimed to be around £6,000"; finding aids MS Thr 245 (1888–1944) and MS Thr 186 (*Swan Lake* 1905–24); Wikipedia says Harvard digitised it in 2024 [P: Wikipedia https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergeyev_Collection ; Petipa Society https://petipasociety.com/the-sergeyev-collection ]. Doug Fullington: "24 ballets and 24 opera ballets" in the collection [D: DanceTabs interview 2015 https://dancetabs.com/2015/06/doug-fullington-on-stepanov-notation]. Key article: Roland John Wiley, "Dances from Russia: An Introduction to the Sergeyev Collection", *Harvard Library Bulletin* 24.1 (Jan 1976) [P]. Harvard research guide confirms Stepanov scores preserved Petipa's choreography for *Swan Lake* and *Nutcracker* and are held at HTC; guide itself is CC BY 4.0 [D: https://guides.library.harvard.edu/c.php?g=1294023&p=9509243]. Uses: Wright/Wiley *Nutcracker* (Royal Ballet 1984); Vikharev *Sleeping Beauty* (Mariinsky 1999); Ratmansky *Paquita* (Munich 2014) and *Sleeping Beauty* (ABT 2015) [P].
- Harvard Library policy: waives any copyright in openly available digital reproductions of public-domain works; "may be used by anyone for any purpose" [D: https://library.harvard.edu/about/policies/policy-access-digital-reproductions-works-public-domain]. So Harvard Theatre Collection PD scans are effectively open (not CC0 by label, but a waiver).
- Australian Performing Arts Collection (Arts Centre Melbourne): >780,000 items incl. costumes, designs, set models, props, photographs, programmes; ballet holdings incl. Ann Church designs (gift of estate 2017), Australian Ballet costumes; "Reveal" open-storage project 2023 [P: PGAV news https://pgav.org.au/Arts-Centre-Melbourne-s-Australian-Performing-Arts-Collection-to-open-to-the-pub~9183 ; APAC blog https://performingartscollection.wordpress.com/tag/the-australian-ballet ]. The National Gallery of Australia (Canberra), separately, holds one of the largest Ballets Russes costume collections, acquired from 1973; exhibition *Ballets Russes: the Art of Costume* (2010–11) [P: Limelight https://limelight-arts.com.au/news/exhibition-extended-ballets-russes-costumes-remain-on-display].
- Royal Danish Library (KB) theatre collection: national archive for staged drama incl. dance company archives, manuscripts, photos, programmes; digitised images searchable in KB search [D: https://www.kb.dk/en/find-materials/guides/acting-and-performing-arts]. Bournonville portrait (photo Emil Lange) credited Royal Library, public domain [P: nordics.info]. Bournonville's own archive/notations and the Royal Danish Theatre's archive need a direct check [gap].
- Met: *Little Dancer Aged Fourteen* original wax is at NGA Washington (1999.80.28), materials: "pigmented beeswax, clay, metal armature, rope, paintbrushes, human hair, silk and linen ribbon, cotton faille bodice, cotton and silk tutu, linen slippers" [D: Met conservation story 2020 https://www.metmuseum.org/about-the-met/conservation-and-scientific-research/conservation-stories/2020/degas-dancer]. Met's bronze cast is the 1922 posthumous cast; the "series of custom-fitted skirts" shows the tutu is replaced, a made object within a made object [D same]. NGA object page links its Open Access policy: https://www.nga.gov/artworks/110292-little-dancer-aged-fourteen [D].
- Cooper Hewitt: collection data CC0; images under Smithsonian Open Access where marked [D: https://www.cooperhewitt.org/open-source-at-cooper-hewitt ; https://www.cooperhewitt.org/2020/05/28/discover-smithsonian-open-access-with-treasures-from-the-cooper-hewitt-collection].
- OPEN TEACHING TEXT FOUND: Hanna Järvinen, "A Cultural History of Ballet – Five Centuries of a European Art Form", in the Uniarts Helsinki (TeaK) open online book *Näkökulmia tanssitaiteen historiaan ja nykypäivään* (Perspectives on the history and present of dance art), English version [D: https://disco.teak.fi/tanssin-historia/en/a-cultural-history-of-ballet-five-centuries-of-a-european-art-form]. Argues ballet is "not a unified form of dance in terms of technique, aesthetics or even its audiences" and names ballet's place in dance education as "a legacy of European colonialism" [D]. Free to read; licence to check. Best single open reading for a foundation student.

### Log 9: the Degas problem and open images
