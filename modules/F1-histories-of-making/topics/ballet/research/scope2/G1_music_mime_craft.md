## 1. The knowledge map

### Tier 1: must know

**1.1 The score is the world's clock, and it is written to a specification.**
*What.* A 19th-century ballet score is a chain of numbers (scène, pas, variation, coda, divertissement, apotheosis) whose length, tempo and character were set by the ballet master before a note was written. Petipa gave Tchaikovsky "extremely detailed instructions … down to the tempo and number of bars" for *The Nutcracker* [D, Wikipedia summarising Wiley]; the *Sleeping Beauty* plan is the best-known example (W1 holds the document) [D in W1].
*Why it matters here.* The commission letter to a composer is a model brief: it fixes duration, pace, entrances and structure, and leaves the composer the invention inside them. It is the clearest real case of the Concept DNA's **obligations** field.
*Sources.* Roland John Wiley, *Tchaikovsky's Ballets* (Clarendon, 1985), with the plans in translation; Nadine Meisner, *Marius Petipa: The Emperor's Ballet Master* (OUP, 2019); Tim Scholl, *From Petipa to Balanchine* (Routledge, 1994).

**1.2 Two kinds of ballet composer: the staff specialist and the symphonist.**
*What.* From about 1830 to 1900 most ballet music was written by salaried specialists who composed fast and to order: Cesare Pugni (St Petersburg's official composer of ballet music from 1850s to his death in 1870; "over 100 known original ballet scores", often a multi-act ballet "in just a few days") [D, Wikipedia], Ludwig Minkus (official ballet composer of the Imperial Theatres 1871–86) [D in W1], Riccardo Drigo (principal ballet conductor at the Mariinsky from 1886 and composer of *Le Talisman*, *Le Réveil de Flore*, *Les Millions d'Arlequin*) [D, Tchaikovsky Research]. Adolphe Adam in Paris wrote opera and ballet alike. Against them stand composers whose scores changed what a ballet could carry: Delibes (*Coppélia* 1870, *Sylvia* 1876), Tchaikovsky (1877, 1890, 1892), then Stravinsky and Prokofiev. In 1877 Moscow critics found *Swan Lake* "too noisy, too 'Wagnerian' and too symphonic" [D, as quoted on Wikipedia].
*Why.* The specialist's score is service design: it is built to be danced, cut, extended and re-used. The symphonist's score is a world-defining anchor. A briefer needs to know which one they are commissioning.
*Sources.* Marian Smith, *Ballet and Opera in the Age of "Giselle"* (Princeton UP, 2000); Wiley (1985); Stephanie Jordan, *Moving Music: Dialogues with Music in Twentieth-Century Ballet* (Dance Books, 2000).

**1.3 The score a company plays is an edited object.**
*What.* Conductors, arrangers and editors re-make ballet music. Drigo's 1895 *Swan Lake* cut whole numbers, reordered the swan dances, built the Black Swan pas de deux from an 1877 pas de deux "for two merry-makers" with a new ending of his own, and orchestrated three of Tchaikovsky's Op. 72 piano pieces into Act III ("L'Espiègle" became Odile's variation) [D, Wikipedia "Swan Lake (1895)"; Tchaikovsky Research]. John Lanchbery's *La Fille mal gardée* (1960) is Hérold's 1828 score as a foundation, plus 1789 material, one Hertel number (the Clog Dance) and new Lanchbery numbers [D, Wikipedia]. A 2026 Bärenreiter critical edition of *Giselle* (eds Doug Fullington and Marian Smith) works from Adam's autograph, original parts and piano reductions, and still prints the "traditional interpolations" [D, EAMDC].
*Why.* A world is maintained by editors as well as made by authors. "Which version?" is the first question for any revival, adaptation or licence.
*Sources.* Wiley (1985); Ivor Guest, *La Fille mal gardée* (Dancing Times, 1960); Fullington and Smith (eds), *Giselle* (Bärenreiter BA08819).

**1.4 Mime is a designed sign language, and its legibility was negotiated with audiences.**
*What.* Ballet mime is a code of conventional gestures (hand on heart for love, pointing to the ring finger for marriage, hands circling above the head for dance, crossed wrists lowered for death, the hand drawn across the face for beauty) used in set scenes: Odette's story to Siegfried, Berthe telling of the Wilis in *Giselle*, the Lilac Fairy against Carabosse [P: the standard repertory; gesture lists are company-specific]. Audiences learned it through the printed livret and programme synopsis, through repetition across ballets, and in Paris through *airs parlants*: tunes the audience already knew, quoted so the music "spoke" the words the dancers could not (Smith 2000) [P]. Reformers attacked it: Noverre wanted gesture to be expressive rather than conventional (1760); Fokine (letter to *The Times*, 1914) admitted conventional gesture only where the ballet's style demanded it [P; W4 §1.6]. Diaghilev, in the late 1920s, judged audiences did not understand Odette's mime and had Balanchine replace it with dance [D, Macaulay]. It survives as living practice in the Royal Ballet's productions (Sergeyev kept the mime for the Vic-Wells *Swan Lake* in 1934) and in the Royal Danish Ballet's Bournonville repertory [D, Macaulay; RBS on Bournonville].
*Why.* This is the course's best case of a **grammar** that lets a world be read without words, and of a code that dies when the audience stops being taught it.
*Sources.* Smith (2000); Edward Nye, *Mime, Music and Drama on the Eighteenth-Century Stage: The Ballet d'Action* (CUP, 2011); Doug Fullington, "Finding the Balance: Pantomime and Dance in Ratmansky's New/Old *Sleeping Beauty*", in Mark Franko (ed.), *The Oxford Handbook of Dance and Reenactment* (OUP, 2017).

**1.5 Choreography has a grammar: vocabulary, phrase, number, act.**
*What.* The daily class supplies the vocabulary (named steps, positions, directions). The **enchaînement** links steps into a phrase, set to counts (usually in eights) against musical bars. Phrases build **numbers**, and the Petipa era fixed a set of number types: the **grand pas de deux** (entrée, adagio, a variation for each dancer, coda, often with fouettés or a fish dive at the climax), the **pas d'action** (danced plot), the **variation**, the **divertissement** of national or character dances, the **ensemble** for the corps. The **corps de ballet** is geometry: lines, diagonals, circles, wedges, unison and canon. The "white acts" (*Giselle* II, *Swan Lake*'s lakeside, *La Bayadère*'s Shades) are pattern-worlds; in the Shades a single phrase (arabesque, step back, port de bras) is repeated by each dancer in turn down a ramp until the stage fills (the number of dancers varies by staging, commonly 24 to 48) [P; check per production].
*Why.* This is ballet's component library: named parts with rules of combination. It lets a learner see "a concept is a generative system" working at the scale of a step.
*Sources.* Janet Adshead (ed.), *Dance Analysis: Theory and Practice* (Dance Books, 1988); Jacqueline M. Smith-Autard, *Dance Composition* (Bloomsbury, 8th ed. 2021); Scholl (1994).

**1.6 The dancer is an interpreter and often a co-author.**
*What.* Roles are made on particular bodies, and later dancers inherit a shape cut for someone else. MacMillan made Juliet on Lynn Seymour (balcony pas de deux first for Canadian television, September 1964), but Fonteyn and Nureyev danced the premiere on 9 February 1965 "for bureaucratic reasons" [D, Wikipedia]. Pierina Legnani's 32 fouettés (1895) became part of Odile [D]; whether one ballerina danced Odette and Odile in 1877 is unsettled [C]. Coaches and répétiteurs carry the detail: Fonteyn coached young Royal ballerinas in Odette's mime late in life [D, Macaulay]; Bournonville style passes from dancer to student by watching and copying [D, RBS].
*Why.* The Concept DNA needs the distinction between the work, its anchor expression and each performance; and credit lines that end at the choreographer misdescribe how ballets are made.
*Sources.* Selma Jeanne Cohen, *Next Week, Swan Lake: Reflections on Dance and Dances* (Wesleyan UP, 1982); Graham McFee, *The Philosophical Aesthetics of Dance* (Dance Books, 2011); Anthea Kraut, *Choreographing Copyright* (OUP, 2016).

### Tier 2: should know

**2.1 Rehearsal music and the répétiteur score.** Nineteenth-century ballets were rehearsed to a violin (often two: melody and accompaniment), not a piano; the répétiteur score is a short score for that purpose, with stage directions [P: Lopukhov, via Still]. The change to piano is dated to the Paris Opéra around 1886 (Lanchbery named Messager's *Les Deux Pigeons*) [C: unverified, see Still]. Répétiteurs are survival documents: Tchaikovsky's lost 1877 pas de deux was recovered from one in 1953 [D]. Today the **class pianist** improvises or adapts music to every exercise, and is the dancer's first musical partner. *Sources:* Fedor Lopukhov, *Writings on Ballet and Music*, ed. Stephanie Jordan (U of Wisconsin Press, 2002); James Parakilas et al., *Piano Roles* (Yale UP, 1999); Jonathan Still's blog (practitioner, open) https://jonathanstill.com/2020/12/11/first-ballet-rehearsed-piano/.

**2.2 Three theories of how music and dance share a world.** Drama first (Noverre; Soviet *drambalet*); music first (Balanchine with Stravinsky: structure read from the score, plotless ballets such as *Serenade* 1934, *Agon* 1957); independence (Cunningham and Cage: made separately, meeting on the night) [P; W4 §1.8–1.9 has the theory]. Massine's *Les Présages* (Monte Carlo, 13 April 1933, Tchaikovsky's Fifth) opened the "symphonic ballet" quarrel over whether concert symphonies may be danced [D premiere; P debate]. *Sources:* Jordan (2000); Charles M. Joseph, *Stravinsky and Balanchine: A Journey of Invention* (Yale UP, 2002); Jordan, *Stravinsky Dances* (Dance Books, 2007).

**2.3 How choreographers work in the studio.** A spectrum from the maker who arrives with the steps to the maker who sets tasks. Ashton worked on his dancers' bodies and hid a signature phrase (the "Fred step", taken from Pavlova's Gavotte) in nearly every ballet [D, Macaulay]. Balanchine read the piano reduction and made on the dancers in front of him [P]. Nijinsky counted the *Rite* aloud, "arithmetic classes" [D, History Today]. Bausch asked questions and built from the answers (from 1978) [D, Klein]. Forsythe built improvisation into the work and published tools (*Improvisation Technologies*, 1999) [P]. Pite generates material with dancers through tasks and builds mass choreography (36 dancers in *Flight Pattern*, 2017) [D for numbers; P for process]. McGregor's company trained an AI tool on his back catalogue to propose next phrases (*Living Archive*, 2019) [D, company source]. *Sources:* Julie Kavanagh, *Secret Muses: The Life of Frederick Ashton* (Faber, 1996); Jann Parry, *Different Drummer: The Life of Kenneth MacMillan* (Faber, 2009); Gabriele Klein, *Pina Bausch's Dance Theater* (transcript, 2020) **[OA, CC BY-NC-ND]**; Steven Spier (ed.), *William Forsythe and the Practice of Choreography* (Routledge, 2011).

**2.4 The border with modern and contemporary dance.** Duncan ("The Dance of the Future", 1903) rejected pointe, corset and the codified step; Graham built a technique on contraction and the floor; Cunningham kept ballet's speed and legwork but threw out narrative and musical dependence; Bausch replaced steps with behaviour. Ballet then borrowed: *Episodes* (1959) put Graham and Balanchine on one bill [D]; Tharp's *Push Comes to Shove* (1976) [P]; Forsythe's dismantling of ballet's vertical; "contemporary ballet" as a category. *Sources:* Kathrina Farrugia-Kriel and Jill Nunes Jensen (eds), *The Oxford Handbook of Contemporary Ballet* (OUP, 2021); Sally Banes, *Terpsichore in Sneakers* (Wesleyan, 2nd ed. 1987); Duncan (1903) **[PD]**.

**2.5 Music as the portable layer: suite, recording, tape on tour.** The *Nutcracker Suite* was played nine months before the ballet (19 March 1892) [D]; Ginastera's *Estancia* suite (1943) became famous nine years before the ballet was staged (1952) [D, LoC]. Touring companies cut costs by dancing to recordings; Northern Ballet's 2024 move to recorded music on some tours drew the Musicians' Union's "unacceptable" [D, Gramophone]. *Sources:* the cases above; scope 05 for economics.

### Tier 3: could know

**3.1 Scores beyond Western Europe.** National idiom through folk material in a Western orchestra: Garayev's *Seven Beauties* (Baku, 1952, after Nizami, with Azerbaijani folk instruments) [D]; Khachaturian's *Gayane* (1942) and *Spartacus* (1956) [P]; Ginastera's *Estancia* ("objective nationalism", malambo finale) [D]; *The Red Detachment of Women* (Beijing, 1964, a score by five composers on Hainan folk songs) [D]; Mayuzumi Toshirō's *Bugaku* for Balanchine (1963, after gagaku) [D]; Cairo's Egyptian-themed ballets, whose scores were often **arranged** from folk and film music (*Majnoun Laila*, arr. Halim El-Dabh; Omar Khairat's *The Nile* from a 1984 film score) [D, Aramco World 1977; Ahram 2013]; *Çeşmebaşı* (Ankara, 1964 or 1965), the earliest ballet to a Turkish composer's score, on folk-dance music [D for 1965 season; C for date]. *Sources:* Simon Morrison, *The People's Artist: Prokofiev's Soviet Years* (OUP, 2009) for Soviet practice; Paul Clark, *The Chinese Cultural Revolution: A History* (CUP, 2008) on the model works; Karkabi (1977) [open web].

**3.2 Tools: notation, video, motion capture, AI.** Feuillet (1700) set steps to bars; Stepanov (1892), Laban (1928) and Benesh (1955) [P; see scope 05/06]; video took over from notation as the working memory of most companies from the 1980s [P]; *Synchronous Objects* (Ohio State with the Forsythe Company, 2009) turned one dance into data visualisations [D]; McGregor's *Living Archive* (2019) [D].

**3.3 Character dance as a second legibility system.** National dances (mazurka, czardas, Spanish, "Oriental") make place legible through rhythm and costume; they are also stereotypes [P; see W1/W3 on *Nutcracker* "Arabian" and "Chinese"].

**3.4 Editions and the musicology of ballet.** Critical editions, the Sergeyev Collection (Harvard Theatre Collection, digitised 2024) [D], IMSLP répétiteurs [D link]. Who restores what, and why productions keep the "wrong" version.

## 3. Stories (15)

**S1. The pas de deux that was lost for 76 years (1877–1953–1960).** Anna Sobeshchanskaya, dancing Odette/Odile in Moscow from April 1877, wanted a better showpiece and had Petipa make one to music by Minkus. Tchaikovsky, angry, wrote new music to fit Petipa's choreography exactly. The music vanished, until a répétiteur score was found in the Bolshoi archive in 1953; in 1960 Balanchine made *Tschaikovsky Pas de Deux* to it [D, Wikipedia "Swan Lake"]. *Why it matters:* a star could rewrite a world; a composer could be made to fit someone else's steps; a rehearsal copy outlived the master.

**S2. The conductor's *Swan Lake* (1895).** Tchaikovsky died in November 1893. For the 1895 revival his brother rewrote the plot and the conductor Riccardo Drigo cut, reordered and added: three Tchaikovsky piano pieces became Act III dances, and Drigo wrote a new ending for the Black Swan adagio [D]. Most companies still play this edit. *Why:* the "authentic" world is often an editor's.

**S3. A brief written in bars (1889–1892).** Petipa sent Tchaikovsky a plan that set, number by number, the action, the tempo and how many bars he wanted [D, W1 and Wikipedia]. Tchaikovsky kept the plan and made masterpieces inside it. *Why:* constraint and invention are not opposites; a good brief names the clock and leaves the colour.

**S4. Lanchbery goes to see the clog dancers (1959–60).** Making *La Fille mal gardée*, Ashton rejected the usual Hertel score, took Hérold's 1828 music on Ivor Guest's advice, and had John Lanchbery arrange it, adding a Donizetti pas de deux Guest found as a violin part in a box at the Opéra. For the Widow Simone's clog dance he took Lanchbery to watch Lancashire clog dancers [D]. *Why:* a new world built from found parts, local craft and archival luck.

**S5. "Arithmetic classes" (1913).** The *Rite of Spring*'s rhythms were so irregular that Nijinsky's dancers learned it by counting aloud, in Russian, through perhaps 120 rehearsals; on the night he stood on a chair in the wings shouting the numbers [D, History Today]. *Why:* when a score breaks the old grammar, the makers have to invent a new working tool (counts) to cross it.

**S6. "Over there, an evil man made me a swan" (1877 to now).** Odette's mime scene tells Siegfried her story in gesture. Diaghilev thought audiences could not read it and had Balanchine replace it with dance (late 1920s); Sergeyev kept it for the British in 1934; Nureyev cut it in 1962 and later came round; Fonteyn spent her last years coaching young dancers in it; Russian companies dropped it [D, Macaulay]. *Why:* a code survives only where someone keeps teaching both the performers and the audience.

**S7. A story without words, with a printed key (1717).** At Drury Lane John Weaver staged *The Loves of Mars and Venus*, telling a myth through dance, gesture and music, and published a scenario describing the gestures [D for the work and scenario; P for the gesture descriptions]. *Why:* the earliest known "how to read this world" document in ballet's line.

**S8. The Fred step (1930–1988).** Ashton hid a five-step phrase taken from Pavlova's *Gavotte* in nearly every ballet, altering its ending and counts so it could pass for part of the scene; he called it "Pavlova", others called it the Fred step [D, Macaulay]. *Why:* an author's signature carried inside the grammar, invisible to most of the audience.

**S9. Juliet made on Seymour, opened by Fonteyn (1964–65).** MacMillan made the balcony pas de deux on Lynn Seymour and Christopher Gable for Canadian television, then built the ballet on them; the Royal Opera House gave the first night to Fonteyn and Nureyev, who took 43 curtain calls [D]. *Why:* roles are cut to bodies, and institutions decide whose body gets the credit.

**S10. Ballet meets Graham (1959).** Kirstein set Graham and Balanchine side by side to Webern in *Episodes*. Graham first gave the NYCB dancer Sallie Wilson ballet steps; Wilson asked for Graham's instead. Graham's half lasted a season; Balanchine's survives [D]. *Why:* neighbours can share a stage without merging, and the institution decides what stays.

**S11. The suite before the ballet (1941–1952).** Kirstein commissioned Ginastera's gaucho ballet *Estancia* on a State Department goodwill tour; the war stopped the staging; the suite (1943) made Ginastera famous; the ballet was danced only in 1952. The Library of Congress holds the holograph [D]. *Why:* music is the layer of a ballet world that travels first.

**S12. Cairo, 1966: "Oriental flavor" and then Egyptian scores.** Egypt's first ballet company opened with a Soviet ballet chosen for its "Oriental flavor"; Nasser came the next night. Egyptian choreographers then built Egyptian themes on scores arranged from folk and film music (*Majnoun Laila*, Halim El-Dabh; *The Nile*, Omar Khairat) [D, Aramco World; Ahram]. *Why:* a borrowed world arrives with someone else's picture of you, and local makers re-score it.

**S13. Questions, not steps (1978).** Pina Bausch began asking her dancers questions and building scenes from their answers; Klein shows the method spread worldwide, but notes the dancers "never really had an equal say" [D, Klein, OA]. *Why:* co-creation methods can still keep one author.

**S14. An archive that suggests the next move (2019).** Studio Wayne McGregor and Google Arts & Culture trained a tool on video of McGregor's past works and the dancers' solos; given a phrase, it offers options for the next [D, company source]. *Why:* a choreographer's grammar, once recorded, becomes a generator.

**S15. The orchestra or the tape (2024).** Northern Ballet replaced live players with recordings on some tours; the Musicians' Union called it "unacceptable" [D]. *Why:* the score is also a workforce, and touring a world means deciding which parts travel live.

## 4. Concepts and debates

1. **Is ballet music lesser music?** 1877 critics found Tchaikovsky "too symphonic" for ballet; later critics despised Minkus and Pugni as hacks. Smith (2000) and Wiley (1985) argue the specialists' scores are skilled dramatic machinery made to a brief [P]. *Argued:* the distinction is commission type (service versus anchor), not talent.
2. **Which comes first: drama, music or neither?** Noverre and *drambalet* (drama); Balanchine and Stravinsky (music: "see the music, hear the dance" is attributed to Balanchine [P]); Cunningham and Cage (independence) (W4). Jordan (2000) offers the method for reading music and dance together.
3. **May you dance a symphony?** The *Présages* quarrel (1933): Massine's critics held concert works self-sufficient; Balanchine settled it in practice [P].
4. **Whose score is authentic?** Drigo's 1895 *Swan Lake* against Tchaikovsky's 1877; Lavrovsky's *Romeo* against Prokofiev's; editions against performance traditions (the 2026 *Giselle* keeps interpolations) [D cases; debate P].
5. **Is mime a code or acting?** Fokine wanted the whole body to express, not the hand alone [P]. Macaulay argues mime conveys meaning even to viewers who miss individual signs, and that it was never universally understood [D]. Ratmansky and Fullington restore 1890s mime from notation as documented practice (W3). The Royal and Danish traditions treat it as a living craft.
6. **Formula or invention?** The pas de deux formula and the Petipa number types are constraints that made huge variety possible (Scholl 1994) [P]; reformers (Fokine, Duncan) saw the formula as dead convention.
7. **Who authors a ballet?** Choreographer (law and posters), dancer (roles made on bodies), répétiteur and coach (what is actually danced), composer and conductor (the clock). Cohen (1982) and McFee (2011) on work versus performance; Kraut (2016) on credit and race; Klein (2020) on limits of co-creation [P/D].
8. **Ballet versus its neighbours.** Duncan and Graham defined modern dance against ballet; by 1959 ballet was borrowing back. Is "contemporary ballet" a style or an institution (ballet companies dancing non-ballet)? Farrugia-Kriel and Nunes Jensen (2021) [P].
9. **Live or recorded?** Unions and many critics: live music is part of the work. Companies: touring costs [D, 2024]. *Argued:* the decision changes the work, since tempo with a live conductor is negotiated with the dancers nightly; a recording fixes it.
10. **National idiom or exoticism?** Ginastera's "objective nationalism", Soviet "national in form" scores (*Seven Beauties*), the Chinese collective score, Cairo's "Oriental flavor" [D cases; P framing]. Who chooses the sounds of a place?

## 5. Lessons for world-builders

1. **Write the clock before the colour.** Petipa's brief fixed bars, tempo and order; the composer filled them. Give your makers the duration, rhythm and sequence of your world first.
2. **Expect your world to be edited.** Drigo, Lanchbery and every conductor re-made the score. Decide what is fixed (the anchor) and what may be cut, reordered or added, and write that down.
3. **A sign system only works if you teach it.** Mime lived while audiences were given livrets, programme notes and repetition, and died where they were not. Every wordless world (a retail floor, a game, a wayfinding system) needs its own synopsis and its *airs parlants*: familiar cues that carry the new meaning.
4. **Build from a small vocabulary with strict combination rules.** Class steps, enchaînement, number types, the corps' geometry: a few parts make an infinite repertory. The Shades show one rule repeated making a world.
5. **Know your template and when to break it.** The pas de deux formula is a reliable experience arc (meeting, slow display, solo proofs, climax). Use it knowingly; reformers broke it when it went dead.
6. **Make on the people who will carry it, and credit them.** Roles cut on Seymour, Guillem or Legnani carry their makers' bodies forever. Record who shaped what.
7. **Plan the portable layer.** Music (the suite, the recording) travels before the full world does. Decide which element of your world goes out first and stands alone.
8. **Hide a signature in the grammar.** The Fred step shows authorship can live inside the system, not in a logo.
9. **Define your world by its neighbours, then borrow.** Ballet said what it was by refusing modern dance and then took its floor work and weight. Write your world's "anti-DNA", and expect to revise it.
10. **Keep the working copies.** A rehearsal score saved a lost Tchaikovsky piece; the Sergeyev répétiteurs let lost ballets be rebuilt. Archive the working documents, not only the finished show.

## 7. Gaps and traps

1. **"First" claims everywhere.** "First ballet rehearsed to piano" (*Les Deux Pigeons*, 1886) is one conductor's unverified claim [C]; "first modern ballet" (Weaver) is Wikipedia's phrase; "first Turkish ballet" (*Çeşmebaşı*) has two dates. Teach "earliest known" or "earliest claimed".
2. **Pugni's output.** "Over 300 ballets" circulates; Wikipedia gives "over 100" originals plus arrangements. Use the lower figure with the qualifier.
3. **Tchaikovsky on *Sylvia*.** The "would not have written Swan Lake" line is flagged "citation needed" on Wikipedia. Verify in Wiley (1985) or the Tchaikovsky Research letters database before quoting.
4. **Odette/Odile as one role.** Contested: some say 1895 (Legnani) began it, others that Karpakova may have danced both in 1877. The black Odile costume dates from the 1940s [D, JSTOR Daily].
5. **Prokofiev "undanceable" and Ulanova's couplet** are widely repeated but were not seen at source here; source to Morrison (2009) before use.
6. **Mime gestures are not one fixed dictionary.** They differ between the Royal, Danish and Russian traditions, and some (the bourrées on "tears") may be 20th-century additions [D, Macaulay]. Present any gesture list as "as taught by X".
7. **The Shades count** varies (24, 32, 48); never give a single number without the staging.
8. **"Drigo ruined/saved Tchaikovsky"** is a value judgement; present the edits as facts and the judgement as debate.
9. **Arranged versus composed.** Cairo's early Egyptian ballets were often "music arranged by"; *The Red Detachment*'s score was collective. Do not assign a single composer.
10. **Gulf.** No ballet score composed in the Gulf was found. ROH Muscat's 2025 *Sindbad* is an opera with an Egyptian composer. Do not stretch it into a ballet case. Royal Opera House Muscat presents touring ballet with imported scores (Les Grands Ballets, 2012).
11. **Sub-Saharan Africa** is unrepresented in this aspect; South African company commissions need a separate check.
12. **Company and union sources** (McGregor's *Living Archive* page; Pite's "18 years" via *The Guardian*; Musicians' Union) are partisan; label them.
13. **Copyright.** Stravinsky (d. 1971), Ginastera, Khachaturian, Mayuzumi and Garayev scores are in copyright in most places; Prokofiev (d. 1953) is PD in life+70 countries from 2024 but not everywhere [P]. Recordings carry separate rights. Use link-only for excerpts.
14. **Japanese names** in Japanese order: Mayuzumi Toshirō.
15. **Not covered here:** the class pianist's repertoire and improvisation as a craft (needs a practitioner source such as Still's writing or the Royal Academy of Dance's guidance); Bournonville mime scholarship (Jürgensen 1990) not re-seen; Royal Danish mime films.

## Research log (raw, folded into sections below)

### Log 1: the score as edited object
- *Swan Lake* 1895 (Wikipedia "Swan Lake (1895)", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swan_Lake_(1895)) [D at source; secondary]: Tchaikovsky d. 6 Nov 1893; Ivanov staged the lakeside scene for a memorial concert 17 and 22 Feb 1894; revival premiered 15/27 Jan 1895, Legnani as Odette/Odile; 32 fouettés in the Act III (now) grand pas, encore with 28 (reported). Modest Tchaikovsky reworked the libretto (four acts to three; lovers die together, apotheosis). Drigo's edits: cuts (Act I pas de trois andante; Act II ballabile and pas de six; the Minkus pas de deux and Russian dance appendices; Act III Dance of the Little Swans no. 27 etc.); reordering (Act I waltz and pas de trois swapped; swan dances re-sequenced); three Tchaikovsky piano pieces from Op. 72 orchestrated and interpolated: no. 12 "L'Espiègle" as Odile's variation, no. 11 "Valse bluette", no. 15 "Un poco di Chopin"; Black Swan pas de deux built from the 1877 "Pas de deux for two merry-makers" (no. 5) with a new adagio ending by Drigo.
- Tchaikovsky Research, "Riccardo Drigo" (https://en.tchaikovsky-research.net/pages/Riccardo_Drigo) [D]: Drigo 1846–1930, Padua; to Russia 1879 for Italian opera; principal ballet conductor Mariinsky 1886; own ballets *Le Talisman* 1889, *La Flûte magique* 1893, *Le Réveil de Flore* 1894, *La Perle* 1896, *Les Millions d'Arlequin* 1900; conducted premieres of *Sleeping Beauty* 1890 and *Nutcracker* 1892; Op. 72 orchestrations issued as supplement to the full score.
- Ashton's *Fille* (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_fille_mal_gard%C3%A9e_(Ashton)) [D at source]: premiere 28 Jan 1960, ROH, Nerina and Blair. Ashton rejected Hertel's 1864 score; on Ivor Guest's suggestion used Hérold's 1828 score; Lanchbery edited and orchestrated it, adding passages of the 1789 Bordeaux music, one Hertel number (the Clog Dance) and new numbers of his own. The Elssler pas de deux uses Donizetti (*L'elisir d'amore*) arranged for Elssler in 1837, found by Guest as a violin reduction at the Opéra. Ashton took Lanchbery to see Lancashire clog dancers. Petipa's mime "When I'm married" taught to Ashton by Karsavina, who had it from Pavel Gerdt.
- *Nutcracker* (Wikipedia) [D at source]: Petipa's "extremely detailed instructions … down to the tempo and number of bars"; Suite Op. 71a first played 19 Mar 1892 under the composer, nine months before the ballet (18 Dec / 6 Dec O.S. 1892, double bill with *Iolanta*), Drigo conducting. Celesta already used in *The Voyevoda* (1891).

### Log 2: composers, interpolation, the lost répétiteur score
- *Swan Lake* (Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swan_Lake) [D at source]: 1877 premiere 20 Feb/4 Mar, Bolshoi, Moscow; Reisinger; conductor Stepan Ryabov; Karpakova Odette (benefit). Critics: score "too noisy, too 'Wagnerian' and too symphonic". Sobeshchanskaya (Odette/Odile from 26 Apr 1877) asked Petipa for a pas de deux; Petipa set it to Minkus; Tchaikovsky, angry, wrote new music to fit Petipa's choreography (same length and divisions per W1). That score was lost until a **répétiteur score turned up in the Bolshoi archives in 1953**; Balanchine set *Tschaikovsky Pas de Deux* to it (1960). Tchaikovsky to Taneyev on Delibes's *Sylvia*: "if I had known of this music then, I would not have written Swan Lake" (Wikipedia marks it citation needed) → [P], check Wiley 1985 or Tchaikovsky Research letters before teaching. Odette and Odile: whether one dancer in 1877 is unsettled → [C].
- Prokofiev *Romeo and Juliet* (Wikipedia) [D at source]: composed Sept 1935 to a Bolshoi contract; original happy ending caused controversy with officials; Bolshoi audition "had no success"; first full staging Brno, Mahen Theatre, 30 Dec 1938 (Vania Psota); Kirov 11 Jan 1940, Lavrovsky, Ulanova and Konstantin Sergeyev; Lavrovsky "significantly changed the score" over Prokofiev's objections. ### Log 3: mime
