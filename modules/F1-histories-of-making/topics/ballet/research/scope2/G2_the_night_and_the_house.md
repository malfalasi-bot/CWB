# G2. The night and the house: ballet as an experience an audience enters

Gap pass for `review/R2_knowledge_gaps.md` §2.4 (the night: audience, ritual, who could attend), §2.5 (theatre buildings as designed worlds), §3.5 (access, audience side) and the "experience design and the service blueprint" and "distinction and access" rows of R2's concept table. Brief: `BRIEF_GAPS.md` (25 October 2026). Not repeated here: the foyer de la danse as a sexual economy (scope 05 §3.8), the Garnier rake and the 2019 strike on the steps (scope 03, 05), Gulf houses as opening dates and the absence of resident Gulf companies (scope 01 §3.5, W3 §10), media beyond the relay (R2 §2.6, a separate pass).

**Confidence words.** *Documented*: seen at source in this pass (a holder's record, a primary document or newspaper of the day, a peer-reviewed or university-press work, a signed reference work, or an institution speaking about its own acts). *Probable*: one press or teaching source, or a reliable secondary not checked at its own source. *Contested*: sources disagree; both readings given. *Argued*: an interpretation, with who argues it. Company, venue and promoter figures are labelled as theirs. [OA] = open access. URLs appear only where seen in a result during this pass (full list in §9, the research log).

**Thesis.** A ballet is never only what happens on the stage: from the court of 1581 to a two-act *Swan Lake* in a Doha convention centre in 2026, the house, the ticket, the dress code, the bells, the interval, the applause and the stage door have been designed as carefully as the choreography, and they decide who may enter the world and what kind of world it can be.

## 3. Stories

Each is short enough to open a lesson. Sources are in §9.

**3.1 The king who was also the audience (Paris, 23 February 1653).** In the Salle du Petit-Bourbon, Louis XIV, aged 14, danced the Rising Sun at the end of a night-long *Ballet royal de la Nuit*, "in front of the Court and a throng of spectators" (CMBV). The performers were the court; the court was the audience; the subject was the king. *Why it matters:* the earliest form of the ballet audience was a society watching itself rehearse its own order. The house and the world were the same thing. [documented; length probable]

**3.2 The silence after the pirouette (Paris Opéra, 1830s).** Under Louis Véron the Opéra's applause was managed by Auguste Levasseur's claque, paid in free tickets that Levasseur resold. In one account, the dancer Pauline Duvernay finished a pirouette and smiled in thanks for the usual salvo, and the house was silent: the claque had been withheld (JSTOR Daily, citing Véron's memoirs). *Why it matters:* applause was a designed and purchasable service, and its absence was a weapon. Audiences learned when to clap from people paid to show them. [probable; primary to check]

**3.3 The ballet must come in Act II (Paris, 13 March 1861).** Paris custom put an opera's ballet in the second act, so members of the Jockey Club could dine and arrive in time to watch the dancers, among them their mistresses. Wagner put his Bacchanale at the start of *Tannhäuser*. The club's members whistled the performances down, and the work was withdrawn after three nights and some 164 rehearsals (BSO notes; Berliner Philharmoniker). *Why it matters:* an audience faction set the structure of the work, not only its reception. The world's timetable was written around the guests' dinner. [documented in outline]

**3.4 A staircase built as a stage (Paris, 5 January 1875).** Charles Garnier gave the Grand Escalier a triple flight in which, the BnF notes, no two steps share the same curve; balconies around it act as boxes, so those climbing are "à la fois acteurs et spectateurs". The Opéra still says it was built so "everyone can see and be seen". At the opening, Garnier, sent no invitation, paid for his own seat; the crowd applauded him on the stairs (HistoFrance; France Today). *Why it matters:* the architect designed the audience's performance, and gave it more floor area than the stage. [staircase documented; seat story probable]

**3.5 The house goes dark, and who did it first (Bayreuth, 13 August 1876).** Wagner's Festspielhaus hid the orchestra under a hood, raked the seats like an amphitheatre and, it is said, darkened the hall for the earliest time. Wagner himself traced the three ideas to a theatre he had worked in at Riga (Bayreuth Festival history). The historian Russell Burdekin (2020) shows that dimming and darkening had already been practised. *Why it matters:* a lesson in "earliest known": the myth makes one genius the inventor of a change many houses made. And it shows what the dark did: it ended the audience's view of itself. [contested]

**3.6 An audience of one (Munich, 1872–85).** From 6 May 1872 Ludwig II of Bavaria had performances staged for himself alone in the court theatres: 209 by 1885, eleven of them ballets, at a cost reported as 97,300 marks. *Why it matters:* it is the court audience reduced to its logical end, and a limit case for any designer of "exclusive" experiences: the world staged entirely for one guest, with the public removed. [first one documented; counts probable]

**3.7 Free seats for the factories (Moscow, 1919–21).** After the Revolution, the State Academic Bolshoi Theatre gave most of its seats away: 84.5% free or discounted in 1919–20, 96.8% in 1920–21, distributed through factories, workers' committees and unions, with a discounted section for union members (Singh 2021, from archives). The Moscow trade unions meanwhile argued for closing the theatre. *Why it matters:* the same building, the same *Swan Lake*, a different public by allocation. A door policy can change a world's meaning without touching the stage. [documented]

**3.8 The Soviet claqueur (Moscow, 1970s–80s).** In a 2016 interview a former Bolshoi claqueur said the claque's passes came from the top of the theatre in the Grigorovich era, that tickets bought at about 1.8 roubles could be resold to foreigners for ten to twenty times as much, and that the claque worked hardest at "meaningful" performances for military regiments. *Why it matters:* the 19th-century Paris service survived the Revolution. Applause, price and the tourist market were managed together. [probable; one voice]

**3.9 Queues in Covent Garden (London, 1956).** The Bolshoi's earliest known Western season was sold out before it opened; people queued for days and lay all night in the street; Ulanova's admirers strewed petals from the theatre to the hotel (Guardian 2022; Independent 1998; CSM 1986). Ulanova's own line: "Our audiences are ordinary people." *Why it matters:* the tour turned an audience in one city into a performance for another, and the queue itself became part of the event. [probable]

**3.10 The theatre that opened for whites only (Cape Town, 1971).** The Nico Malan Theatre Centre opened on 19 May 1971 as CAPAB's home for opera, ballet, drama and the orchestra, and admitted white audiences only. Protest opened it in 1975, but by permit, so the boycott continued; the law barring mixed audiences went in March 1978 (Artscape; ESAT; the Presidency, 2023). Johaar Mosaval, a Royal Ballet principal born in Cape Town, became the earliest known Black dancer on its stage, as Petrushka, under a contract that barred him from touching a white dancer with bare hands. *Why it matters:* the house enforced a political world's rules at the box office, on the stage and in the choreography. [documented; dates in detail contested]

**3.11 The opera house that burned and the one that was given (Cairo, 1869–1988).** Khedive Ismail's opera house, built in about six months of wood for the Suez Canal festivities, opened on 1 November 1869 with *Rigoletto* because *Aida* was not ready. It was "frequented by an elite in evening dress and black tie" (Magda Saleh, NYT 1988). It burned at dawn on 28 October 1971 with a century of costumes and archives; the site became a car park, still called "Garaj el-Opera". In 1983 Japan offered a new house; Shikida Koichiro proposed a Pharaonic style, an Islamic style was chosen, and the Cairo Opera House opened on 10 October 1988. *Why it matters:* the house Egypt's State Information Service calls the first opera house in Africa (earliest known, in this pass) was built as a stage for a canal and an empire's guests; its replacement was a diplomatic gift that became home to the Arab world's main ballet company. [documented]

**3.12 The roof you may walk on (Oslo, 2008; London, 2018).** Snøhetta made the roof of the Oslo Opera House a sloping plane of Carrara marble rising from the fjord, so the public could climb it in any season; the lobby is "keyless". The tourist board's line: usually you are arrested for walking on roofs. Ten years later the Royal Opera House spent £50.7m on "Open Up", connecting its Bow Street foyer to the street "for the first time" (RIBA) and telling the public "you don't need a ticket to come in". *Why it matters:* both designs move the threshold outward, so that the world begins before the ticket. [documented]

**3.13 A dhow that changes shape, and a dress code (Dubai, 2016; Muscat, 2011).** Dubai Opera, by Atkins with lead architect Janus Rostock, is shaped like a dhow; its 2,000-seat hall converts from proscenium theatre to concert hall to a flat floor for banquets, weddings and fashion shows. The Royal Opera House Muscat, opened in 2011, requires suits or dinner jackets, "conservative dress" for women, and "Dishdasha and Massar" for Omani men, and permits "absolutely no late seating". *Why it matters:* two Gulf answers to the same absence (no resident company). One designs a building flexible enough for any market; the other designs a strict ritual that makes national dress part of the house's world. [documented]

**3.14 *Swan Lake* in two acts, at 3pm and 8pm (Riyadh 2024; Doha 2026).** A touring company called Moscow Ballet La Classique, founded in 1990 by Elik Melikov, plays *Swan Lake* in two acts, twice in a day, in a university theatre in Riyadh (3 May 2024, SAR 200–1,250) and at a convention-centre theatre in Doha (30 October 2026, from QAR 225). *Why it matters:* the full-length ballet is cut and re-timed into a touring product for a market with houses but no companies. It is the night re-engineered, and a lesson in brand: "Moscow" and "Imperial" are sold by private troupes, not by the Bolshoi. [documented as listings]

**3.15 Touch before you see (Birmingham, now).** Birmingham Royal Ballet runs free touch tours on the morning of audio-described performances, where blind and partially sighted patrons handle costumes, props and the set; its relaxed *Nutcracker* has "a relaxed approach to noise levels", a spoken introduction by the main characters and a BSL interpreter at the side of the stage. A Northern Ballet patron wrote that she had thought she would never see dance again. *Why it matters:* access is not a ramp added afterwards; it is a second design of the whole world, in words and touch, with its rules of silence deliberately relaxed. [documented]

## 4. The concepts and debates

### Concepts

- **The audience as performer.** Garnier's staircase, the box as a stage for the box opposite, the foyer as "a second stage" (Smarthistory/LibreTexts, teaching). Goffman's front region is the same idea in sociology: performances need a stage and a guarded back region where "the vital secrets of a show are visible" (Goffman 1956). The foyer de la danse was a deliberate breach of that line for paying men (scope 05; Orsay).
- **The threshold and the sequence.** Stanislavsky's 1933 letter makes the cloakroom part of the performance; *les trois coups*, the bell, the lights and the overture are a graded series of thresholds. Shostack's "line of visibility" is the blueprint form of the stage edge.
- **The experience economy.** Pine and Gilmore (1998): an experience occurs when a company uses services "as the stage" and goods "as props"; their principles (theme the experience; harmonise impressions with positive cues; eliminate negative cues; mix in memorabilia; engage all five senses) read like a description of the Garnier night, with the programme as memorabilia. *Argued (this pass):* the metaphor runs backwards; the theatre was the source, business the borrower.
- **Pacification and sacralisation.** Johnson (1995) asks "Why did audiences grow silent?" and answers with changes in how music was understood; Levine (1988) argues the American elite "sacralised" high art and taught a silent, dressed audience as a mark of class. Both explain the modern ballet audience; they differ on cause (aesthetic change versus class boundary-making). *Argued.*
- **Distinction.** Bourdieu (1979): taste and its rituals mark class. The dress code, the subscription and the price ladder are its instruments; the touring two-act *Swan Lake* and the relaxed performance are attempts to move them.
- **Kinaesthetic empathy.** Reason and Reynolds (2010) report audiences describing felt movement as a core pleasure of watching dance; Foster (2011) historicises the idea. This is what the night is for, and what a relay must carry.
- **Liveness.** Nesta (2010): cinema audiences valued knowing the performance was happening "live that evening" and reported higher emotional engagement than theatre audiences. The "live" is a designed property, not a technical one.
- **Atmosphere** (from R2): Böhme's term fits gaslight, the dark house and the white act as designed moods.

### Debates

1. **Who darkened the house?** Received story: Wagner, Bayreuth 1876 (Britannica and most guides). Burdekin (2020): earlier dimming and darkening were practised; Wagner's novelty was leaving it dark by design. Teach as **contested**, and as a model of "earliest known".
2. **Why did audiences go quiet?** Johnson (aesthetic change in listening) versus Levine (class sacralisation). Both **argued**; they can be taught as two lenses on one change.
3. **Do relays build or cannibalise audiences?** Nesta (2010): new audiences, intense engagement. AEA for ACE (2016): Live in HD "did not cannibalise live opera attendance" but did not increase it either; most of those surveyed prefer both. The dispute over whether relays drain regional houses is **open**; no ballet-specific study found.
4. **What are the Gulf houses for?** Hennessey: ROHM-type houses are mainly "sites of cultural importation and consumption" (scope 01, **argued**). The houses' own statements: platforms for local talent (JACC's vision "a platform and forum for young Kuwaiti creatives"; Cairo Opera Ballet and ROHM co-operation; Arab commissions in W3 §10). Petrocelli's view is close to the houses' (declare his ROHM role). **Contested**; the evidence of programming (touring *Swan Lake*, local ballet-school recitals on the JACC stage) supports both.
5. **The dress code.** ROHM: formal dress and national dress, entry refused otherwise (keeps a ceremonial world, and makes Omani dress part of it). ROH: no dress code, children from five, under-16s free at some ENB *Nutcrackers* (removes a barrier). **Argued** both ways; the course can set it as a brief.
6. **Restoration as truth.** The Bolshoi's 2005–11 work restored "pre-Soviet glory" and acoustics damaged by Soviet changes; the cost is **contested** (officially about 21bn roubles; reported up to double; a 2009 criminal investigation). What period does a house "really" belong to? The same question as a ballet reconstruction.
7. **Symbol versus brief.** Sydney's shells won; its ballet stage and pit were too small until 2017. Mariinsky II's golden cocoon won the competition and lost the building. **Argued**: both are cases of the image of a world beating its working plan.
8. **The *Nutcracker* economy: lifeline or dependence?** Dance/USA: share of season revenue rose from 22–25% (2008) to 48% (2017); Dance Magazine asked whether companies were "making too much money" from it. **Argued.**
9. **Immersive versus proscenium.** Punchdrunk's audience chooses its path; critics of immersive theatre (lead: Machon 2013; others not checked here) ask whether that is agency or consumption. Ballet's fixed seat and fixed sequence are a deliberate opposite design. **Argued.**
10. **The foyer de la danse.** R2: keep it as sexual exploitation and add it as the hinge between stage world and house world. Orsay's 1988 framing ("privileged meeting point", "between ephemeral glory and everyday sordidness") shows how institutions soften it. **Contested framing.**

## 5. Lessons for world-builders

1. **The experience starts at the door, not at the curtain.** Design the cloakroom, the stairs and the queue as part of the world (Stanislavsky 1933; Garnier 1875).
2. **Write down your door policy.** Price, dress, paperwork and timing decide who enters as surely as a law (Nico Malan; ROHM; the Bolshoi passport rule). If you cannot say whom your world excludes, you have not designed it.
3. **Your audience will reshape your world.** Name the factions (subscribers, claque, tourists, children) and what each controls. The Jockey Club fixed when the ballet happened.
4. **Sequence is a design material.** Thresholds, signals (knocks, bells, dimming), rest stops (intervals) and a coda (curtain call, flowers, stage door) make time into an experience. Blueprint them.
5. **Decide what is front stage and what is backstage, and who may cross.** The foyer de la danse shows what happens when a paying public is let behind the line.
6. **Darkness and light are choices about the audience's relation to itself.** A lit house is a society; a dark house is a set of private dreamers.
7. **The building fixes what worlds are possible.** A small pit, a flat floor, a raked stage: Sydney and Dubai show a hall decided before the world it must hold.
8. **A world can be re-cut for a new market.** The two-act touring *Swan Lake* and the relayed performance keep some elements and drop others; know which elements are load-bearing.
9. **Access is a redesign of the whole world, not an add-on.** Touch tours and relaxed performances translate and relax the rules; they often clarify the world for everyone.
10. **The same world means different things to different publics.** Free factory seats in 1920, a $25 opening in 1959, a Riyadh matinee in 2024: same ballet, different worlds.
11. **Keep the souvenir.** Programmes, flowers and photographs carry the world home; Pine and Gilmore's "memorabilia" was the ballet's habit long before.
12. **Say "earliest known".** Every "first" in this field (the dark house, the "first" Gulf ballet) has a predecessor.

## 7. Gaps and traps

**Traps**
1. **"First" claims everywhere.** The dark house (Wagner), "the first opera in Africa" (SIS), ROHM "the first opera house on the Arabian Peninsula" (Opera Online, house's framing), "first time ever in the GCC" (BTEA promoter), "the first resident ballet company in Japan" (NNTT's own claim), "for the first time" at ROH (RIBA). Write "earliest known" and name whose claim it is.
2. **Contested costs.** Bolshoi: $688m–$804m, 21–24bn roubles, "double" in press. Mariinsky II: €500m+ or $600m. ROH 1999: £214m (BBC) or £140m (Guardian, perhaps construction only). Teach the range and the source.
3. **Nico Malan dates.** 1975 (permit), 11 Mar 1978 (law abolished), Feb 1979 (Opera Quarterly for an opening "to all races"): teach as a sequence, not one date.
4. **89 curtain calls.** The Guinness record was not seen; one Nureyev Foundation page misdates the night to 1977. Keep "reported".
5. **Garnier's words.** Only an English press translation of the "crowd which observes and knows itself observed" line was seen; check the French in *Le Théâtre* (1871, Google Books/Gallica) before quoting.
6. **The claque anecdote.** Seen via a teaching page citing Véron and a 1939-era article ("Auguste and His Claque", JSTOR 739132); check Véron's *Mémoires* for Duvernay.
7. **Brand confusion in the Gulf.** "Moscow Ballet La Classique", "Imperial Russian Ballet", "Romae Capital Ballet" are private touring companies; do not let learners take them for the Bolshoi or Mariinsky. Their posters exploit the confusion.
8. **Goffman and Pine-Gilmore are metaphors.** Neither studied ballet houses. Say "the ballet night is the literal case of their metaphor", not "Goffman wrote about the opera".
9. **The foyer de la danse.** Do not let the "hinge" reading erase the exploitation documented in scope 05; children were among the dancers.
10. **The "Tsar's box".** At the Mariinsky the family is said to have used a side box (Wikipedia only); verify before teaching.
11. **Stanislavsky's proverb.** The letter is documented via a reference work; the proverb's wording is folk, and Nemirovich-Danchenko is also credited.

**Gaps**
1. **Arabic-language sources were not searched**: Cairo Opera dress code (jacket and tie in the Main Hall is widely reported, not seen), Arab audience studies, Gulf audience demographics, women's attendance history in Gulf houses. A reader of Arabic is needed.
2. **Doha**: Katara Opera House opening date and architect; QNCC architect (Isozaki Arata, not seen in this pass); ballet history in Qatar before 2018.
3. **Saudi Arabia**: the earliest ballet performance in the kingdom was not established; Ithra's programme not searched.
4. **Race and US audiences**: segregated Southern houses on ballet tours (Ballet Russe de Monte Carlo, 1930s–50s) not found in this pass.
5. **Women in the Paris stalls**: whether women were admitted to the *orchestre* in the 19th century was not established; do not claim.
6. **Japan and China**: NNTT only; Beijing NCPA (2007), Shanghai and Seoul houses, and Japanese audience culture (the strong ballet-school audience) not searched; a Japanese reader is needed.
7. **Cuba**: the two-currency ticket (Cubans in pesos, tourists in CUC) at the Gran Teatro de La Habana was searched but not found at source.
8. **The programme as souvenir** and the **stage door** have no scholarly source in this pass; both rest on general practice. A collection search (V&A Theatre and Performance; Bibliothèque-musée de l'Opéra) for programmes and *livrets* is needed.
9. **Royal Opera House relays**: start year of ROH ballet cinema relays not fixed (by 2011 per Nesta); Bolshoi cinema start year not fixed.
10. **Access in the Gulf**: ROHM and Dubai Opera access services were not searched.
11. **Ballet-specific audience data now**: no up-to-date demographic study of ballet audiences found (Bachtrack counts performances, not audiences).
12. **Soviet percentages**: Singh "calculates" the 84.5% and 96.8% figures; check her source table.

### Batch 4: ritual, applause, *Nutcracker*
- **Les trois coups**: before the curtain the stage manager strikes the floor with the *brigadier* (a length of theatre batten covered in red velvet with gilt nails), traditionally eleven quick knocks then three; the Comédie-Française strikes six, three for each of the two troupes it merged in 1680 (Comédie-Française "Did you know?", institutional) [D]. Origin probably to get silence when theatre was played by daylight (WebLettres dossier, teaching page) [P]. Paris 2024 Olympics borrowed the ritual for sessions (NPR/MPR, 4 Aug 2024) [D]. https://www.comedie-francaise.fr/en/did-you-know
- **"The theatre begins at the cloakroom"** (Театр начинается с вешалки): traced by Gramota.ru's phraseology reference to Stanislavsky's letter of 23 Jan 1933 to the Moscow Art Theatre's cloakroom staff, answering their greetings on his 70th birthday: in our theatre "the performance begins from the moment one enters the building"; you are the first to meet the spectators [D: reference work quoting the letter]; also attributed to Nemirovich-Danchenko (Markov, 1974) [contested attribution]. → the best one-line statement of front-of-house as part of the work. https://gramota.ru/biblioteka/spravochniki/spravochnik-po-frazeologii/teatr-nachinaetsya-s-veshalki
- **Curtain calls**: Nureyev's *Swan Lake*, Vienna State Opera, 15 Oct 1964, Fonteyn and Nureyev: 89 curtain calls, described as a Guinness record (Rudolf Nureyev Foundation; PBS *Great Performances* 2025) [P: foundation and broadcaster figure; Guinness record not seen; one Foundation page wrongly dates the first night 1977]. PBS interviewee: queued all night, bought "masses of daffodils" to throw from the gods [D: broadcast transcript]. NYT, 8 May 1967: Fonteyn and Nureyev's return to the Met, "ovation of 17 minutes" ended only by lowering the fire curtain, "illegally thrown flowers" [D: newspaper of the day] → flower-throwing was against house rules at the Met. https://nureyev.org/discover-nureyev/the-nureyev-database/works/swan-lake-nureyev ; https://www.nytimes.com/1967/05/08/archives/dance-swan-lake-twice-and-doubly-memorable-fonteyn-and-nureyev.html
- ***Nutcracker* as door and engine**: first full-length US *Nutcracker*, San Francisco Ballet, 1944, Willam Christensen, budget $1,000; Balanchine NYCB 1954 credited with making it a holiday tradition; 750+ US productions estimated 2011 (NEA blog, 2021) [D: federal agency teaching page; figures as NEA reports them]. ### Batch 5: Paris subscribers and the Jockey Club; tickets, touts, dress today
- **Véron's 1831 decision**: subscribers (*abonnés*) "officially" gained the right to attend rehearsals and to enter the Foyer (France Musique, 15 Dec 2017, quoting the scholarly literature) [P: press; the Opéra's own history page says the bourgeoisie "came to meet the ballerinas at the Foyer de la danse" [D, institutional]]. - **The Jockey Club and *Tannhäuser*, 13 March 1861**: custom of a ballet in Act II, timed to the arrival of Jockey Club members after dinner; Wagner put his Bacchanale at the start; whistles and catcalls; withdrawn after three performances after 164 rehearsals (BSO programme note; Berliner Philharmoniker; Wikipedia) [D for the event; P for "dog whistles"/"silver whistles" detail; the BSO quotes Wagner's own description of "rich, frivolous gentlemen who have their mistresses in the ballet"]. → the clearest case of an audience faction setting the structure of a work (when the ballet could happen). https://www.bso.org/works/overture-and-venusberg-music-from-tannh%C3%A4user

### Batch 6: the audience of one; Soviet seat allocation; Gulf touring market
