## 0. In one paragraph

Ballet is a 325-year experiment in **storing a world that exists only in moving bodies and handing it to people who were not in the room**. Its first storage technology was paper (Feuillet's *Chorégraphie*, 1700); its second was a person (the répétiteur, the choreologist, the coach); its third was the screen (film, then video); its fourth, from 1989, was the computer (Cunningham's Life Forms, Forsythe's CD-ROM, motion capture, Motion Bank), and its fifth, from about 2018, is the generative model that proposes movement it has learned from other people's bodies. Each new store raised the same two questions, which are exactly the program's two threads. **AI Collaboration:** when a machine proposes and a person selects, who is the author, and whose body is in the training data? **Handover Grammar:** what must travel with a world (score, bible, plot, rider, licence, a living person) for a stranger to rebuild it without losing it? The record says, consistently, that **paper alone and memory alone both fail**, and that the documents that work best are the ones that state rules and minimums in numbers and then send a person to supervise the judgement the numbers cannot hold.

## 3. Stories

Each can open or carry a lesson. Status in brackets.

**1. The suitcase of notations (1918–2024).** Nikolai Sergeyev, régisseur of the Imperial ballet, left Russia with the Stepanov notations of the repertoire. In 1921 he used them to stage *The Sleeping Princess* for Diaghilev in London, then *Giselle* in Paris (1924) and the classics for the Vic-Wells in the 1930s. After his death the notations sat unread because almost no one could read Stepanov; Harvard bought them in 1967–69, and only after scholars learned the system did they feed the Kirov's 1999 *Sleeping Beauty* and Ratmansky's reconstructions. *Why it matters:* a handover document is useless without a trained reader. [D, W4 §3.2]

**2. "The bible" (1956–now).** Kenneth MacMillan worked with Benesh notators beside him for most of his career; the 1998 catalogue lists 82 scores of 60 of his works by 26 notators. His widow, Deborah MacMillan, insists his ballets are staged from the score, which he called "the bible"; he said Benesh caught his "original intention of movement ... every time". His earliest works were never notated. *Why it matters:* the record exists from the day someone was paid to keep it. [D, RAD PDF]

**3. Rebuilding a riot (1913–1987).** Nijinsky never wrote down *Le Sacre du printemps*, danced eight times in 1913. Millicent Hodson and Kenneth Archer spent more than a decade collecting fragments: Marie Rambert's score annotated in Russian, Stravinsky's notes on entrances, Valentine Gross's pastels of the formations, critics' descriptions, about half the costumes (found through Sotheby's), Roerich's designs, and Bronislava Nijinska's notes on the Chosen One's solo, the only part written down. The Joffrey premiered it in Los Angeles in 1987; Robert Joffrey estimated it "85 percent Nijinsky". *Why it matters:* paper without the bodies yields a plausible world, never a certain one. [D, CSM 1987; authenticity C]

**4. The Track button (1989–1991).** At about seventy, Merce Cunningham began composing with Life Forms, figure-animation software whose original design team included Thecla Schiphorst and Tom Calvert (developed at Simon Fraser University [P]); Schiphorst supported his use of it from 1989. *Trackers* (City Center, 19 March 1991) was the earliest dance he developed "in part" with it; the title came from the software's "Track" button. *Why it matters:* the earliest well-documented case of a choreographer treating software as a generator of options he then tested on dancers. [D, Cunningham Trust; Langlois]

**5. Dancing inside herself (1999).** For *BIPED* (Berkeley, 1999), Paul Kaiser and Shelley Eshkar motion-captured about five minutes of Cunningham's choreography and projected it as dot, stick and "chronophotographic" bodies alongside the live dancers; Cunningham set the order of the animations by dice. Jeannie Steele, haloed by her own captured motion, said it felt "as if I were dancing inside myself". *Why it matters:* the dancer meets her data double on stage, 11 years before *Black Swan* used one without telling the audience. [D, Kaiser]

**6. The CD-ROM that died (1994–2023).** Forsythe and the ZKM built *Improvisation Technologies* so new dancers joining Ballett Frankfurt could learn his movement operations on their own: 63 short lecture-demonstrations with white lines drawn over the video so that movements "leave visible traces in space". Published on CD-ROM in 1999, it became unplayable on current computers; in 2023 the ZKM and Forsythe Productions rebuilt the animations from the original video as a website. *Why it matters:* a handover medium has a lifespan; somebody must budget the migration. [D, ZKM]

**7. Planning the end (2007–2012).** Cunningham and his foundation drafted a Legacy Plan in 2007: after his death the company would tour for two years and then close, and the works would live on through licensing and digital "Dance Capsules". He read the draft and said, "I'm glad you're taking care of the dancers." He died on 26 July 2009; the Legacy Tour played 152 performances; the last shows at the Park Avenue Armory (29–31 Dec 2011) sold $10 tickets at his request; 86 Capsules (planned: 50) passed to the Trust. *Why it matters:* the clearest case of a maker designing the handover of a world as deliberately as the world. [D, Legacy Plan]

**8. Who inherits a dance (1983–2005).** Balanchine left his ballets to individuals in his will (Karin von Aroldingen, for example, received rights in six ballets and a share of royalties on 37); the heirs then formed the Trust that licenses them. Martha Graham left hers to her heir Ronald Protas, but the courts held that from 1966 she had been an employee of her own Center, so most of her later dances were works "for hire" that belonged to the Center. *Why it matters:* ownership decided whether the worlds could be staged at all. [D, Wikipedia; Playbill 2005]

**9. The disappeared double (2011).** In *Black Swan* (2010), ABT soloist Sarah Lane danced many of the full-body shots for Natalie Portman; her face was in some shots digitally replaced with Portman's. After the Oscar, Lane said only "5 percent" of the full-body shots were Portman; the director's editor counted 111 of 139 dance shots as Portman. Lane's credit was "Stunts". *Why it matters:* the clearest public case of a composited dancer and an erased co-author, and of credit as a contested measurement. [D for statements; C for the shares]

**10. The archive that suggests (2019).** For *Living Archive* (Dorothy Chandler Pavilion, Los Angeles, 12 July 2019; music by Thomas Adès), Google Arts & Culture Lab built a tool from McGregor's 25-year video archive (Google: almost half a million "moments" of movement, about 100 hours of video for the performance tool; the company: "hundreds of hours", plus solos made with each current dancer). Given a phrase, it proposed several possible next phrases. The company credits the choreography to McGregor "in collaboration with the dancers", and names nine. *Why it matters:* the closest ballet case to the program's Stranger: a system that has read the world's past and proposes its next move. [D, company and Google pages; figures differ]

**11. The dataset with no ballet and no consent terms (2019–2023).** The dance-generation models most often cited (AI Choreographer, 2021; EDGE, 2022–23) learn from AIST++, reconstructed from Japan's AIST Dance Video Database: 40 professional dancers recorded "in a professional studio" in ten street and jazz genres. The founding paper says the music is "copyright-cleared" but reports nothing on how the dancers were recruited, paid, credited or what they consented to; ballet appears only as "ballet jazz". *Why it matters:* the model's "world" is the sum of uncredited bodies, and a gap in the record is itself a finding. [D, papers read]

**12. Four counts in Fortnite (2023–2024).** Choreographer Kyle Hanagami sued Epic Games, saying the emote "It's Complicated" copied four counts of his routine to Charlie Puth's "How Long". The district court dismissed the claim; on 1 Nov 2023 the Ninth Circuit reversed, holding that individual moves are like unprotectable ideas but a choreographer's "selection and arrangement" can be protected, and that reducing choreography to poses is like reducing music to "notes". The case settled in 2024. *Why it matters:* the law protects the arrangement, not the vocabulary, which is exactly what a world's DNA is. [D ruling; P settlement]

**13. The likeness clause (2025).** On 24 November 2025 AGMA ratified a three-year agreement with New York City Ballet that includes a "New article addressing the use of artificial intelligence as it relates to Artists' likenesses" and policies on "technology and data". Since 1 January 2025 New York law has voided certain contracts for a performer's digital replica if they lack a "reasonably specific description" of the use and the performer had no counsel or union. In London, Equity balloted film and TV performers, dancers included, on refusing digital scans until producers agree informed consent for AI training (4–18 Dec 2025). *Why it matters:* the dancer's body is being written into contracts as data; the dispute has moved from outputs (replicas) to inputs (training). [D; clause text not public, thin]

**14. A choreographer flies to Cairo (1958–1970).** Egypt's Minister of Culture Tharwat Okasha asked the Soviet Union for help with a national ballet school in 1958; a Bolshoi expert came, 35 children were chosen, and in 1963 five students went to study at the Bolshoi. On 3 December 1966 the Bolshoi's Leonid Lavrovsky came to stage *The Fountain of Bakhchisarai* at the Cairo Opera House; Serge Lifar made *Daphnis and Chloe* there in 1970. The stated goal was "a completely independent Egyptian company as soon as possible." *Why it matters:* the Cairo company's first full-length ballet arrived by person, not by paper, and the handover was planned to end in independence. [D, Aramco World 1977]

**15. What *Swan Lake* asks of a building (2025).** A touring *Swan Lake* rider (Ballet Jörgen, Canada, V3, Sept 2025) asks for a stage at least 27 × 25 ft, a sprung floor, stage and dressing rooms at 22°C before the dancers arrive, a house plot so the company's lighting plot can be pre-hung, a stage manager calling every cue from stage right, and paths "illuminated for barefoot dancers". Les Ballets Trockadero's international rider (2023) wants the plot four weeks ahead, 32 consecutive hours of set-up, a translator if the stage language is not English, and no recording without written permission. *Why it matters:* the rider is a world's minimum conditions, stated in numbers to a stranger. [D, riders read]

## 4. The concepts and debates

1. **Archive against repertoire.** Diana Taylor (2003) argues that documents ("supposedly enduring materials") and embodied practice are two systems of memory, both selective, neither reducible to the other [D via secondary, p. 19–21]. Peggy Phelan (*Unmarked*, 1993) argues that performance exists only in the present and that recording makes it something else [P, book not seen]; Philip Auslander (*Liveness*, 1999) answers that "live" is itself a category made by recording media [P]. André Lepecki (2010) argues the dancer's body is itself an archive that re-enactment opens [P]. *For the course:* a handover package is an archive; the répétiteur is the repertoire; the Auditor should check that both are present.

2. **Notation against video.** Benesh International and the MacMillan estate: start from the score, because video shows "one interpretation on the day", errors and all, from one angle and without reasons [D, Crompton 2024]. Rambert: works are now kept on film [D, same]. The Ashton Foundation: film is "a vital part of the tool chest" but not the primary record [D]. Underneath: is a world handed over as **rules** or as **instances**? [A]

3. **Authentic reconstruction against living tradition.** Reconstructors (Hodson and Archer; Vikharev; Ratmansky) treat paper sources as authority; companies that never stopped dancing a work treat their own lineage as authority, and have denounced reconstructions (Scholl, in W4) [D]. Joffrey's "85 percent" shows the reconstructors' own uncertainty [D]. [C]

4. **Who is the author when the machine proposes?** The Cunningham Trust: *Trackers* was developed "in part" with Life Forms (the choreographer stays author) [D]. Studio Wayne McGregor: the vocabulary of *Living Archive* was generated by the AI and the choreography is McGregor's "in collaboration with the dancers" [D, company's description]. McGregor and deLahunta elsewhere frame such systems as "thinking tools" that extend the choreographer's options (W4) [D]. Forsythe frames choreography as "an organizational practice" that can be held in objects (W4) [D]. The datasets' silence on the dancers (story 11) is the counter-case: authorship claimed for the model's builders, none recorded for the bodies. [A]

5. **What copyright protects.** US law protects choreographic works as "selection and arrangement", not single moves or social dances (*Hanagami*, 2023) [D]. Kraut (2016) argues choreographic copyright has historically served some dancers (often white, often concert-stage) better than others whose movement was copied [P, book summary]. *For the course:* protecting a world means protecting its arrangement rules, which is what the Concept DNA holds. [A]

6. **Training against output.** Equity and Pact (Dec 2025) agree on consent and payment for **outputs** (digital replicas) and disagree on **inputs** (using recordings and scans to train AI) [D, Televisual]. The AIST case shows inputs gathered with no terms reported [D]. Union position: consent, transparency, pay. Producers' position: output uses settled; training terms unresolved [D].

7. **Work for hire against personal authorship.** The Graham rulings made the employer the author of works made by an employee-choreographer [D]. Balanchine's will treated each ballet as personal property to bequeath [D]. The Trust model and the Cunningham Trust model both turn a maker's worlds into licensed assets. [A]

8. **Data about the dancer's body.** Performance scientists argue load data can reduce injury risk [D, Shaw, vendor post]; Shaw also says he cannot ask artistic staff to change schedules from the previous day's data [D]. No injury-reduction figures were seen. Who owns and sees a dancer's jump count is not discussed in sources found [thin].

9. **The licence as quality control or gatekeeping.** The Balanchine Trust sets fees by the licensee's budget and requires a Trust-approved répétiteur [D]; defenders call this protecting the work's style; critics call it limiting who may dance it (scope 05 has the debate) [C].

## 5. Lessons for world-builders

1. **Hand over a package and a person.** Every ballet that survived well travelled with paper and with someone who had danced or coached it (Sergeyev, MacMillan's notators, the Balanchine répétiteur, Lavrovsky in Cairo). Paper alone gave the *Sacre* an "85 percent" world; memory alone left MacMillan's earliest works unrecorded. Your package should name its répétiteur: the person who can answer what the documents cannot.
2. **Write rules, not only examples.** A video is one performance on one day; a score is the rule that produced it. Hand over the grammar (operations, constraints, tolerances), then the exemplars. This is Forsythe's and Benesh's lesson, and it is what the Stranger needs.
3. **Layer the handover set.** Ballet's set is a working template: the score (what moves), the bible (what it looks like, with swatches), the plot and cue sheet (light and time), the rider (what the receiving building must provide), the licence (who may change what) and the répétiteur (judgement). Audit your world package against those six layers.
4. **State minimums in numbers.** 27 × 25 ft, 22°C, 32 hours of set-up, the plot four weeks ahead. A rider works because a stranger can check it. Vague requirements become disputes.
5. **Expect the building not to be yours.** The rider and the venue specification are a negotiation ("advancing"); the Royal Danish Opera's rig "cannot be moved or reconfigured". Design the world so it degrades gracefully when the host cannot comply.
6. **Evaluate the receiver.** The Balanchine Trust asks for a video of the whole company before it licenses. A handover brief should say what the receiving team must already be able to do.
7. **Plan the format's death.** The 1999 CD-ROM died in 24 years; RePlay went offline after 12. Put a migration owner and budget into the package.
8. **Decide inheritance early.** Balanchine's will, Graham's "work for hire" and Cunningham's Legacy Plan show that ownership and succession decide whether a world can be staged after its maker. Write the lifecycle field before you need it.
9. **When a machine proposes, keep the selection visible.** Record which options the tool offered and which the people chose, as Cunningham's "in part" and McGregor's credit line do imperfectly. That log is your authorship evidence.
10. **Name every body in the data.** If your world's tool learned from people, list them and their terms; if you cannot, say so. Separate consent for training from consent for outputs.
11. **Credit is a measurement; agree the method in advance.** *Black Swan* produced three different percentages from the same footage. Decide before the work how contributions will be counted and credited.
12. **Distrust "first".** Every "first AI ballet" has an earlier one. Say "earliest known" and date it.

**Thread checkpoints (proposed).**
- *AI Collaboration (Stranger Lab; F2.13 parametric first rule; M2.15; R5.7):* give the Stranger only the learner's written rules (no archive, no conversation), let it propose three "next phrases" of the world, and have the learner select and log, as McGregor's dancers did with *Living Archive*. Then ask: what in the Stranger's output came from your rules, and what from its training data?
- *Handover Grammar (Handover Auditor; F2; M4.4; R3.8, R3.11):* the learner writes a one-page rider (minimums in numbers), one costume-bible page (with a swatch or material code) and a one-paragraph licence (what a receiver may and may not change), then names the répétiteur and lists three questions only that person could answer.

## 7. Gaps and traps

**Where the record is thin.**
- **AI in casting:** no documented case found of a ballet company using AI to cast. Do not imply one exists.
- **AI in rehearsal and teaching:** mostly prototypes (Johannesburg 2018, 59.6% accuracy; aiDance, a design study with no outcome data).
- **Injury monitoring:** the main source is the vendor's own blog (Catapult, 2019); no injury-reduction figures; who owns the data not discussed.
- **The AGMA–NYCB AI article:** only the union's summary is public in what was seen; do not paraphrase its terms beyond "an article addressing the use of artificial intelligence as it relates to Artists' likenesses".
- **Production and costume bibles:** almost never public; one V&A example; the ROH "costume bible" source is student journalism. No scholarship on the bible as a genre was found.
- **Riders:** the public ones are from small and mid-size North American companies; the Royal Ballet's, Bolshoi's and Mariinsky's are not public. Gulf venue specifications (Dubai Opera, ROHM) were not found online.
- **Global:** the Gulf, sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America and post-1991 Russia are thin on both parts. Candidate leads: Cuba's transmission of Alonso's *Giselle*; the Bolshoi and Mariinsky touring riders to Gulf houses; South African companies' licensing of Balanchine and MacMillan.
- **Life Forms history:** the SFU development dates, Tom Calvert's role and the commercial DanceForms versions (Credo Interactive) were not confirmed at source.
- **The Chinese model-ballet film as the copying standard:** probable, not confirmed at source.

**Traps.**
1. **"First" claims everywhere.** The Cunningham Trust calls *Trackers* "the first dance" made partly with Life Forms; Wallpaper* calls *Fusion* (2023) "the world's first AI ballet"; *Atomos* (2013) and *Living Archive* (2019) precede it. Use "earliest known" and date it.
2. **Conflicting numbers.** *Living Archive*: "hundreds of hours" (company) against about 100 hours and half a million moments (Google). *Black Swan*: 5% (Lane) against 80–90% (director) against 12 of 35 full-body shots (editor). Give both readings and the source of each.
3. **Company claims are claims.** McGregor's page says the vocabulary was generated by the AI; label it as the company's description.
4. **Silence is not refusal.** The AIST papers report no consent terms; say "not reported", not "no consent was given".
5. **Copyright is jurisdiction-bound.** *Hanagami* and the Graham rulings are US law; UK and EU rules differ. Do not generalise.
6. **Notation is not a recording, and nor is video.** Both are selective (`../scope/02` trap; Crompton 2024).
7. **Links die.** RePlay (offline since 2021) and the Forsythe CD-ROM show it; recheck every URL before learner release.
8. **Quotes via secondary sources.** Taylor's phrases and page numbers came from a secondary summary; check against the book before quoting to learners.
9. **Japanese names in Japanese order:** 真鍋大度 Manabe Daito; MIKIKO is a single professional name. The AIST authors are listed here in the papers' Western order (Shuhei Tsuchida et al.); if named in learner text use Tsuchida Shuhei.
10. **Do not duplicate W4.** Forsythe's and McGregor's theory sits there; this file supplies the dated cases and documents.

### Log 1. Machine as co-choreographer (25 Oct 2026)

### Log 2. Generative models and their datasets
- AIST Dance Video Database (Tsuchida, Fukayama, Hamasaki, Goto; AIST, Japan; ISMIR 2019): 40 professional dancers (25 male, 15 female), 13,939 videos, 10 street and jazz genres; no ballet genre except "ballet jazz"; recorded "in a professional studio"; music "copyright-cleared". The paper says nothing on how dancers were recruited, paid, or what they consented to, and states no database licence [D, ISMIR PDF read].
- AIST++ (Li, Yang, Ross, Kanazawa; arXiv 2101.08779, 2021; ICCV 2021): 5.2 hours, 1,408 sequences, 10 genres, 3D motion reconstructed from the AIST videos; Google blog 13 Sept 2021 says "30 subjects"; no consent discussion [D].
- EDGE (Tseng, Castellon, Liu; arXiv 2211.10658, Nov 2022; CVPR 2023): diffusion model plus Jukebox music features; "joint-wise conditioning, and in-betweening" (the user can fix some joints and fill gaps) [D, abstract]. Training set AIST++ [P, from paper body, not seen].
- Reading [A]: the field's benchmark dance data are street dance captured from 30–40 named-but-uncredited professionals in Japan; ballet is almost absent from the training data; consent and credit are not reported in the founding papers.

- Les Ballets Trockadero, International Engagement rider (2023): preferred stage 44 ft × 32 ft; black Marley floor always, sprung floor for more than three performances; rosin allowed; 72°F at least 3 hours before class; light plot sent at least four weeks ahead (up to 7 electrics, 260 lamps, 200 dimmers); presenter supplies all equipment and crew; at least 32 consecutive hours of pre-hang and set-up; wardrobe for 60 costumes; translator if the stage language is not English; §19 no recording "without the express written permission of the Company Director", archival taping only by agreement [D, PDF read].
- New York digital replica law (S7676B, effective 1 Jan 2025): a contract for a performer's digital replica "in place of work the individual would otherwise have performed in person" is void if it also lacks a "reasonably specific description" of use and the performer had no counsel or union [D, Littler summary]. Context for the NYCB clause [A].
