# S. How to structure a ballet module: a structure study

21 October 2026. Research on organising principles for a 30–40 learner-hour ballet module in F1 "Histories of making", not the module itself. Inputs read: `README.md`, `schema/unit-spec-template.md`, `schema/program.json`, `schema/concept-dna.schema.json`, `../UNIT_PLAN.md`, the "Size" sections of `../scope/01`–`06`, and `BRIEF_WORKS.md` (the seventh strand: each ballet as a made work).

Confidence marks: **documented** (seen at source in this pass), **probable** (secondary or summary seen, primary not opened), **contested** (both readings given), **argued** (our own judgement). No URL here is composed; each was returned by a search or fetch.

## 0. The design constraints this study assesses against

- Adult, self-paced, design students and briefers; phone-first; sittings of about twenty minutes.
- Two lanes in every unit (Maker, Briefer); three depths (Orientation, Practice, Mastery).
- One artifact across the program: the Concept DNA (fields PRM premise, PER perimeter, RUL rules, ANT anti-DNA, ALN, OBL obligations, TST tests, BRF brief, and others).
- Existing material: six angle files (A court and paper; B made body; C stage; D things made for dancing; E who pays, owns, survives; F routes out; G who was kept out) each sized at 4–8 hours; a seventh strand (works as made concepts) in progress.
- The current plan (UNIT_PLAN §6) is thematic by angle: six sessions of about two hours.
- F1's threshold concept: "Design history is a web with obscured strands, not a line". F1 units test at 45–55 minutes (about ten on a phone) and carry three consumption forms (five-minute, session, reference). In the Concept DNA, F1 fills mainly SRC; premise, rules and translation fields belong to later modules (Log 4).

## 1. Evidence base: what the learning science says, and how firm it is

| Claim | Strength | Source (see logs for URLs) | What it means for this module [argued] |
|---|---|---|---|
| Learners need a usable framework of the past to hang depth studies on; depth alone leaves fragments | Probable (UK history-teaching research tradition; argued more than tested) | Shemilt; Lee and Howson; Howson 2009; Dawson; Barnes (Log 2) | Whatever the order, give an overview early and return to it. Barnes's "overview frames the unit" is the model |
| Chronological vs thematic order: neither is shown better for outcomes | Contested; evidence mostly teacher opinion | EJ1241024 (2020); Dordt thesis (2026) | Do not choose on chronology alone. Choose on reference points: a few anchor works and dates the learner meets repeatedly |
| Sense of time grows by comparing against reference points, not by sequence | Probable (secondary citation of Stow and Haydn 2000; Barton) | Log 2 | Anchor works (e.g. 1581, 1841, 1890, 1913, 1964, 1995) serve better than a full sequence |
| Interleaving helps learners tell visual styles apart | Documented (meta-analysis; paintings g = 0.67) | Kornell and Bjork 2008; Brunmair and Richter 2019 | Mix images of different periods and works when teaching how to *see* ballet's looks; block arguments |
| Interleaving does not help (or hurts) with expository text and words | Documented (same meta-analysis) | Brunmair and Richter 2019 | Keep each sitting's argument in one block |
| Worked examples beat problem solving for novices, also in design-style tasks | Documented (two experiments, undergraduates learning designers’ styles) | Rourke and Sweller 2009 | Take several works apart fully before asking the learner to do the same with their own world |
| Guidance should fade as knowledge grows (expertise reversal) | Probable | Kalyuga; Kirschner et al. 2006 | Early sessions guided; later sessions open; the last act can be studio-like |
| Problem-based learning improves skills, not knowledge | Documented (meta-analysis of 43) but contested in interpretation | Dochy et al. 2003; Kirschner et al. 2006 vs Hmelo-Silver et al. 2007 | A pure studio structure would cost F1 its history knowledge, which is F1's job |
| Stories are understood and remembered better than essays | Documented (meta-analysis, g ≈ 0.55; some publication bias) | Mar et al. 2021 | A work's life (idea, team, premiere, afterlife) is a ready story; exploit it |
| Case-based learning is liked and active; outcome evidence is mixed | Documented (systematic review) | BEME Guide 23 (2012) | Cases are a sound vehicle but not magic; their value lies in the template and the transfer task |
| Case libraries work when cases share a template and are indexed by the issues met | Probable (design-based science classrooms; thin evaluation) | Kolodner et al. 2003–05 | Write every work on one template and index it to Concept DNA fields so the learner can reuse it |
| Threshold concepts are portals that should be revisited, not crammed | Contested framework; useful heuristic | Meyer and Land 2003; Land et al. 2006; critique 2019 | Choose three or four ballet thresholds (below) and pass through each more than once |
| Spiral: revisit topics at rising difficulty | Descriptive; indirect support from spacing research | Bruner 1960 (names history); Harden and Stamper 1999; Cepeda et al. 2006 | Return to one or two works across the module at greater depth |
| Learner-paced segments beat continuous delivery | Documented (multimedia research) | Mayer 2005 | Twenty-minute sittings suit this; each sitting a complete segment |
| Backward design: decide the end result and evidence first | Practitioner framework | Wiggins and McTighe | The comparison in §5 starts from each structure's end product |

**Candidate threshold concepts for ballet** [argued, from UNIT_PLAN §2 and §4]: (1) the work is a set of versions, and the record decides what the work is; (2) the credit names one person and the making involves many; (3) the dancer's body is a made thing; (4) the canon line (Paris, St Petersburg, Paris, New York) is one drawing of a web, which is F1's own threshold in ballet form. A structure should pass through each of these at least twice.

## 2. Precedents: how others structure this material

| Precedent | Organising principle | Reading for this module [argued] |
|---|---|---|
| V&A, *Diaghilev* (2010) | Chronological galleries (to 1914; 1920s) with a middle gallery "behind the scenes": *Firebird* from designs to backcloth | Chronological frame + one work taken apart. The closest precedent for the hybrid |
| NGA, *Diaghilev* (2013) | Same objects re-cut by discipline: Diaghilev; choreographers; music; design; legacy | Thematic sections each re-run 1909–29: the repetition cost of angle structures, visible |
| Homans, *Apollo's Angels* (2010) | Chronological by national tradition (France, Denmark, Italy, Russia, Britain, America) | The "line" F1 argues against; a good reference spine, a poor course spine |
| Kant (ed.), *Cambridge Companion* (2007) | Four periods, each named by a thesis ("ballet is a woman"); chapters on themes and works; index of ballets | "Periods with a thesis": a strong pattern for naming acts |
| Royal Ballet School *Ballet History Timeline* | Linear chronology, 750 images, 67,000 words, desktop only | Reference form, not a course; too dense for a phone |
| Company resources (ENB, RBO) | One production at a time: story, character, design, credits, extracts | The industry explains ballet by the work. Supports cases |
| University courses (scope 06 §5) | Mostly chronological survey (Ball State, Swarthmore, UCSC); Aberdeen MU4085 by ten ballets, each a named collaboration; Penn by libretti; Bennington by design projects | Works-as-cases is an established university pattern for the Ballets Russes; design courses use making |

## 3. Dose: how much per hour, per sitting, per module

- **Per sitting (20 minutes).** Adults read non-fiction silently at about 238 words a minute (Brysbaert 2019) [documented]. Study reading with images, captions and a short task runs far slower. A realistic sitting carries about **1,000–1,500 words, 4–6 images, one short video under 6 minutes (Guo et al. 2014), and one task** [argued]. This matches the scope files' own sizing ("one page, about 20 minutes", about 1,200 words). One sitting should carry **one claim and one object or one work-moment**, with at most a handful of new names [argued from cognitive-load theory; no study sets the number].
- **Per hour.** About three sittings: roughly one F1 unit at the tested 45–55 minutes [documented: F1 deep dives 1]. So 30–40 hours is about **90–120 sittings**, or about **35–45 unit-sized pieces** [arithmetic].
- **Per module.** *A History of the World in 100 Objects* ran 100 sittings of 15 minutes over 20 weeks at five a week [documented]. A ballet module of 90–120 sittings at five a week is **18–24 weeks** [arithmetic]. MOOC completion falls as courses lengthen; the first two weeks decide engagement; after that active learners level off (Jordan 2015) [documented]. Fewer than one in five learners who intend to finish an open course does so; paying learners finish about half the time (Reich and Ruipérez-Valiente 2019) [documented]. There is no measured "maximum length" for a module; there is a measured cost to length [documented], and the "10-minute attention span" is not well founded (Bradbury 2016) [documented].
- **Design rule this implies [argued].** Build the module as **three or four acts of 8–12 hours**, each completable alone, each ending in a product the learner keeps (a filled Concept DNA record). Make the first two weeks the strongest and most concrete part (a work, a body, an object, not a court treatise). Let a learner enter at any act after a short shared overview.

## 4. The seven structures assessed

Each entry gives: what it is here; evidence; precedents; strengths; risks; how the learner builds a concept; verdict. Concept DNA note: in the program, F1 fills mainly **SRC**; PRM, RUL, ANT, GRM, ADJ, RGT and LCY belong to later modules. So in F1 a learner "builds a concept" by collecting **dissected precedents**, filed in SRC and tagged to the fields they will inform later, plus at most a first-draft premise [argued from `concept-dna.schema.json`].

### 4.1 Chronological survey (court to now)

- **Here:** six to ten periods from 1581 to now, each with its works, people, institutions and objects.
- **Evidence:** supports a framework and narrative (Log 2, Log 4); thematic-vs-chronological outcome evidence is contested.
- **Precedents:** Homans; the *Cambridge Companion*'s parts; the RBS timeline; most university surveys; *100 Objects* (with objects as the unit).
- **Strengths:** clear sense of period and cause; easy to navigate; the Historian voice fits; works sit in their time.
- **Risks:** reproduces the line F1's threshold concept rejects (Paris, St Petersburg, Paris, New York); angles (labour, materials, money) become background; the module opens on the court, the least concrete material for design learners, in the two weeks that decide retention; the learner's own concept waits until the end.
- **Concept building:** weak. Each period yields facts; the learner collects no reusable pattern unless the template is added.
- **Verdict [argued]:** good as the *frame* (the order of acts, a reference timeline), poor as the whole structure.

### 4.2 Thematic by angle (the current six-session plan)

- **Here:** court and paper; the made body; the stage; things made for dancing; who pays, owns, survives; routes out.
- **Evidence:** thematic teaching is argued to aid multiple perspectives and links to the present (contested); interleaving periods inside a theme helps visual discrimination (documented) but not argument (documented).
- **Precedents:** NGA 2013 (by discipline); Tate 2000 (thematic hang, later revised); the six scope files themselves.
- **Strengths:** each angle is a design discipline the program later teaches (body → S3, stage → S1, objects → S2, money and rights → R2), so the module seeds later work; concepts lead and facts serve; matches the research already done.
- **Risks:** the timeline is rebuilt six times (the NGA effect), raising load at each session start; a single work is scattered across sessions (*Giselle* appears in body, stage, things and money), so the seventh strand has no home; the making tasks are separate exercises with no cumulative product; at 30–40 hours each angle would run 5–7 hours, deep enough to feel like six short courses.
- **Concept building:** moderate. Each angle can feed a later field (body → OBL and GRM candidates; stage → ANC candidates; money → RGT; routes → ADJ and LCY), but the learner never sees one concept whole.
- **Verdict [argued]:** right for the 12-hour unit (where the plan already sits), wrong as the spine of a 30–40-hour module. Keep the angles as **lenses**.

### 4.3 Case-based around works

- **Here:** each session takes one ballet from idea to afterlife (premise, roots, originator, team, world rules, production, reception, versions), with the six angles distributed so each case foregrounds one or two.
- **Evidence:** worked examples help novices in design-style tasks (documented); stories beat essays (documented); case-based learning is active and liked, with mixed outcome evidence (documented); case libraries work when cases share a template and are indexed by issue (probable).
- **Precedents:** Aberdeen MU4085 (ten Ballets Russes works, each a named collaboration); company production resources; the V&A's *Firebird* gallery; the *Companion*'s index of ballets; CalArts' master-class weeks.
- **Strengths:** gives the seventh strand its home; each case is a worked example of exactly what the program asks the learner to do later (a concept with roots made into a world and an experience); each case is completable in a week, which suits self-paced attrition; the credit question (who had the idea, who is named) arises in every case, so the Attribution thread runs through.
- **Risks:** **template fatigue** (the same eight headings eight times); angles uneven across works (*The Rite* has no shoe story; court ballet has little budget record); **canon capture**: famous works are the line again, and the Cairo, Havana, Beijing and Tokyo routes have fewer documented "works", so cases must be chosen to include them; **overload per case** if every angle is attempted; chronology lost unless cases are ordered in time.
- **Concept building:** strong. Each case files an SRC precedent card with candidate entries for PRM (premise), PER, RUL and ANT (the world's rules and refusals), GRM (its vocabulary), RGT (who paid and owned) and ADJ/LCY (how it travelled and was remade).
- **Verdict [argued]:** the strongest single unit of content for this module, if (a) the template is light and varies its emphasis, (b) cases are ordered chronologically, and (c) the case list is chosen against the canon line.

### 4.4 Object-based (one object per session, read outward)

- **Here:** a Feuillet page, a Taglioni shoe, a Ciceri sketch, a Bakst drawing, a *Parade* curtain, a Sergeyev notation, a brown shoe, a Cairo programme, each read outward to makers, institutions and routes.
- **Evidence:** object-based learning in higher education rests mainly on case reports (probable); it fits F1's existing machinery (Atlas canon nodes, verified open objects).
- **Precedents:** *100 Objects*; the V&A/RCA "Artefacts in History"; F1's own units.
- **Strengths:** concrete; one object fits one phone sitting; strong for attribution and materials; uses the open records already verified.
- **Risks:** ballet's central fact is that "the work leaves no object" (UNIT_PLAN §2.2); an object structure privileges what survives and what is open (Degas, Bakst), which is the record's own bias; the work and the body slip out; objects alone give no model of a concept.
- **Concept building:** weak to moderate (good SRC records; little on premise or rules).
- **Verdict [argued]:** the right **unit of a sitting**, not of the module. Each case should be entered through an object.

### 4.5 Problem- or studio-based

- **Here:** each session poses a making problem (write a notation; brief a body; scale a cloth; credit a production; fund a season; remake a classic) and uses history as evidence.
- **Evidence:** PBL helps skills and may cost knowledge (documented); novices need worked examples first (documented); the guidance debate is contested.
- **Precedents:** CalArts' concept pipeline; Bennington's Ballets Russes design projects; the scope files' making tasks; architecture's history-studio gap.
- **Strengths:** motivating for designers; natural two-lane fit (Maker solves, Briefer briefs and judges); direct Concept DNA work.
- **Risks:** history becomes a quarry of examples; F1's job is history, and the M-modules (M1 premise, M2 Concept DNA, M4 make and test) already own the studio sequence, so this would duplicate them; chronology disappears; open problems too early overload novices.
- **Concept building:** strongest on paper, but in the wrong module: F1 should collect precedents, not set rules.
- **Verdict [argued]:** use for **the apply task of each act** and possibly a short **closing act**, not as the structure.

### 4.6 Spiral or braided

- **Variant A: a few works revisited at rising depth** (e.g. *Giselle* and *Swan Lake* met in the overview, again as full cases, again as remakes).
- **Variant B: one work re-made across centuries as the spine.** *Swan Lake* (Moscow 1877; Petipa–Ivanov 1895; Soviet versions; Tokyo 1946; Bourne 1995; later remakes) or *Giselle* (Paris 1841; Petipa's St Petersburg revisions; Alonso's Cuban version; Ek 1982; Khan 2016; Masilo) [dates probable; to be verified in W1–W3].
- **Evidence:** spiral curricula are well described and little tested (descriptive); spacing is robust (documented); F1's Edo research found "the same design shown in multiple impressions" a strong move (documented in repo).
- **Precedents:** scope 06's "Spine B: one work re-made"; reconstruction debates (Sergeyev notations); F1's edition-and-states teaching.
- **Strengths:** makes threshold 1 (the work is its versions) concrete; chronology rides inside one familiar story, so context costs fall and capacity goes to the lens; remaking is the learner's own later task (ADJ, LCY); routes out appear naturally (Tokyo's *Swan Lake*, Havana's *Giselle*); Bourne's *Swan Lake* is a design-led remake suited to design learners.
- **Risks:** one work cannot carry 30–40 hours; court ballet (1581–1700) has no surviving work to braid; versions on film are mostly link-only; risk of boredom with one score and story; *Swan Lake* as spine re-centres the canon.
- **Concept building:** strong for ADJ and LCY candidates (how a concept survives translation), moderate elsewhere.
- **Verdict [argued]:** excellent as a **recurring thread** (one sitting per act returns to the braid work), too narrow as the whole spine.

### 4.7 Hybrids

- **7a. Chronological spine of works with angle lenses.** Acts are periods named by a thesis (the *Companion* pattern); inside each act, two or three works are taken apart as cases; each case foregrounds one or two angles, so every angle appears at least twice across the module, interleaved across periods. Precedents: V&A 2010; Tate 2006 hubs; *100 Objects*' weekly headings.
- **7b. Acts within a module.** Three or four completable acts of 8–12 hours, each with its own end product, with a short shared overview at the start (Barnes) and a closing act that turns precedents into a brief.
- **Evidence for hybrids:** each component inherits its own evidence above; no study tests the combination [argued]. The museum field's movement from pure thematic to framed hybrids is a practice signal, not evidence of learning.
- **Strengths:** each level of the module has one organising principle, which keeps it legible: **module → acts (chronology); act → works (cases); work → sittings (objects and lenses); across acts → a braid work (spiral); end of each act → a making task (studio)**.
- **Risks:** complexity for authors; risk of trying to carry all six angles in every case; needs strict rules on case length and lens choice.
- **Verdict [argued]:** the best fit, provided the nesting is strict.

## 6. The strongest candidates

### Candidate A. "Works in acts": a chronological spine of works, angles as lenses, a braid work, one product per act (7a + 7b, with 3, 4, 6 and 5 nested)

- **Shape.** A short overview (about 1–2 hours: the web, the anchor dates, how to read a work and its record, the V&A/RCA "method first" move). Then three acts of about 10–12 hours, each a period named by a thesis, for example: *Act I, Court, academy and the Romantic stage (1581–1850): a dance becomes a standard and a picture*; *Act II, Empire and the avant-garde (1850–1930): the work as a total design*; *Act III, States, routes and remakes (1930–now): who carries a ballet, and who changes it*. Each act takes apart two or three works as cases, entered through an object, with one or two lenses foregrounded per case so that each of the six angles appears at least twice in the module. One braid work (*Giselle* or *Swan Lake*) returns once per act. Each act ends with a making task.
- **Case list rule.** At least one case per act outside the Paris–St Petersburg–New York line (e.g. a Soviet-model work made in Beijing or Havana; Tokyo's 1946 *Swan Lake*), with the canon cases read for their routes [argued; candidates to be verified in W1–W3].
- **Why.** It keeps chronology as the frame (Barnes, Howson), uses works as worked examples (Rourke and Sweller) and stories (Mar et al.), interleaves visual looks across periods (Brunmair and Richter), spaces the braid work (Cepeda), and gives each act an end, which addresses length-related attrition (Jordan). It matches the V&A's own structure for the Ballets Russes.
- **Risks to manage.** A light case template (five headings, not ten); a case runs 5–8 sittings, no more; lenses chosen per case, not all six; first act opens on a concrete work and object, not on a treatise.
- **Learner's end result.** A **dated case library of six to nine dissected ballets** in SRC, each card holding premise, roots, originator, team and credit gaps, world rules and refusals, production, and afterlife, and each tagged to the Concept DNA fields it will inform (PRM, RUL, ANT, GRM, RGT, ADJ, LCY); plus a **version history of the braid work**; plus three act products: Maker lane, a remake proposal for one case; Briefer lane, a commissioning brief and judged set for the same. This feeds M1 and M2 directly without doing their work.

### Candidate B. "One work, remade": a braided spine with satellite cases (6b + 3)

- **Shape.** One work (*Giselle* is the stronger choice for a design course: Heine's source, Gautier's livret, Paris 1841, Petipa's revisions, Alonso in Havana, Ek, Khan for ENB, Masilo [probable; to verify]) runs through the whole module as five or six versions. Each version is the hub of a session block and pulls in satellite cases and lenses of its period (court and academy as the prehistory of 1841; the Ballets Russes as the break; Soviet and Cuban state ballet; the contemporary remake). A short court-ballet prologue covers 1581–1700.
- **Why.** It makes the work-as-versions threshold the spine; it lowers context costs (one story, one score) so lenses can go deeper; remaking is exactly the adjacent-translation skill the program builds; routes out appear inside one work.
- **Risks.** Canon re-centring; boredom; reliance on link-only film; the court prologue sits awkwardly; one work may not carry all six angles evenly (money and labour need satellites).
- **Learner's end result.** A **lineage map of one concept across 180 years** (who changed what, why, and who paid), and a **remake brief of their own**: Maker lane, a translation of the work's premise into their world; Briefer lane, a brief that states what must not change (obligations) and what may (permitted variation). Strong candidates for ADJ, OBL and LCY.

### Candidate C. "Studio in acts": backward-designed from the Concept DNA, with ballet cases as fading worked examples (7b + 5 + 3)

- **Shape.** Acts follow the stages of a concept rather than time: *Roots and premise*; *A world with rules*; *Bodies, stages and things*; *Who pays and owns*; *Remaking and travel*. Each act front-loads two fully worked ballet cases, then a partly worked one, then the learner's own problem (guidance fading, per the expertise-reversal effect).
- **Why.** Best motivation for designers; direct two-lane tasks; a clear product.
- **Risks.** Duplicates M1, M2 and M4; chronology lost (needs a timeline reference form beside it); PBL evidence suggests knowledge gains suffer; F1's DNA role is SRC, not RUL.
- **Learner's end result.** A **first-draft Concept DNA** for their own world with each field backed by a ballet precedent. Valuable, but it belongs to the Method level more than to F1. Recommended only if the ballet module is moved or cross-listed to M2.

### Recommendation [argued]

Candidate A, with Candidate B's braid inside it, is the best fit for F1's purpose, its threshold concept, phone-first sittings and the seventh strand. It keeps the six angles (as lenses) and the existing scope research, and adds the works research as the content unit. The current 12-hour thematic plan can remain the **Orientation-depth unit** in F1, and Candidate A becomes the **Practice and Mastery extension** of 30–40 hours, which uses the program's three depths rather than replacing the plan [argued; a decision for the Hub].

### Open questions for the decision

1. Three acts of 10–12 hours or four of 8–10 hours? The completion data favour shorter parts; the content favours three periods.
2. Which braid work: *Giselle* (literary roots, strong global remakes) or *Swan Lake* (Tokyo 1946, Bourne's design-led remake)?
3. Which non-canon cases are documented well enough to be full cases rather than routes? (W3 should answer.)
4. Whether the closing act's product counts as F1's SRC contribution only, or also seeds PRM for M1 (a cross-module decision).
5. No adult ballet-history MOOC was found to test against; a small pilot (one act, timed sittings, completion and a transfer task) would be the first evidence.


## Research log (raw, appended as found)

### Log 1: ballet precedents (exhibitions, books)

- **V&A, *Diaghilev and the Golden Age of the Ballets Russes 1909–1929* (25 Sep 2010 – 9 Jan 2011)** [documented: V&A press release, https://media.vam.ac.uk/media/documents/legacy_documents/press_release/68662_press_release.pdf]. Three galleries. Gallery 1 is chronological-biographical: Diaghilev's St Petersburg, early Paris, the company "up to the outbreak of War in 1914", ending on nine Roerich costumes for *The Rite of Spring*. Gallery 2 breaks the chronology: it "will take visitors behind the scenes … their inspiration, choreography, music and creation of the sets", with Nijinsky's *Faune* notation, the *Pulcinella* score, and *The Firebird* "examined through a series of designs … concluding dramatically with the actual backcloth". Gallery 3 returns to chronology: the 1920s. Over 300 objects. Design by Tim Hatley. **Reading [argued]:** a hybrid: a chronological spine with a process "act" in the middle, where one work is taken from design to cloth. This is the closest exhibition precedent to a "chronology + one work taken apart" structure.
- **NGA Washington, *Diaghilev and the Ballets Russes, 1909–1929: When Art Danced with Music* (12 May – 2 Sep 2013)** [documented: NGA exhibition page https://www.nga.gov/exhibitions/2013/diaghilev.html and brochure https://www.nga.gov/sites/default/files/2025-07/diaghilev-brochure.pdf]. Adapted from the V&A show (some 80 V&A works plus about 50 added); 130+ objects; attendance 227,660. Brochure sections: Introduction; Serge Diaghilev (biography); Transformation of Ballet (five choreographers, one by one); Revolution in Music (composers); When Art Danced with Music (design: "First seasons", then "The international avant-garde"); Legacy. **Reading [argued]:** the same material re-cut by discipline (a person, then dance, music, art), with chronology running inside each section. It shows the trade-off directly: thematic sections repeat the 1909–29 timeline three times (Fokine to Balanchine; Rimsky to Prokofiev; Bakst to Rouault).
- **Homans, *Apollo's Angels* (2010)** [documented: table of contents in Internet Archive record https://archive.org/details/apollosangelshis0000homa]. Introduction "Masters and traditions"; Part 1: France and the classical origins; Kings of dance; The Enlightenment and the story ballet; The French Revolution in ballet; Romantic illusions and the rise of the ballerina; Scandinavian orthodoxy; Italian heresy. Part 2: Light from the East; Tsars of dance; East goes West (Diaghilev); Left behind? (Communist ballet); Alone in Europe (Britain); The American century I and II; Epilogue "The masters are dead and gone". **Reading [argued]:** chronological and national (a "relay" of capitals), which is exactly the "usual line" UNIT_PLAN §2.4 calls the obscured version of the web. Robert Gottlieb's review praises it as "cogently organized" [documented: NYRB, https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2010/12/09/waking-beauty].
- **Kant (ed.), *The Cambridge Companion to Ballet* (2007)** [documented: CUP contents page https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-companion-to-ballet/introduction/C619CA91D0A562DE89027B76424D091A]. Four chronological parts, each with a thematic subtitle that carries an argument: I "From the Renaissance to the baroque: royal power and worldly display"; II "The eighteenth century: revolutions in technique and spirit"; III "Romantic ballet: ballet is a woman"; IV "The twentieth century: tradition becomes modern". Chapters inside are thematic or work-based (e.g. "English masques", "The baroque body", "Balanchine and the deconstruction of classicism", "European ballet in the age of ideologies"); it has an index of ballets. **Reading [argued]:** "periods with a thesis": a chronological spine where each period is named by an idea. Useful pattern for acts.

### Log 2: learning-science evidence (first pass)

- **Chronology and "frameworks" in history teaching.** The UK "Usable Historical Pasts" project (ESRC, Institute of Education) and its writers argue that pupils leave school with fragments, not a usable big picture; Howson (Teaching History 127; 2009 "Potential and pitfalls in teaching 'big pictures' of the past") compares Dawson's and Rogers's approaches [probable: Historical Association abstracts, https://www.history.org.uk/publications/resource/2692/potential-and-pitfalls-in-teaching-big-pictures and https://www.history.org.uk/secondary/resource/2594/change-and-continuity]. Dawson (Teaching History 117) separates chronological order from "a sense of scale", "a sense of period" and "the frameworks of the past"; Barnes (Teaching History 107) proposes an overview that "frames" the whole unit so pupils can "hold different aspects of a period in their minds simultaneously" [probable: HA abstracts, same URL]. Shemilt and Howson, "Frameworks of knowledge: dilemmas and debates", in *Debates in History Teaching* (2010) [documented as a chapter title: ERIC ED532908, https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED532908]. Shemilt (2000), "The caliph's coin: the currency of narrative frameworks in history teaching" [probable: cited in Iowa DoE review, https://educate.iowa.gov/sites/default/files/2022-10/understandingwhatchildrenknowabouthistory.pdf]. **Bearing [argued]:** the history-teaching consensus is not "chronology or themes" but "a framework the learner can hang depth studies on". Depth without an overview leaves fragments; an overview without depth leaves a list.
- **Thematic vs chronological is a live debate, mostly opinion.** A 2020 study of teachers' social-media posts lists claimed advantages of thematic teaching (multiple perspectives, links to the present, "big picture") and of chronological teaching (intelligible, good for chronology, retention) [documented: Journal of Education and Learning 9.1, ERIC EJ1241024, https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1241024.pdf]. It cites Stow and Haydn (2000) and Barton: children's sense of historical time develops "by comparing people, events, and periods with specific reference points", not by sequential exposure [probable: secondary citation in same]. A 2026 Dordt master's thesis notes the research on outcomes is "limited", and that "most thematic teachers still teach chronologically within" themes [documented but weak source: https://digitalcollections.dordt.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1104&context=med_theses]. **Mark: contested.** No robust experimental evidence favours either order for adults; the evidence favours *anchoring reference points* whichever order is used.
- **Interleaving: strongest when learning to tell visual styles apart.** Kornell and Bjork (2008, *Psychological Science* 19: 585–592) showed interleaved paintings by 12 artists beat blocked presentation for learning each artist's style [probable: abstract and many citing papers]. Brunmair and Richter (2019, *Psychological Bulletin* 145(11): 1029–1052), meta-analysis of 59 studies: overall g = 0.42; paintings g = 0.67; maths g = 0.34; expository texts ambiguous; words favoured blocking (g = −0.39); stronger effects when categories are similar to each other and complex [documented: APA abstract, https://psycnet.apa.org/getdoi.cfm?doi=10.1037%2Fbul0000209]. **Bearing [argued]:** for *seeing* (telling a Romantic white act from an Imperial féerie from a Ballets Russes stage), interleave images across works and periods. For *reading arguments* (expository text), the evidence does not support interleaving; block the argument inside a session.
- **Threshold concepts.** Meyer and Land (2003): a threshold concept is "akin to a portal", likely transformative, probably irreversible, integrative, possibly bounded, potentially troublesome [documented: definition quoted in Waterloo handout https://uwaterloo.ca/engineering-teaching-learning/sites/default/files/uploads/documents/handout_1.pdf and in Springer 2026 review https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10734-026-01752-9]. Land et al. (2006) on course design: threshold concepts as "jewels in the curriculum", against the tendency to "stuff" the curriculum [probable: Oakland CETL slides citing Land, Cousin, Meyer and Davies 2006]. The framework is **contested**: critics argue the criteria are vague and the definitions inconsistent ("Threshold concepts, obstacles or scientific dead ends?", *Teaching in Higher Education* 2019) [documented: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13562517.2019.1632828]. "Contestability" is listed as a history threshold concept in practitioner material [probable]. **Bearing [argued]:** useful as a design heuristic (pick three or four portals and build the module to pass through each more than once), not as evidence.
- **Spiral curriculum.** Bruner (1960) described successful teaching of mathematics, physical sciences "and even the field of history" as "a metamorphic spiral": ideas met intuitively, then "revisited and reconstrued in a more formal or operational way" [documented: quoted in Harden 1999, https://lchcautobio.ucsd.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/10/R.M.-Harden-Medical-Teacher-1999.pdf]. Harden and Stamper (1999, *Medical Teacher* 21(2): 141–143) list the features: topics revisited; increasing difficulty; new learning related to previous; competence increases; "not simply the repetition of a topic taught" [documented: same]. Evidence for spirals as a whole design is mostly descriptive; the spacing and retrieval research gives indirect support [argued; a 2020 business-studies paper notes the spiral "has not received comprehensive attention" — documented: https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1258243.pdf].
- **Guidance, problems and worked examples.** Kirschner, Sweller and Clark (2006): minimal guidance is "less effective and less efficient" for novices; the advantage of guidance "begins to recede only when learners have sufficiently high prior knowledge" [documented: ERIC abstract EJ736299, https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ736299]. Hmelo-Silver, Duncan and Chinn (2007) reply that PBL and inquiry are "highly scaffolded", not discovery learning, and effective [documented: ERIC EJ772220, https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ772220]. **Mark: contested,** but both sides agree that novices need worked examples or scaffolds before open problems; the expertise-reversal effect (Kalyuga) says the scaffolds should fade as knowledge grows [probable].
- **Case-based learning.** BEME Guide 23 (Thistlethwaite et al. 2012), systematic review in health professions: CBL is liked by students and teachers; effectiveness "seems to relate to the active learning" and "application of knowledge … to different cases"; evidence of outcome gains is weak and mixed [documented: abstract and text, https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.3109/0142159X.2012.680939]. Kolodner's case-based reasoning model: people learn by interpreting experiences, indexing them, and reusing them; *Learning by Design* used case libraries organised as overview, design challenge, "issues encountered", solution; students "make reference to what they experienced and learned from previous" cases [documented: Kolodner, Cox and González-Calero 2005, https://folk.idi.ntnu.no/agnar/CBR%20papers/KER/15.%20CBR-inspired%20approaches%20to%20education%20(Kolodner,%20Cox,%20&%20Gonzalez-Calero).pdf; Bhat and Kolodner, https://cdn.aaai.org/ocs/907/907-4215-1-PB.pdf]. Evaluation is thin ("There has not been a great deal of evaluation") [documented: same]. **Bearing [argued]:** the case library is a close model for "works as cases" in a design course: each case written to the same template, indexed by the issues the makers met, reused when the learner meets the same issue in their own world.

### Log 3: dose, attention, precedents for format

- **Video length and engagement.** Guo, Kim and Rubin (2014), 6.9 million video sessions on four edX courses: "median engagement time is at most 6 minutes, regardless of total video length"; students "often make it less than halfway through videos longer than 9 minutes"; 0–3 minute videos had the highest engagement; recommendation "segment videos into short chunks, ideally less than 6 minutes"; pre-production planning raised engagement [documented: https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf]. Caveat: engagement is time watched, not learning [argued].
- **Reading rate.** Brysbaert (2019), meta-analysis of 190 studies (18,573 participants): adult silent reading in English averages 238 wpm for non-fiction (most adults 175–300) and 260 for fiction; reading aloud 183 wpm [documented: abstract, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0749596X19300786 and preprint record https://europepmc.org/article/PPR/PPR331394]. **Bearing [argued]:** a 20-minute sitting holds at most about 4,700 words of plain reading at 238 wpm, but study reading (images, captions, notes, a task) runs far slower; a realistic page is 1,000–1,500 words plus 4–6 images and a short task. This matches the program's own page sizes (UNIT_PLAN: "One page (about 20 minutes)"; scope 03: "about 1,200 words, 4–6 images").
- **The "10-minute attention span" is weakly founded.** Bradbury (2016, *Advances in Physiology Education* 40(4): 509–513): the 10–15 minute decline is "common knowledge" resting on "scant few primary investigations"; the most cited source "barely discusses student attention at all" [documented: PubMed abstract https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28145268/]. **Mark: contested.** Twenty-minute sittings are a product constraint (phone, commute), not a cognitive limit; design for interruption, not for a fixed span [argued].
- **Course length and completion.** Jordan (2015), 221 MOOCs: completion 0.7%–52.1%, median 12.6%; completion "vary significantly according to course length (longer courses having lower completion rates)"; "the first and second weeks appear to be critical", after which active users level out [documented: IRRODL 16(3), https://www.irrodl.org//index.php/irrodl/article/view/2112]. Reich and Ruipérez-Valiente (2019, *Science* 363: 130–131): on edX, 52% of registrants never enter the courseware; completion among learners who said they intended to complete was about 11–18% (2013–18), and among paying "verified" learners about 44–56% [documented: post-print supplementary Table S3, https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/136215/post_print-MOOC_Pivot.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=2]. Coursera courses typically "last approximately four to twelve weeks, with one to two hours of video lectures a week" [probable: Wikipedia, https://en.m.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coursera]. **Bearing [argued]:** 30–40 hours is long by MOOC standards (a typical MOOC is 10–30 hours). Attrition is front-loaded, so the first two sessions decide the module; and a long module should be built as **shorter, completable parts** (acts of 8–12 hours) each with its own end product.
- **Precedent: *A History of the World in 100 Objects*** (BBC Radio 4 / British Museum, 2010): 100 programmes of 15 minutes, weekdays over 20 weeks; each week five objects under a dated period heading (e.g. "The World of Our Making (1914–2010 AD)", "Mass Production, Mass Persuasion (1780–1914 AD)") [documented: BBC programme pages https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b00nrtd2/episodes/player and https://www.bbc.co.uk/programmes/b00vcqcz; Wikipedia https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_History_of_the_World_in_100_Objects]. It won the 2011 Art Fund Prize [probable: Wikipedia]. **Reading [argued]:** the strongest precedent for *object-based + chronological + 15-minute sittings*. Each object is read outward to its world; the weekly period heading carries chronology; the week (five sittings) is the act. (A web page claiming the series is "not chronological" and in "ten thematic groups" contradicts the BBC's own episode list and is disregarded.)
- **Precedent: Royal Ballet School, *Ballet History Timeline*:** "an easy-to-explore linear chronology", nearly 750 images and about 67,000 words, 1860s–1956, desktop only "due to the extent of the materials" [documented: https://www.royalballetschool.org.uk/discover/history/ballet-history-timeline]. **Reading [argued]:** a reference spine, not a course; and a warning that a dense chronology does not fit a phone.
- **Precedent: company resources are organised by production.** ENB's page for Akram Khan's *Giselle* gathers trailer, "The character of Giselle", "Ballet meets kathak", extracts, "Explore the story", and full credits (Tim Yip designs; Ruth Little dramaturgy; Mark Henderson lighting) [documented: https://www.ballet.org.uk/production/akram-khan-giselle]. Royal Ballet and Opera Schools organise by programme (Sing, Dance, Design) and production assets (e.g. "Images from The Nutcracker"), plus a KS5 Design Challenge [documented: https://www.rbo.org.uk/schools and https://www.rbo.org.uk/schools/resource/images-from-the-nutcracker]. **Reading [argued]:** the industry's own unit of explanation is the work (production), which supports a case-by-work structure for the seventh strand.
- **Precedent: museum collection hangs.** Tate (Britain, 2000) "for the first time in its century-old history … opted for a full thematic display, abandoning the usual chronological approach" [documented: The Art Newspaper, https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2000/03/01/tate-britains-new-thematic-display-revealed-in-representing-britain-as-international-works-move-to-bankside]. Tate Modern's 2006 rehang replaced its thematic suites with four "hubs" each built on "major historic moments" (e.g. "States of Flux: Cubism, Futurism, Vorticism"); Kathy Halbreich: "using chronology to shape the display can be both accessible and fascinating"; Max Hollein suggests rooms that "break the chronology" then return [documented: Tate Etc. 7 (2006), https://www.tate.org.uk/tate-etc/issue-7-summer-2006/private-pleasure-public-good]. **Reading [argued]:** the museum world tried pure thematic, and moved to a chronological frame with thematic rooms that break it: the hybrid.
- **Precedent: design-history MA.** V&A/RCA History of Design MA (founded 1982): term 1 "Artefacts in History" (30 credits; "artefact analysis, exploring and analysing different object types using core theoretical approaches and archival/primary resources") with "History as Public Practice"; term 2 "Designing Research" and a specialist option; "Working from objects as historical evidence is a core part of our programme"; range "from 1400 to the contemporary" and "global geographies" [documented: https://www.rca.ac.uk/study/programme-finder/history-of-design-ma; https://2023.rca.ac.uk/programmes/history-of-design-ma?location=battersea-kensington]. **Reading [argued]:** method first (how to read an object), then depth by choice; chronology is the range, not the order of teaching. Object-based learning as an HE pedagogy: Chatterjee and Hannan (eds), *Engaging the Senses* (Routledge 2015) [documented: TOC, https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/edit/10.4324/9781315579641/engaging-senses-object-based-learning-higher-education-leonie-hannan-helen-chatterjee]; its evidence is mainly case reports [probable].
- **Precedent: world-building and concept courses are built as a production pipeline.** CalArts' Coursera specialisation *Game Design: Art and Concepts*: Introduction; Story and Narrative; World Design; Character Design; Game Design Document (capstone); "World Design" has learners "explore game worlds in existing games and study the art and influences that inform their themes and styles" with "a weekly challenge" [documented: Class Central syllabus https://www.classcentral.com/course/game-design-18863; CalArts release https://calarts.edu/news/calarts-launches-open-online-game-design-art-and-concepts-courses-coursera]. Its Character Design course uses three weeks as "master class" observing three professionals [documented: same]. USC World Building Media Lab (Alex McDowell): "the design of a well-researched, richly-detailed world precedes the emergence of story" [documented: http://worldbuilding.usc.edu/]. **Reading [argued]:** concept courses order by the stages of the learner's own artifact (premise, world, character, document) and use existing works as worked examples inside each stage. That is the problem/studio structure with cases as evidence.

### Log 4: evidence, second pass

- **Narrative beats exposition for memory.** Mar, Li, Nguyen and Ta (2021, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 28: 732–749), meta-analysis of 150 effect sizes, over 75 samples, more than 33,000 participants: "stories were more easily understood and better recalled than essays"; the estimate stayed at g ≈ .54–.57 when any one effect was removed; the authors also report evidence of publication bias [documented: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8219577]. A 2026 paper summarising it: the gap was "about half a standard deviation" and "greater in children and adolescents than in adults" [documented: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12892345]. **Bearing [argued]:** the life of one ballet (idea → team → premiere → afterlife) is a narrative; a thematic page on "patronage" is exposition. Both chronological and case structures get this advantage; the angle structure has to work harder for it.
- **Worked examples help novices even in ill-defined design tasks.** Rourke and Sweller (2009, *Learning and Instruction* 19: 185–199), two experiments with undergraduates learning to recognise designers’ styles (131 participants per a ResearchGate summary): novices with "a moderate level of visual literacy skills are more successful at identifying a designer's work after studying worked examples" than after problem-solving tasks [documented: abstract, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S095947520800039X]. Van Gog, Kester and Paas (2011) cite it as evidence the effect holds "with less structured tasks" [documented: https://research.ou.nl/files/1026339/Van%20Gog_%20Kester%20_%20Paas%20-%20CEDPSYCH%202010.pdf]. **Bearing [argued]:** a ballet taken apart from idea to afterlife is a worked example of the learner's later task (a concept with roots made into a world). Show several worked examples before asking for the learner's own.
- **PBL: better skills, not better knowledge.** Dochy, Segers, Van den Bossche and Gijbels (2003, *Learning and Instruction* 13: 533–568), meta-analysis of 43 articles: "a robust positive effect on skills, but a tendency to a negative effect … on student knowledge" [documented: ERIC EJ678509, https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ678509]. Architecture education reports a persistent gap between history lectures and the design studio [probable: ICCAUA 2024 chapter, https://www.iccaua.com/PDFs/ICCAUA2024/Book_Chapter_Architecture2024/N5ICCAUA2024IN0399_Manoj_Panwar_&_Shalini_Sheoran.pdf; CMU "the studio is king", http://code.arc.cmu.edu/archive/dmgftp/public_html/publications/pdfs/edutech97-eyd.pdf].
- **Backward design.** Understanding by Design: curriculum "planned backward from long-term, desired results through a three-stage design process (Desired Results, Evidence, and Learning Plan)" [documented: ASCD white paper, https://files.ascd.org/staticfiles/ascd/pdf/siteASCD/publications/UbD_WhitePaper0312.pdf]. Evidence for UbD itself is mostly practitioner reports [probable]. **Bearing [argued]:** decide first what the learner's end product is (the comparison table in §5 does this per structure), then the evidence, then the order.
- **Segmenting and spacing.** Mayer's segmenting principle: "people learn more deeply when a multimedia message is presented in learner-paced segments rather than as a continuous unit" [documented: APA record of Mayer 2005, https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2006-00633-011]. Cepeda et al. (2006, *Psychological Bulletin* 132: 354–380), 839 assessments in 317 experiments: spacing benefits retention, and the best gap grows with the retention interval [documented: ERIC EJ742345, https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ742345]. **Bearing [argued]:** short sittings suit segmenting; returning to a work or theme across weeks (a braid or spiral) uses spacing.
- **The program's own constraints, read in the repository** [documented]. F1's threshold concept: "Design history is a web with obscured strands, not a line" (`modules/F1-histories-of-making/README.md`). F1 units are tested at "45–55 minutes" per unit (`docs/04-deep-dives-1/index.md`), with a phone version of about ten minutes (`docs/02-pass-2-v2/index.md`, F1.13 scenario). Every unit has three consumption forms: five-minute, session, reference (`docs/02-pass-2-v2/index.md`; `schema/unit-spec-template.md`). In the Concept DNA, F1 fills mainly **SRC** (sources, attributions, consultation record); PRM is M1's, RUL/ANT/OBL/GRM are M2's, ADJ is M4's, RGT is R2's, LCY is R4's (`schema/concept-dna.schema.json`). F1's Edo research found the strongest units show "the same design shown in multiple impressions" so that "'the' print is seen as an edition with states" (`research/edo-2026-10-04/teaching.md`), the direct analogue of "the" ballet as a set of versions.
