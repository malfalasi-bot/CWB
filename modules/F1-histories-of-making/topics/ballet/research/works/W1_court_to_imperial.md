## 1. *Balet comique de la Royne* (Paris, 1581)

Scope 01 and 04 already give the date, venue dispute and Patin's credit. This entry adds the concept, its making and its documents.

**1. Premise.**
- *As framed by its makers.* The enchantress Circe, who turns men into beasts, holds the world in disorder; the gods and the virtues defeat her, and she is led captive to the King of France, whose rule restores harmony. Beaujoyeulx frames the novelty as a form, not a story: in his preface "Au lecteur" he says "i'ay animé & fait parler le Balet" and made "chanter & resonner la Comedie", mixing the two [documented: 1582 livret, archive.org OCR]. He calls ballet itself "des meslanges geometriques de plusieurs personnes dansans ensemble" [documented, same; modernised spelling]. The title "comique" means "with a dramatic plot" (comédie in the sense of a play), not "funny" [probable: standard reading, McGowan].
- *As scholars read it.* Frances Yates reads it as a magical-political rite of the Valois court: harmony of music and measured verse (the programme of Baïf's Académie de Poésie et de Musique) meant to act on the audience and heal a France split by the Wars of Religion; Circe is civil discord [probable: Yates 1947, ch. XI, via Wikipedia's summary]. Mark Franko reads court ballet more generally as a "body politic" display whose geometry made power legible [probable: Franko, *Dance as Text*, 1993/2015].

**2. Roots.**
- Literary: Homer's Circe (*Odyssey* X), via the Renaissance allegorical tradition in which Circe stands for the passions or sensual disorder (Natale Conti's *Mythologiae*, 1567, is the commonly cited mythographic source) [probable; the Conti link is argued in secondary literature, not confirmed at source in this pass].
- Musical and poetic: Baïf's Académie (chartered 1570) and *musique mesurée à l'antique*, which tried to revive the union of Greek verse, music and movement [probable: Yates; Wikipedia states it was the earliest work to combine the arts under the Académie's rules].
- Performance: Italian *intermedi* and the Valois *magnificences* that Catherine de' Medici had staged since the 1560s (Fontainebleau 1564, Bayonne 1565, the 1573 *Ballet des Polonais*) [probable: McGowan 1963; Lee 2002]. Beaujoyeulx himself was Piedmontese (Baldassare da Belgioioso), brought to the French court as a violinist in 1555 [probable: Britannica].
- Political: the wedding of Anne, duc de Joyeuse (Henri III's favourite) to Marguerite de Vaudémont, sister of Queen Louise; the *magnificences* for this wedding ran for about two weeks [probable].

**3. Who had it and how it was developed.**
- The title names the patron as originator: it is the *Queen's* ballet (Louise de Lorraine), and the livret's narrative says the Queen commanded the parts: La Chesnaye was ordered to "faire les poesies", the "sieur de Beaulieu" the music with "maistre Salmon" among the king's musicians, and "maistre Iacques Patin" the painting "par commandement de la Royne" [documented: 1582 livret OCR].
- The plot idea is contested. The Protestant poet Agrippa d'Aubigné later claimed that a Circe entertainment he had devised years earlier was the source [contested: the claim rests on d'Aubigné's own memoir *Sa vie à ses enfants*; IMSLP lists "Story: Agrippa d'Aubigny" (documented at IMSLP); scholars treat it as an unverifiable self-attribution (probable)]. Teaching point: the "idea person" is often a claimant, not a credit.
- Beaujoyeulx is the deviser and *ordonnateur* who unified the parts, and he signed the book: "PAR BALTASAR DE BEAVIOYEVLX, VALET DE CHAMBRE DV ROY" [documented: title page].
- The development documents are the livret itself (1582), printed by the king's printers Le Roy, Ballard and Patisson "avec privilege", with the dedication "Au Roy" saying the book exists to communicate the event "à ceux qui ne l'ont point veu" [documented]. No rehearsal records or letters on the making were found in this pass.

**4. The world it built.**
- Setting: the hall itself became the world. Circe's garden and castle at one end; Pan's grove (bois) to one side; a gilded "voûte" of clouds for musicians to the other; the King on a dais at the opposite end, so that the action played *towards* the monarch [probable: the livret's engraved plan; Wikipedia notes dancers entered from both sides, unusual for its time].
- Vocabulary: spoken verse, sung airs and *récits*, instrumental "concerts", and the closing *grand ballet* of geometric figures danced by the Queen and princesses [documented: livret structure; the count of figures "15 passages... 40 passages" is often cited, probable].
- Machines: a three-tier fountain carrying the Queen and her ladies as naiads; chariots for Minerva and Glaucus; Jupiter descending [probable: livret engravings].
- Rules (implicit): every character points to the King; aristocrats dance, professionals sing and play; the plot must end in the restoration of order. Anti-DNA: no improvised masquerade, no fragmented "entrées" without a thread (the thing Beaujoyeulx says he went beyond) [argued].

**5. The team and the credit.**
| Role | Name | Credit status |
|---|---|---|
| Patron, performer | Queen Louise de Lorraine; Catherine de' Medici as Queen Mother | In title ("de la Royne") [documented] |
| Deviser, choreographer, producer | Balthasar de Beaujoyeulx | Author on title page [documented] |
| Verses | Nicolas Filleul de La Chesnaye, royal almoner | Named in the narrative, not on the title page [documented] |
| Music | "Sieur de Beaulieu" (now usually Girard de Beaulieu, bass singer; earlier misidentified as Lambert de Beaulieu by Fétis) with Jacques Salmon | Named in narrative [documented]; identification [probable, Wikipedia citing modern scholarship] |
| Scenery, costumes | Jacques Patin, painter | Named in narrative [documented] |
| Singers | Girard de Beaulieu and his wife Violante Doria among others [probable] |
| Printers | Adrian Le Roy, Robert Ballard, Mamert Patisson [documented] |
| Dropped | carpenters, machinists, tailors, the musicians beyond those named |

**6. Production.** 15 October 1581, Paris, the great hall of the Hôtel de Bourbon (Petit-Bourbon) beside the Louvre [probable; venue contested, see scope 04]. About five and a half hours, from 10 p.m. to about 3:30 a.m. [probable]. Cost: Britannica gives "3,600,000 gold francs" [documented at Britannica]; the figure appears without primary support and should be taught as a contemporary hearsay figure [argued]. Audience: the court and invited ambassadors; estimates of up to 10,000 are repeated in secondary sources [probable, unverified].

**7. Reception and afterlife.** Performed once. Its afterlife is the book: the printed livret made a one-night event into a model for court ballet across Europe [probable: Britannica calls it the earliest ballet with "a complete printed account"]. Modern facsimile: McGowan ed., *Le Balet comique* (MRTS, Binghamton 1982) [probable]. Modern performances of the music are rare. Adjacent translation, unexpected: the closing bell tune was re-arranged by Henri Ghys in the nineteenth century (misattributed to Louis XIII as "Amaryllis"), sung in Japan on NHK's *Minna no Uta* (1968), and is now a school chime and a Zojirushi rice-cooker signal [probable: Wikipedia]. The "first ballet" label itself is a trap (scope 01).

**8. Primary documents and open images.**

- Internet Archive copy "baletcomiqvedela00beau", digitised 2014, full OCR [documented]: https://archive.org/stream/baletcomiqvedela00beau/baletcomiqvedela00beau_djvu.txt
- Modern typeset score of selections by Marc Lanoiselée, CC BY 4.0 (WIMA, on IMSLP) [documented].
- Engravings from the livret are on Wikimedia Commons (category "Ballet comique de la reine") as PD reproductions [probable; check each file].

**9. Best sources.**
- Margaret M. McGowan, *L'Art du ballet de cour en France, 1581–1643* (CNRS, 1963) [probable].
- McGowan (ed.), *Le Balet comique by Balthazar de Beaujoyeulx, 1581: a facsimile* (MRTS, 1982) [probable].
- Frances A. Yates, *The French Academies of the Sixteenth Century* (Warburg Institute, 1947), ch. XI [probable].

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].**
- *Premise:* disorder (Circe) undone by royal harmony.
- *Perimeter:* one hall, one night; the world ends at the King's dais.
- *Rules:* everything faces the monarch; verse, song and dance must be "mixed" into one thread.
- *Anti-DNA:* the plotless masquerade.
- *Anchor expression:* the final geometric *grand ballet*.
- *Handover/lifecycle:* the printed livret is the handover document; the work survives as a book, not a repertory.
- *Rights and money:* royal privilege on the book; the cost was borne by the Crown.
- *Sources:* Homer through Renaissance mythography; Baïf's Académie.

## 2. *Ballet royal de la Nuit* (Paris, 1653)

**1. Premise.**
- *As framed by its makers.* One night in twelve hours, divided into four "veilles" (watches) from 6 p.m. to 6 a.m.; every creature of the night passes, from thieves and beggars to witches and gods, until the Sun rises in the person of the fourteen-year-old Louis XIV (born 5 September 1638; corrected 25 Oct 2026 after review R2) [documented: Wikipedia summary of the livret "Divisé en quatre Parties ou quatre Veilles"; probable for the hours].
- *As scholars and remakers read it.* A political allegory after the Fronde: the darkness of revolt dispelled by the young king. Sébastien Daucé calls it "le sacre profane", a secular coronation [documented: Caen press dossier, 2020]. Burden and Thorp (2009) read it through the court's organisation of spectacle and Mazarin's patronage [probable].

**2. Roots.**
- The *ballet à entrées* of Louis XIII's court (loosely linked burlesque entrées) and the Italian opera that Mazarin imported (Luigi Rossi's *Orfeo*, Paris 1647) [probable]. Torelli's Venetian machinery, brought to Paris c. 1645 (scope 04).
- Iconography of night: the Ripa-style personifications (Night, Moon, Venus, Hours), the "Cour des Miracles" of Paris beggars, the witches' Sabbath; the ballet mixes the mythological and the low-life "realistic" entrée [probable: Wikipedia lists shepherds, gypsies, thieves, lamplighters, beggars].
- Political: the Fronde (1648–53); Mazarin had returned to Paris weeks earlier [documented: Caen dossier says Mazarin returned with the king after the Fronde des Princes].

**3. Who had it and how it was developed.**
- Patron and commissioner: Cardinal Mazarin "commissioned and supervised" it as a tool to assert royal authority [documented as the reconstruction's account, Caen dossier; probable as history].
- Organiser: contested. The Caen dossier names a "Sieur Clément" as director of the project [documented there]. Waddesdon's catalogue calls Louis Hesselin (1602–1662), *surintendant des plaisirs du roi*, "the likely original owner and the ballet's organiser", whose arms are on the presentation copy, and who danced as Master of the Court of Miracles and as Jupiter [documented: Waddesdon]. [contested: both may be right, Hesselin as intendant and Clément as practical director.]
- Text: Isaac de Benserade wrote the *vers* "pour les personnages": short poems in the livret that speak both for the role and about the courtier dancing it, a double address that is the genre's signature [probable: standard reading].
- Documents of development: the printed livret (Ballard, 1653); Gissey's costume drawings (two large sets survive); Philidor's late-seventeenth-century copy of the dance music (first violin only); Cambefort's book of airs (1655) [documented: Caen dossier for Philidor and Cambefort; Waddesdon and Wikipedia for drawings].

**4. The world it built.**
- Structure: four veilles, about 43–45 entrées, three inset "ballets within the ballet", ending in a *grand ballet* at sunrise [documented: Wikipedia gives 45 entrées; Caen dossier names the veilles' themes as Night, Venus, Hercules in love/the Moon, Orpheus].
- Rule of descent and ascent: low characters (beggars, thieves, cripples) early; supernatural terrors in the dark hours; gods at the end; the King last and highest [argued].
- Costume language: Gissey's designs make each role readable at a glance, with emblems sewn into the costume (eyes, stars, flames); the Sun costume is gold with rays, the head crowned with plumes and a sun mask [probable: Gissey drawings; the Rising Sun drawing at BnF is the most reproduced].
- Anti-DNA: no continuous plot; no professional star outranking the King; no darkness left undispelled [argued].

**5. The team and the credit.**
| Role | Name | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Patron | Cardinal Mazarin (for Anne of Austria and Louis XIV) | [documented in reconstruction dossier; probable] |
| Organiser | Louis Hesselin, or "Sieur Clément" | [contested] |
| Verses | Isaac de Benserade | [documented] |
| Music | Jean de Cambefort (airs), Jean-Baptiste (or Antoine) Boësset, Michel Lambert; dance music by unknown violinists, possibly Mollier, Constantin, Lazarin, Vertpré, Mazuel | [documented: Caen dossier lists possible names] |
| Costume design | Henri Gissey, *dessinateur ordinaire du roi* | [probable: attribution moved from Stefano Della Bella to Gissey, documented at Waddesdon] |
| Machines | Giacomo Torelli | [documented: Caen dossier] |
| Dancers | Louis XIV; courtiers; professional dancers; the young Lully danced in it | [probable] |
| Dropped | the instrumentalists, tailors, the composers of the dance tunes (Philidor's copy gives none) |

**6. Production.** Premiere 23 February 1653, Salle du Petit-Bourbon; seven evenings in early 1653 [documented: Caen dossier]. About thirteen hours of spectacle [probable: Wikipedia]. No budget found. Audience: the court, Paris elites, ambassadors; the livret was sold or distributed so spectators could identify dancers [probable].

**7. Reception and afterlife.** The image outlived the work: Louis as the Sun became the reign's emblem [probable]. Modern edition: Burden and Thorp, *Ballet de la Nuit: Rothschild B1/16/6* (Pendragon, 2009), with Lionel Sawkins's edition of the surviving music, described there as the earliest complete publication of a ballet de cour (a priority claim to check) [documented: Wikipedia]. Reconstruction: Ensemble Correspondances (Daucé, music, from 2012 work) with Francesca Lattuada (staging, choreography, design), Caen and tour October to December 2020; Daucé filled blank staves, and *added* Rossi's *Orfeo* (1647) and Cavalli's *Ercole amante* (1662) [documented: Caen dossier]. So the "reconstruction" is a new concept built around the old one. Film: the finale appears in Gérard Corbiau's *Le Roi danse* (2000) [probable].

**8. Primary documents and open images.**
- Livret, BnF RES F-501 (Philidor compilation, 1690), on Gallica since 11 Nov 2018 [documented via Wikipedia]; Gallica NC terms.
- Gissey drawings: Waddesdon Manor (National Trust), Rothschild B1/16/6, accession 3666.1-3, with the 1653 livret; 129 designs per the catalogue; provenance Hesselin → Pichon → Rothschild; "All images subject to copyright" [documented]: https://waddesdon.org.uk/the-collection/item/?id=3784
- Bibliothèque de l'Institut, MS 1004: 119 costume designs (Papillon de la Ferté copy) [documented via Wikipedia].
- The Rising Sun costume drawing (BnF, Estampes) is widely reproduced as PD on Wikimedia Commons [probable; check BnF terms].
- Caen 2020 programme and press dossier (PDF) [documented]: https://theatre.caen.fr/sites/theatre/files/2020-09/Le%20Ballet%20royal%20de%20la%20nuit%20-%20%28BD%29%20dossier%20de%20presse%20fr.pdf

**9. Best sources.**
- Michael Burden and Jennifer Thorp, *Ballet de la Nuit: Rothschild B1/16/6* (Pendragon, 2009; rev. 2010), essays by Burden, David Parrott, Thorp, Catherine Massip [probable].
- Margaret McGowan, *L'Art du ballet de cour* (1963) and *The Court Ballet of Louis XIII* (V&A, 1986) [probable].
- Per Bjurström, *Giacomo Torelli and Baroque Stage Design* (1962) [probable].
- Mark Franko, *Dance as Text* (1993/2015) [probable].
- Georgia Cowart, *The Triumph of Pleasure: Louis XIV and the Politics of Spectacle* (Chicago, 2008) [probable].

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].**
- *Premise:* night as disorder, dawn as kingship.
- *Perimeter:* twelve hours of one night.
- *Grammar:* the four watches as a time-based structure, each with its own palette and population.
- *Anchor expression:* the Rising Sun costume, which became a logo for the reign (an early case of a stage costume turned brand).
- *Collection map:* 43–45 entrées as a catalogue of night types.
- *Lifecycle:* one season in 1653; remade from 2012 (music) to 2020 (staging) as a reconstruction that is partly pastiche.

## 3. Noverre: *Médée et Jason* (Stuttgart, 1763) and the *Lettres sur la danse* (1760)

This is the one case in this file where a **theory of the concept** was written and published before the work it was used to make. The *Lettres* are the manifesto; *Médée et Jason* is the test.

**1. Premise.**
- *The theory.* Noverre opens Letter I: "Un Ballet est un tableau, la Scène est la toile" [documented: 1760 Lyon edition, BnF scan via libraryofdance.org, OCR]. The ballet should be a wordless drama, "le Ballet Pantomime doit toujours être en action" (Letter III) [documented]. He wants faces seen: "quittez ces masques froids, copies imparfaites de la nature" (Letter IV) [documented].
- *The work.* Medea, abandoned by Jason for Créuse, daughter of King Créon of Corinth, summons infernal spirits who give her Fire, Poison and Iron; Créon dies in flames, Créuse in a poisoned robe, Medea kills her children before Jason and escapes through the air as the palace falls [documented: plot as summarised in Dartois-Lapeyre's review of the 2012 reconstruction].
- *As scholars read it.* Iris Julia Bührle reads it as an application of the *Lettres*, comparing Noverre's programme with his main source, Corneille's *Médée* (1635), to show how a text of "powerful discourse" was turned into wordless ballet-pantomime [documented: abstract, New College Oxford page]. Pappacena argues that the split Noverre draws between "dance" and "pantomime" is overstated; his programmes and stagings integrate them [documented: Pappacena essay, Acting Archives].

**2. Roots.**
- Literary: Euripides' *Medea*; Corneille's *Médée* as the main literary source [documented: Bührle abstract]; the tragic-opera tradition of Medea (Charpentier's *Médée*, 1693) [probable].
- Theatrical: David Garrick's naturalistic acting, which Noverre saw in London in the 1750s; Diderot's theory of the *tableau*; Cahusac's *La Danse ancienne et moderne* (1754) and Batteux's imitation theory [documented as Pappacena's argument; probable as history].
- Painting: the *Lettres* repeatedly model the ballet on history painting, "Réunissons le génie du Poëte & le génie du Peintre" [documented, OCR].
- Earlier works it answers: the opera-ballet *divertissement*, with symmetrical figures and masked dancers, which Noverre attacks [documented: Letters II–IV]; John Weaver's *The Loves of Mars and Venus* (London, 1717) and Hilverding and Angiolini in Vienna were parallel reformers; who was earliest is a long priority dispute [contested: Angiolini attacked Noverre's claim in print, 1773; teach as a priority quarrel].

**3. Who had it and how it was developed.**
- Noverre is originator, choreographer and theorist at once. The *Lettres* were printed at Lyon by Aimé Delaroche, 1760, with a royal privilege dated Versailles, 21 December 1759, and the title page already styles him "Maître des Ballets de Son Altesse Sérénissime Monseigneur le Duc de Wurtemberg" [documented]. So the book was a job application that worked.
- Patron: Duke Carl Eugen of Württemberg, who gave Noverre "unprecedented means and support" [documented as Bührle's claim].
- Development documents: the printed *programme* (Noverre's word) distributed to spectators. The *Lettres* already discuss the problem: a ballet whose plot one can guess only "un Programe à la main" (Letter II) [documented]. Pappacena argues that his programmes are not transcriptions but autonomous prose texts beside the performance [documented: Pappacena]. The later St Petersburg edition of the *Lettres* (1803–04) added a volume of programmes [documented: Pappacena]. The Warsaw University Library holds a manuscript set of Noverre's ballet programmes with costume designs by Louis-René Boquet, sent to King Stanisław August [probable; not checked at source in this pass].
- The *Lettres* also specify the ballet master's knowledge: history, fable and ancient poems (Letter V), "une teinture de Géométrie", anatomy, and music, since a ballet master who "ignorera la musique, phrasera mal les airs" [documented, OCR]. This is the earliest known job description of the choreographer as concept-holder [argued].

**4. The world it built.**
- Rules from the *Lettres*: action, not display; faces unmasked; costume made truthful to character rather than to the conventional *tonnelet* and panniers; groups composed like history paintings, asymmetrical; music written to the action [documented in the letters as programme; how far Stuttgart practice met it is probable only].
- Vocabulary: *pas d'action* and expressive mime set into a five-act-like arc; allegorical figures (Fire, Poison, Iron, Spirit of Evil) as embodied props [probable].
- Anti-DNA: the symmetrical *figure*, the mask, the virtuoso *entrée* that stops the story.
- Palette: the infernal scene in fire-light against the cool court of Corinth [argued from the plot; no Stuttgart design is confirmed here].

**5. The team and the credit.**

**6. Production.** 11 February 1763, Stuttgart Hoftheater, for the Duke's birthday, between the acts of Jommelli's opera [documented: BSECS review]. The Stuttgart ballet establishment under Carl Eugen was large and costly; figures were not found in this pass.

**7. Reception and afterlife.** The work travelled under other people's hands: Vestris inserted it into La Borde's *Ismène et Isménias* before Louis XV's court in June 1763; it was mounted at Fontainebleau 1769, at the Paris Opéra 1770, and last given there in 1804 [documented: BSECS review]. London, King's Theatre, 29 and 31 March 1781 "By Command of Their Majesties": Medea, Mme Simonet; Jason, Vestris Sen.; Creusa, Mlle Baccelli; a young Prince, Vestris Jun.; Creon, Simonet; the London Stage Database bill gives no choreographer [documented]. The question of whose ballet it was in Paris and London (Noverre's or Vestris's) is the work's authorship problem [probable; contested in the literature]. No notation survives; Marie-Geneviève Massé's reconstruction (Compagnie L'Éventail, with Hervé Niquet, Opéra-Comique, December 2012) was built from programmes, theory, iconography and music, with music added by Berton, Dauvergne, Grétry and Vogel [documented: BSECS review]. The *Lettres* had the bigger afterlife: five editions to 1807, the last retitled *Lettres sur les arts imitateurs* [documented: Pappacena]; Noverre's birthday, 29 April, is International Dance Day (UNESCO/ITI, from 1982) [probable].

The text is PD; scan terms follow Gallica (NC).

**9. Best sources.**

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].**
- *Premise:* a tragedy told with bodies alone.
- *Rules:* the Letters' list (action, unmasking, painterly grouping, truthful costume).
- *Anti-DNA:* the mask and the symmetrical figure, stated outright.
- *Grammars:* the *Lettres* are a written grammar, the earliest the course can show learners as a stand-alone "rules" document.
- *Handover:* the programme for spectators; the *Lettres* for future makers.
- *Lifecycle:* the work dies, the grammar survives; remade only by reconstruction (2012).
- *Obligations:* the choreographer's required knowledge list.

## 4. *La Sylphide* (Paris Opéra, 1832)

Scope 01 and 04 cover the date, the Véron concession, Ciceri and the *ballet blanc*. This entry covers the idea and its passage from a novella to a ballet.

**1. Premise.**
- *As framed.* A young Scottish farmer, James, on the eve of his wedding to Effie, is drawn away by a sylph, an air spirit only he can see; he follows her into the forest, and the witch Madge gives him a scarf to bind her; the scarf makes her wings fall and she dies, while Effie marries his rival [probable: standard synopsis of the 1832 livret].
- *As scholars read it.* The founding Romantic split between a daylight world of farm, wedding and reason and a moonlit realm of desire and the ideal [probable: EBSCO summary of Garafola, Guest]. Feminist readings take the sylph as the unattainable woman constructed by male desire, the ballerina as the "ballet is a woman" moment (scope 01, Kant) [probable]. Garafola (ed., 1997) reads the Romantic ballet's sexual and economic politics around the female star [probable].

**2. Roots.**
- Literary: Charles Nodier, *Trilby, ou le Lutin d'Argail* (1822). Nodier's Trilby is a *male* household sprite who loves Jeanie, a fisherman's wife; a monk exorcises him. Nodier's preface credits his 1821 Highland journey and says the subject came from a note or preface to one of Walter Scott's novels [documented: Wikipedia article on *Trilby*]. The ballet swaps the genders: female sylph, male mortal [documented: same; and *La Sylphide* page].
- The Scott craze: tartan, Highlands and the *Waverley* novels in Paris in the 1820s, plus Nodier's travel book *Promenade de Dieppe aux montagnes d'Écosse* (1821) [documented].
- Theatrical: the Ballet of the Nuns in Meyerbeer's *Robert le diable* (Opéra, 21 Nov 1831), danced by Marie Taglioni and staged by her father, with Ciceri's gas-lit cloister; Nourrit sang Robert in it [documented: Wikipedia; scope 04]. The witch scene draws on Paganini's *Le Streghe*, itself after Viganò and Süssmayr's ballet *Il noce di Benevento* (1812) [documented as Wikipedia's claim; probable].
- Earlier sylph and air-spirit ballets: Didelot's *Zéphire et Flore* (London 1796) introduced flying wires for dancers [probable].

**3. Who had it and how it was developed.**
- **The originator was a singer.** Adolphe Nourrit, the Opéra's leading tenor, wrote the scenario [documented: Wikipedia, EBSCO]. His name did not appear on the livret, which was published anonymously in the custom of the day [probable]. Teaching point: the concept came from the opera side of the house.
- Filippo Taglioni choreographed it as a vehicle for his daughter Marie [documented: Wikipedia].
- Sequence (reconstructed): the success of the Nuns (Nov 1831) proves the white supernatural act and Marie's power; Nourrit, who shared the stage, writes a scenario on Nodier's tale; Véron, running the Opéra as a business from 1831, approves a new ballet for his star; premiere four months after *Robert* [argued from dates; probable].
- Development documents: the printed livret (1832); Schneitzhoeffer's score (BnF); Lami's and Ciceri's designs; *Marian Smith (ed.), La Sylphide: Paris 1832 and Beyond* (2012) reconstructs the production from these [probable].

**4. The world it built.**
- Two-act grammar: Act I a Scottish farmhouse interior with a window and hearth (real, warm, populated); Act II a misty forest glade (unreal, cool, female) [probable].
- Movement vocabulary: Taglioni's light elevation and *pointe* held as a sign of weightlessness, the earliest well-known use of pointe for expressive rather than acrobatic purpose [probable: Wikipedia, cautioned with "earliest known"]. Flying sylphs on wires; the sylph slipping up the chimney [probable].
- Costume: a white, bell-shaped, calf-length muslin skirt over a fitted bodice, usually credited to Eugène Lami working with Ciceri; this is the ancestor of the Romantic tutu [probable; attribution contested, scope 04].
- Light: gas, bluish, with gauzes [documented: EBSCO; scope 04].
- Music: Schneitzhoeffer's score, with quotations of familiar airs to help the audience follow the mime (the *air parlant* practice studied by Marian Smith) [probable].
- Anti-DNA: no mythological gods, no court, no happy ending; the supernatural is not allegory but desire [argued].

**6. Production.** 12 March 1832, Paris Opéra, Salle Le Peletier [documented]. Made for the Opéra's subscribers and the July Monarchy's new bourgeois public under a commercial concession [probable: scope 01]. No budget found in this pass.

**7. Reception and afterlife.**
- The Taglioni version was lost. August Bournonville, who saw it in Paris, made his own in Copenhagen (28 Nov 1836) with Lucile Grahn, to a new score by Herman Severin Løvenskiold, because the Opéra asked too high a fee for Schneitzhoeffer's score [documented: Wikipedia]. The Bournonville version, carried by the Royal Danish Ballet's living tradition, is now "the" *La Sylphide* [probable].
- Pierre Lacotte "reconstructed" Taglioni's version for television (1971) and the Paris Opéra (1972) from prints, notes and drawings; critics call it a pastiche [documented: Wikipedia; contested on authenticity].
- Remakes and translations: Barnett's opera *The Mountain Sylph* (London 1834) [documented]; Gilbert and Sullivan's *Iolanthe* (1882) satirises the fairy-mortal love [probable]; Matthew Bourne's *Highland Fling* (1994) [documented]. Fokine's *Les Sylphides* (1909) is a different, plotless work, often confused with it [documented].
- Fashion and objects: the "Taglioni" became the name of a coat and a dance shoe style; lithographs of Taglioni (Chalon, Lane) circulated as collectibles [probable].

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].**
- *Premise:* a man who leaves the real for the ideal destroys both.
- *Perimeter:* one Scottish farm and its forest, one day and night.
- *Rules:* only James sees the sylph; touching the ideal kills it.
- *Anti-DNA:* allegory, gods, court, happy resolution.
- *Anchor expression:* the white-skirted sylph on pointe.
- *Adjacent translation:* a male sprite in a French novella becomes a female spirit in ballet; the female ballet sylph then becomes an opera, a satire and a fashion name.
- *Lifecycle:* the original is lost; the work survives as a Danish remake and a French pastiche, which shows the "work" is the concept, not the steps.

## 5. *Giselle, ou les Wilis* (Paris Opéra, 1841)

The best-documented "origin story" in this file, because the originator, a critic, wrote it down himself, and the teaching case for credit (Perrot).

**1. Premise.**
- *As framed.* A peasant girl who loves to dance is deceived by a disguised nobleman, Albrecht (Albert in 1841); she goes mad and dies. She rises among the Wilis, ghosts of brides who died before their wedding and dance men to death at night; Giselle's love protects Albrecht until dawn [probable: standard synopsis].
- *How its originator framed the idea.* Gautier's own account: reading Heine on the Wilis, he exclaimed "What a pretty ballet this would make!" and wrote "Les Wilis, a ballet" at the top of a sheet [documented as English quotations of Gautier in the Pacific Northwest Ballet article, 2011; the source is Gautier's open letter to Heine, *La Presse*, 5 July 1841, probable].
- *As scholars read it.* Marian Smith reads *Giselle* as part of an Opéra where ballet and opera shared stories, music and techniques, and its mime as legible through the music [probable: Smith 2000]. Feminist readings see the Wilis as female revenge for male betrayal, contained by the ballerina's forgiveness [probable]. Postcolonial and race readings centre on remakes (Creole *Giselle*, Masilo) [documented in scope 01].

**2. Roots.**
- Heinrich Heine, *De l'Allemagne* (French edition 1835), the passage on the Wilis as dead brides who dance at night [documented as the source in Wikipedia and the Petipa Society page; probable on edition].
- Victor Hugo, "Fantômes" (*Les Orientales*, 1829), on a fifteen-year-old Spanish girl who loved dancing and died of a chill after a ball; Gautier took the Act I death from it [documented: Wikipedia].
- Folk: the Slavic *vila* (a female spirit) as Heine's ultimate source, filtered through German and Austrian folklore [probable; the Slavic derivation is commonly asserted but Heine's own source is unclear].
- Earlier works: *La Sylphide* (1832) and the nuns of *Robert le diable* (1831) as models of the white act; Adam's own *La Fille du Danube* (1838), whose Act I set was reused [documented: Wikipedia].

**3. Who had it and how it was developed.**
- Originator: Théophile Gautier, poet and the critic of *La Presse*, in love with Carlotta Grisi [probable for the attachment].
- First draft rejected: his first idea, an empty ballroom with Wilis of several nations in "discoloured" costumes casting a spell, was judged a mere succession of dances [documented: Wikipedia and Petipa Society, quoting Gautier via Guest 2008, pp. 345–6].
- Saint-Georges, an experienced librettist, built the plot; Gautier said that "three days later the ballet of Giselle was written and accepted" and that Adam "sketched out the music" in a week [documented as Gautier's claims, PNB]. The PNB article and Wikipedia say it took about two months, so Gautier compressed the story [documented]. Teaching point: origin stories told by originators are designed too.
- Producer: Léon Pillet, Opéra director, needed a vehicle to launch Grisi and pushed for speed [documented: Wikipedia].
- Jules Perrot, Grisi's teacher, partner and lover, backed the libretto, took it to Adam, and choreographed Grisi's dances, but was not credited on poster or programme [documented: Petipa Society page; probable as fact]. Adam's memoirs: "I was on terms of a very close friendship with Perrot and Carlotta" [documented as quoted by Petipa Society].
- Delays: postponements for Grisi's absence, lighting, trapdoor and scene-change problems, and cuts [documented: Wikipedia].

**4. The world it built.**
- Two-world grammar (from *La Sylphide*, sharpened): Act I a sunny Rhineland (or Silesian) vine-harvest village with Giselle's cottage and Loys's hut; Act II a moonlit forest grave by a lake [probable].
- Music as dramaturgy: Adam used recurring motifs for Giselle, Albrecht, Hilarion, the "he loves me" flower test, the Wilis and the hunting horn, and reprised Act I's love theme in the mad scene [documented: Wikipedia]. He borrowed eight bars from a romance by Loïsa Puget and three bars of Weber's *Euryanthe* hunting chorus; the Peasant pas de deux used Burgmüller's music, added at a dancer's request [documented: Wikipedia].
- Movement: Act I's earthy *ballonné* and peasant waltz versus Act II's floating, crossing lines of Wilis and Myrtha's commanding *arabesques*; the Wilis' lines as the course's clearest example of *corps* geometry turned to menace [argued].
- Costume: Lormier's designs for the principals: peasant bodice and skirt for Giselle in Act I; white Romantic dress with small wings for Act II [probable; scope 06 lists Lormier's *Giselle* designs on Gallica, D216-13(75-81)].
- Anti-DNA: no happy marriage; no court allegory; the supernatural is moral (forgiveness), not divine [argued].

**6. Production.** 28 June 1841, Paris Opéra, Salle Le Peletier [documented]. Audience: the Opéra's subscribers and the Jockey Club public [probable]. Budget not found.

**7. Reception and afterlife.**
- Immediate success; Gautier's own review praised Grisi [documented via Petipa Society quoting Guest].
- Paris notation: Henri Justamant's notation of the 1860s Paris version; published in facsimile (Deutsches Tanzarchiv Köln, ed. Frank-Manuel Peter, 2008) [probable]. The ballet dropped out of the Paris repertory in 1868 and returned in 1924 for Olga Spessivtseva [probable; documented 1924 in Wikipedia].
- St Petersburg: Titus 1842 "from memory"; Perrot's own 1850 production with Petipa assisting; Petipa revivals 1884 (Gorshenkova, Minkus pas de deux added, Act II expanded into a *Grand Pas des Wilis*), 1887, 1899, 1903 (Pavlova); Stepanov notation of Act II (1899) and Act I (1903) in the Sergeyev Collection [documented: Petipa Society]. The "Russian" *Giselle* became "the" *Giselle* and returned west with the Ballets Russes (Covent Garden 1911, Karsavina and Nijinsky) [documented].
- Reconstruction: Ratmansky's Bolshoi production (2019), mainly from the Sergeyev scores with details from Justamant [documented: Petipa Society].
- Remakes: Mats Ek (Cullberg Ballet, 1982), Act II in an asylum [probable]; Frederic Franklin's Creole *Giselle* for Dance Theatre of Harlem (1984), 1840s Louisiana [documented]; Akram Khan for English National Ballet (2016), migrant garment workers [probable]; Dada Masilo (2017), South Africa [probable; scope 06 lists DRJ OA article].
- Adjacent translation: "Giselle" as a name for fashion and perfume lines, and the mad scene in film (e.g. Herbert Ross's *Dancers*, 1987) [probable; not checked].

**8. Primary documents and open images.**

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].**
- *Premise:* love outlasts betrayal and death.
- *Sources:* Heine's legend, Hugo's poem, the Sylphide model.
- *Rules:* Wilis must dance any man to death before dawn; love can only delay it.
- *Anti-DNA:* the critic's own first draft (a plotless ballroom), rejected for lack of drama.
- *Anchor expression:* the Act II Wilis' crossing lines in white.
- *Rights and money:* Pillet's need to launch a star drives the timetable; Perrot's work is unpaid in credit.
- *Lifecycle:* a Paris work survives through Petersburg, is notated there, and returns west as "Russian".
- *Adjacent translation:* the Creole, asylum and garment-factory remakes keep the rules and change the perimeter.

## 6. *Coppélia, ou la Fille aux yeux d'émail* (Paris Opéra, 1870)

**1. Premise.**
- *As framed.* In a village, the young Franz is infatuated with a girl he sees on the balcony of old Dr Coppélius's house. His fiancée Swanilda breaks in, finds that Coppélia is an automaton, and impersonates her to bring the doll "to life", fooling Coppélius and rescuing Franz; Act III is a wedding festival with allegorical dances of the hours [probable: standard synopsis].
- *As scholars read it.* A comedy that inverts a horror story; a ballet about mechanisation and the Industrial Revolution (Owens; Jackson, as cited by Mandradjieff) [documented as cited]. Mara Mandradjieff reads the Act II automata, labelled in the libretto as "Negro", "Moor" and "Chinaman", as "human-objects" that carry "the ontological effects of slavery and colonialism" [documented: DRJ 55.3 (2023, online 2024), CC BY 4.0]. Lynn Garafola reads Franz *en travesti* as part of the Second Empire Opéra's turn of male roles into female display [probable: "The Travesty Dancer in Nineteenth-Century Ballet", DRJ 1985].

**2. Roots.**
- E. T. A. Hoffmann, *Der Sandmann* (1816): the student Nathanael falls in love with Olimpia, an automaton made by Spalanzani and Coppola, and goes mad [documented as the source: Wikipedia]. The ballet keeps the doll, the maker's name (Coppola becomes Coppélius) and the deluded lover, and discards the madness and death [argued].
- Gender reversal again (as in *La Sylphide*): Hoffmann's story is about a man's delusion; the ballet makes the woman the active intelligence who sees through it [argued].
- Automata culture: Vaucanson and Jaquet-Droz automata, toy shops, Paris's fascination with mechanical life; Tom Standage's *The Mechanical Turk* is cited in this connection [probable].
- National dance: the mazurka and *csárdás* in Act I; the setting is a village on the border of Galicia (then Austrian Poland) [probable: the 1870 livret's setting; not seen at source], drawing on the vogue for Hungarian and Polish dance [probable].
- Musical: Delibes had collaborated on *La Source* (1866) [probable].

**3. Who had it and how it was developed.**
- Charles Nuitter, librettist and archivist of the Opéra, devised the libretto with the choreographer Arthur Saint-Léon; Nuitter is also credited for the *mise-en-scène* [documented: Wikipedia credits]. An archivist as concept-maker is unusual and worth noting [argued].
- Saint-Léon was also a violinist and the inventor of a dance notation (*La Sténochorégraphie*, 1852) [probable].
- Development took several years, with the Opéra's management delaying it; Bozzacchi, an Italian student, was trained for the role from her early teens [probable: Guest, *The Ballet of the Second Empire*].
- Documents: livret (Paris, 1870); Delibes's score (Heugel); designs by Lormier and Albert at BnF-BMO [probable].

**4. The world it built.**
- Two spaces: the public square (community, courtship, national dances) and Coppélius's workshop (a cabinet of automata, solitude, artifice) [documented: Mandradjieff quotes the libretto's description of the studio].
- Movement grammar: the "doll" vocabulary of stiff, mechanical movement as a choreographic mode set against fluid human dance; Swanilda passes between them [argued].
- Music: Delibes's symphonic ballet score with national dances, a mazurka and *csárdás*, the doll's waltz, and the "Hours" in Act III [probable].
- Costume and casting rule: Franz danced *en travesti* by Eugénie Fiocre [documented].
- Anti-DNA: the supernatural (there are no ghosts here; the "magic" is mechanical and exposed) [argued]. This is the Romantic ballet's world inverted.

**5. The team and the credit.**

|---|---|---|
| Libretto, mise-en-scène | Charles Nuitter | [documented] |
| Choreography, co-librettist | Arthur Saint-Léon | [documented] |
| Music | Léo Delibes | [documented] |

| Scenery | Charles-Antoine Cambon (Act I sc. 1; Act II sc. 1); Édouard Despléchin and Jean-Baptiste Lavastre (Act I sc. 2) [as credited on Wikipedia; the act numbering there is probably garbled] | [documented, with caution] |

**6. Production.** 25 May 1870, Théâtre Impérial de l'Opéra (Salle Le Peletier), Paris [documented]. Two months later France declared war on Prussia; the Opéra closed during the siege; Bozzacchi died on her seventeenth birthday (23 November 1870); Saint-Léon had died in September 1870 [documented for Bozzacchi; probable for dates]. The last ballet of the Second Empire's Opéra [probable].

**7. Reception and afterlife.**
- Success, then interruption by war; it became the Paris Opéra's most performed ballet [probable].
- St Petersburg: Petipa's 1884 revival, with later revisions by Cecchetti and Ivanov (1894); notated in Stepanov notation c. 1900; those notations, carried by Sergeyev, underlie the Vic-Wells production (1933) and the English tradition [documented: Wikipedia on Stepanov and Vic-Wells; probable for dates].
- Balanchine's version for New York City Ballet (1974), with Alexandra Danilova staging Petipa's Act II [documented].
- Remakes: Roland Petit (Marseille, 1975), Maguy Marin (Lyon, 1993, a suburb with many Coppélias), and others [probable].
- Afterlife of the concept: the doll-come-alive as a ballet type (*Die Puppenfee*, Vienna 1888; *Petrushka*, 1911) [probable]; Offenbach's *Les Contes d'Hoffmann* (1881) treats Olympia as a singing doll [probable]. The racialised automata are now being redesigned or cut [documented: Mandradjieff surveys Royal Ballet 1957, 2000, 2019, Australian Ballet, Novosibirsk, POB School and others].

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].**
- *Premise:* a woman out-thinks a man who loves a machine.
- *Sources:* Hoffmann's horror story, turned to comedy.
- *Rules:* the doll can only move when wound or impersonated; the human always wins.
- *Anti-DNA:* the supernatural, explicitly demystified.
- *Collection map:* the workshop of automata, a set of "types", which is where the racial caricature lives.
- *Obligations:* the course can use Mandradjieff to show that a world's "collection" encodes its makers' hierarchy and that remakes inherit it unless they redesign it.

## 7. *La Bayadère* (St Petersburg, 1877)

**1. Premise.**
- *As framed.* In an imagined India, the temple dancer (*bayadère*, from Portuguese *bailadeira*) Nikiya loves the warrior Solor; the High Brahmin desires her; the Rajah marries Solor to his daughter Gamzatti, who has Nikiya killed by a snake hidden in a flower basket; Solor, in an opium dream, sees Nikiya among the Shades; in the lost Act IV the gods destroy the temple at the wedding [documented: Wikipedia on acts and the temple destruction; probable on synopsis].
- *As scholars read it.* "A vision of the Southern Orient through 19th century European eyes" [documented: Petipa Society]. Orientalist fantasy in Said's sense: an India assembled from European texts, travellers' accounts and earlier ballets, not from Indian dance [argued, following Said 1978 and scope 03]. The Kingdom of the Shades is read separately as a peak of pure classical form, the scene that survives as an abstract piece [probable: Croce 1974 on Petipa's "motor impulse", documented as quoted on Wikipedia].

**2. Roots.**
- Ballet ancestors: Filippo Taglioni's opera-ballet *Le Dieu et la Bayadère* (Paris, 1830, Auber, after Goethe's ballad "Der Gott und die Bajadere", 1797); Petipa danced in it at Bordeaux and his first wife danced it in St Petersburg in 1854 [documented: Petipa Society; the Goethe link probable].
- Kalidasa's Sanskrit drama *Abhijñānaśākuntalam*, via Gautier's livret for Lucien Petipa's *Sacountala* (Paris Opéra, 1858), which Marius would have known [documented: Petipa Society].
- Real Indian dancers: a troupe of temple dancers (devadasis) toured Paris and London in 1838 and was a sensation [documented: Petipa Society]; Gautier wrote about them [probable].
- Current events: the Prince of Wales's tour of India (1875–76), widely reported [documented as Petipa Society's suggestion; argued].
- Ethnographic reading: Khudekov's own *History of Dance* describes devadasis, and the libretto names Indian instruments, plants and social groups, which Tikhonenko links to Khudekov's research [documented: Tikhonenko 2018].
- Visual: Gustave Doré's illustrations to Dante's *Divine Comedy* inspired the groupings of the Shades [documented: Wikipedia; widely repeated, probable as fact].

**3. Who had it and how it was developed.**
- **Authorship is contested, with a primary document on each side.** Sergei Khudekov, dramatist, journalist and later author of a four-volume *History of Dance*, is credited with the libretto [documented: Wikipedia]. In a letter of 1900 Petipa wrote, "Сюжет балета «Баядерка» принадлежит всецело мне" ("the plot belongs entirely to me"), conceding only Khudekov's advice on one scene and an eight-line marginal note in Khudekov's hand [documented: Tikhonenko, *Vestnik of the Vaganova Academy* 6(59), 2018]. Tikhonenko concludes Khudekov is "exceptionally well suited to the role of author of the plot and dramaturgy" and suggests the Russian text may be Khudekov's own or his translation of a French draft by Petipa [documented]. The Petipa Society takes a joint-authorship view [documented]. Teach as [contested].
- The ballet was made for Ekaterina Vazem's benefit performance [documented: Wikipedia]. Vazem's memoir praises "its beautiful, very theatrical scenario" [documented as quoted].
- Producer: Imperial Theatres Directorate; director Karl Kister raised ticket prices above those of the Italian Opera, which worried Petipa [documented: Petipa Society].

**4. The world it built.**
- Setting: a composite India of temples, sacred fire, palace, tiger hunt and Himalayan peaks (the Shades moved to the Himalayas in 1900) [documented].
- Movement grammar in two registers: "character" dances (temple, fakirs, the *Danse Manu*, the Golden Idol added in Soviet versions) coded as Indian; and the strictly classical Kingdom of the Shades, where 32 (from 1900, 48) women descend a ramp in repeated *arabesques* [documented for numbers]. The Orient is costume and gesture; the "ideal" is classical [argued].
- Music: Minkus wrote functional dance music with little use of Indian forms [documented: Petipa Society].
- Scenic spectacle: the temple collapse by the machinist Roller [documented: Wikipedia].
- Anti-DNA: Indian dance technique itself; any Indian collaborator [argued]. Make-up practices (darkened skin, brownface/blackface for some roles) belong to the world's rules and are now being removed [probable: scope 03].

**6. Production.** 4 February 1877 (N.S.; 23 January O.S.), Imperial Bolshoi Kamenny Theatre, St Petersburg [documented]. A success; the Shades and the Act IV destruction were most praised [documented: Petipa Society].

**7. Reception and afterlife.**
- 1900 revival for Gerdt's benefit, with Kschessinska; notated by Sergeyev in rehearsal, almost complete [documented].
- Vaganova revision (1932); Ponomarev and Chabukiani (Kirov, 10 Feb 1941), which dropped Act IV and is the basis of most productions [documented].
- West: the Kingdom of the Shades alone, staged by Nureyev for the Royal Ballet (1963), became a concert piece [probable]; Makarova's full version for ABT (21 May 1980) [documented]; Nureyev's Paris version (8 Oct 1992), designs by Ezio Frigerio drawing on the Taj Mahal, Ottoman architecture and drawings of the 1877 décor, costumes by Franca Squarciapino [documented].
- Reconstruction: Vikharev, Mariinsky, 31 May 2002, restoring Act IV from the notation and Minkus's full score from the theatre archive, while keeping most Soviet choreography [documented].
- Critical remakes: Shobana Jeyasingh, *Bayadère – The Ninth Life* (2015), a British Indian choreographer's response [probable: title seen in results]; companies have redesigned costumes and dropped skin darkening [probable].
- Film: Alexander Shiryaev's puppet films were used in restoring it at the Mariinsky (scope 02).

- *Premise:* sacred love betrayed by caste and ambition, avenged by the gods.
- *Sources:* Goethe, Kalidasa via Gautier, Taglioni's 1830 ballet, the 1838 tour, the press.
- *Rules:* two registers of movement, "Oriental" character dance and classical ideal.
- *Anchor expression:* the descent of the Shades, which outlived the rest.
- *Rights:* the libretto dispute is a rights dispute about the concept itself.

## 8. *The Sleeping Beauty* (St Petersburg, 1890)

The course's anchor case: the clearest surviving paper trail of a concept being proposed (Vsevolozhsky's letter), accepted (Tchaikovsky's reply), specified (Petipa's written plans), executed (Tchaikovsky's dated sketches and score) and changed in rehearsal. Scope 01 and 04 already cover the political-reading dispute (Scholl vs Kirstein/Hammond/Mahiet), the 1890 scene painters, Messel 1946 and the Sergeyev notation; this entry does not repeat them.

**1. Premise.**
- *As framed by its originator.* Vsevolozhsky proposed Perrault's *La Belle au bois dormant* with "a mise en scène in the style of Louis XIV" and a "musical fantasia written in the spirit of Lully, Bach, Rameau, etc." [documented: Tchaikovsky Research, quoting the letter of 13/25 May 1888]. For the last act he asked for quadrilles of Perrault's tales, "Puss-in-Boots, Hop o' My Thumb, Cinderella, Bluebeard, etc." [documented, same], or in the Petipa Society's rendering, "indispensably necessary is a quadrille of all of Perrault's fairy tales" [documented].
- *As scholars read it.* A *ballet-féerie*, a spectacle genre of transformations and processions, given symphonic weight by Tchaikovsky; Scholl calls it a "legend in progress" because each age remakes it [probable: Scholl 2004]. Its politics are contested (scope 01).

**2. Roots.**
- Charles Perrault, "La Belle au bois dormant", *Histoires ou contes du temps passé* (1697); the ballet uses only the first half (to the wedding), dropping the ogress mother-in-law [probable].
- Louis XIV's court as an aesthetic source: Versailles, the *ballet de cour*, Lully; the Act III wedding processions echo the *carrousel* and the court ballet's final grand ballet (see entries 1–2 of this file) [argued; Vsevolozhsky's letter documents the intention].
- Earlier ballet versions of the tale: Aumer and Hérold's *La Belle au bois dormant* (Paris Opéra, 1829), with diorama effects by Ciceri and Daguerre (scope 04) [probable].
- The féerie theatre of Paris and the Italian spectacle ballets (Manzotti's *Excelsior*, 1881) then popular in St Petersburg; Italian virtuoso ballerinas like Carlotta Brianza [probable].
- Vsevolozhsky's own francophilia: he had served as a diplomat in Paris [probable].

**3. Who had it and how it was developed (the document sequence).**

Tchaikovsky sketched the fairies' Prologue numbers in October 1888, before Petipa's detailed Prologue scheme of 6/18 November [documented: Tchaikovsky Research dates], and Wiley's evidence suggests some detailed plans postdate composition, so Petipa may at first have given only outlines [documented as reported from Wiley]. Teach both readings: the plan as brief, or the plan as a record of a conversation already underway.

**4. The world it built.**
- Structure: Prologue (christening and curse), Act I (sixteenth birthday, spindle), Act II (a hundred years later, the hunt, vision, panorama, awakening), Act III (wedding with fairy-tale divertissements) [probable].
- Rule of good and evil fairies: Lilac Fairy (Marie Petipa, a mime role) vs Carabosse (Enrico Cecchetti, a man in a mime role); each fairy's variation is a "gift" with a character (Candide, Coulante, Breadcrumb, Canary, Violente) [probable].
- Movement grammar: academic classicism at full scale; the Rose Adagio's balances; the Act III divertissements as a catalogue of tales; the Bluebird *pas de deux* for Cecchetti's virtuosity [probable].
- Music: Tchaikovsky's symphonic ballet with leitmotifs for Carabosse and the Lilac Fairy [probable], to Petipa's lengths.
- Look: Vsevolozhsky designed the costumes himself in Louis XIV and later styles (the Prologue in Louis XIV period, Act III a century on) [probable: Wiley; scope 04]; four scene painters for different acts (scope 04).
- Anti-DNA: realism, tragedy and politics on stage; the "dramatic" ballet that critics feared the féerie would kill [documented: Petipa Society notes such fears].

**6. Production.** Premiere 3/15 January 1890, Mariinsky Theatre, St Petersburg [documented]. The production was famously costly; a figure of about 42,000 roubles is repeated in secondary literature [probable, unverified in this pass]. Audience: the court, subscribers, the Imperial family.

**7. Reception and afterlife.**
- Success; the Garland Waltz brought Petipa a bow and Puss in Boots was encored; some critics feared the féerie genre would end ballet drama [documented: Petipa Society]. Tchaikovsky called it "perhaps the best of all my compositions" [documented as quoted, Tchaikovsky Research].
- Notated in Stepanov notation c. 1903 (Sergeyev Collection) [documented].
- Diaghilev's *The Sleeping Princess* (London, Alhambra, 1921), Sergeyev staging from notation, Bakst designs, Stravinsky re-orchestrations, Nijinska changes; a financial failure that nearly bankrupted the Ballets Russes [documented: Petipa Society].
- Sadler's Wells Ballet reopened the Royal Opera House on 20 Feb 1946 (de Valois, Sergeyev, Ashton; Fonteyn; Messel designs; scope 04, 05) [documented].
- Reconstructions: Vikharev, Mariinsky, 30 April 1999, met with hostility from the company and balletomanes and later retired; Ratmansky, ABT, March 2015, designs by Richard Hudson after Bakst, and La Scala 30 Sep 2015 [documented: Petipa Society, Wikipedia].
- Adjacent translation: Disney's animated *Sleeping Beauty* (1959) used Tchaikovsky's music; Disney later registered a "Princess Aurora" trademark [documented: Wikipedia]. Matthew Bourne's gothic *Sleeping Beauty* (2012) [probable]. The Lilac Fairy, Bluebird and Rose Adagio as fashion and perfume references [probable, not checked].

- *Premise:* time suspended by a curse and restored by love, staged as the return of a golden age.
- *Originator:* an administrator, not an artist.

## 9. *The Nutcracker* (St Petersburg, 1892)

Scope 01 and 05 cover the American *Nutcracker* economy (Fisher) and Mahiet's political reading; this entry covers the making.

**1. Premise.**
- *As framed.* At a Christmas Eve party, the girl Clara (Marie in Hoffmann) is given a nutcracker by her godfather Drosselmeyer; at night the toys fight the mice, the Nutcracker becomes a prince, and he takes her through a snow forest to the Kingdom of Sweets (Confiturembourg), where the Sugar-Plum Fairy entertains them with national dances [probable: standard synopsis].
- *As scholars read it.* A child's-eye féerie in which domestic Christmas becomes a world tour; Fisher reads its American form as a story communities tell about their values (scope 05); Mahiet reads the 1890s ballets through imperial ceremony and international relations (scope 01). The "national" dances of Act II (Spanish chocolate, Arabian coffee, Chinese tea) are read as a catalogue of consumer goods from empire, coded as peoples [probable: Fisher, "Arabian Coffee in the Land of the Sweets", DRJ 2003/4].

**2. Roots.**
- E. T. A. Hoffmann, *Nussknacker und Mausekönig* (1816), a darker tale with a long inset story (the "Tale of the Hard Nut") and an ambiguous ending [documented as source: Tchaikovsky Research].
- Alexandre Dumas *père*, *Histoire d'un casse-noisette* (1844/45), a softened French adaptation, which was the direct source [documented: Tchaikovsky Research]. The ballet discards the Hard Nut tale and Marie's illness [probable].
- Christmas culture: the German Christmas tree and children's party, imported into Russian elite homes [probable].
- Commodity culture: confectionery, Paris shop windows and exhibitions; Tchaikovsky found the celesta in Paris [documented for celesta].

**3. Who had it and how it was developed.**
- Originator and commissioner: Ivan Vsevolozhsky, who held talks with Tchaikovsky in November–December 1890 for a double bill of a one-act opera (*Iolanta*) and a two-act ballet [documented: Tchaikovsky Research].
- Libretto: compiled by Vsevolozhsky and Petipa; Petipa's manuscript plan of Act I is dated 5 February 1891 (O.S.); the Act II plan was sent to Tchaikovsky in Paris on 9 March 1891 [documented].
- **The composer edited the brief.** After meeting Vsevolozhsky, Tchaikovsky crossed out the characteristic dances in Petipa's plan and wrote new numbers, including the Galop and the parents' entrance [documented]. Vsevolozhsky urged "great dances" rather than variations and said Petipa's solos would bore the audience [documented as summarised]. So the specification was negotiated among three people, not handed down [argued].
- Composer's resistance: Modest Tchaikovsky said the composer was "very little pleased by the subject" [documented as quoted]; Vsevolozhsky felt "agonies of remorse" for imposing it [documented as quoted]. On hearing of his sister's death in Rouen, Tchaikovsky wrote he felt incapable of depicting Confiturembourg [documented]. He finished the sketches in "a feverish haste" in late June 1891 [documented].
- Credit and copyright: Tchaikovsky wanted to credit Hoffmann and Dumas on the title page; his publisher Jurgenson warned of copyright trouble with Dumas's heirs, and Dumas's name was dropped [documented: Tchaikovsky Research]. An early case of a source erased for rights reasons [argued].
- Choreographer change: Petipa fell ill and the choreography was made by his second ballet master Lev Ivanov [probable; Tchaikovsky Research credits Ivanov with choreography and production]. How much Ivanov followed Petipa's plan is debated [contested: Wiley 1997 argues for Ivanov's authorship within Petipa's scheme].

**4. The world it built.**
- Two-world grammar again, now domestic: a bourgeois German parlour with a tree that grows, then a snow pine forest, then a palace of sweets [probable].
- Music: the celesta, ordered from Paris (a "Celesta-Mustel" with "a divinely unusual sound"), for the Sugar-Plum Fairy [documented]; children's toy instruments in the party scene; a descending-scale *pas de deux* adagio [probable].
- Movement: children on stage (Imperial school pupils), mechanical-doll dances, the Waltz of the Snowflakes for the corps, national-character dances in Act II [probable].
- Look: Act I sets by Konstantin Ivanov; Act II by Mikhail Bocharov; costumes by Vsevolozhsky and Ivan Ponomarev [probable].
- Anti-DNA: Hoffmann's darkness, madness and sickbed; adult tragedy [argued].

**5. The team and the credit.**

|---|---|---|
| Idea, commission, co-libretto, costumes | Ivan Vsevolozhsky | [documented for idea and libretto; probable for costumes] |
| Scenario and plan | Marius Petipa | [documented] |
| Choreography | Lev Ivanov | [documented] |
| Music | Tchaikovsky | [documented] |
| Conductor | Riccardo Drigo | [documented] |
| Sources | Hoffmann (credited), Dumas (dropped for copyright) | [documented] |
| Scenery | Konstantin Ivanov, Mikhail Bocharov | [probable] |

**6. Production.** 6/18 December 1892, Mariinsky Theatre, St Petersburg, double bill with *Iolanta* [documented]. Tchaikovsky: the staging was lavish and went perfectly but "it seemed to me that the public did not like it" [documented as quoted].

**7. Reception and afterlife.**

- Remakes: Mark Morris, *The Hard Nut* (1991); Matthew Bourne, *Nutcracker!* (1992); Alexander Ekman; the "Tea" and "Coffee" dances redesigned after Phil Chan and Georgina Pazcoguin's Final Bow for Yellowface pledge (2017) (scope 03) [probable].

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].**
- *Premise:* a child's Christmas night becomes a journey to a kingdom of sweets.
- *Sources:* Hoffmann via Dumas, with Dumas erased.
- *Rules:* toys live at night; the journey goes from home, through snow, to abundance.
- *Collection map:* Act II as a tray of sweets, each a "nation"; this is where the caricature sits.
- *Handover:* the brief changes hands three times (Vsevolozhsky → Petipa → Tchaikovsky's edits → Ivanov).
- *Lifecycle:* a failure in 1892 becomes the most performed ballet in the world through American seasonal ritual.
- *Rights and money:* copyright fear erased a source; the ballet later became a company's revenue engine (scope 05).

## 10. *Swan Lake* (Moscow 1877; St Petersburg 1895)

Two works under one title: a Moscow commission that failed as a production, and a posthumous Petersburg remake that became "the" ballet. The best case in this file for "the work is remade into itself".

**1. Premise.**
- *1877.* Prince Siegfried, urged to marry, meets by a lake Odette, a swan-maiden protected by a crown against a persecuting stepmother (an owl-sorcerer); he betrays her at a ball with Odile; the lake floods and the lovers drown [probable: 1877 libretto as summarised in Wiley and Wikipedia; documented that Vsevolozhsky later questioned "the flood and crown-destruction ending", Tchaikovsky Research].
- *1895.* Modest Tchaikovsky's revised libretto: Odette a mortal cursed by the sorcerer Rothbart (the stepmother removed), three acts not four, the ending a double suicide by drowning and an apotheosis of reunion [documented: Wikipedia].
- *As scholars read it.* The swan-maiden as an ideal that the male hero betrays; the white/black doubling of Odette and Odile as the ballerina's double role and the century's split image of woman [probable]. Bourne's 1995 version read it as a story about the prince's sexuality and the pressures of royalty [probable].

**2. Roots.**
- Folk and literary: the swan-maiden myth found across Europe and Asia; Johann Karl August Musäus's tale "Der geraubte Schleier" (1784) is usually cited, though accounts "bear only limited resemblance" to the ballet [documented: Wikipedia]; Russian and German folk traditions [probable].
- Family prototype: Tchaikovsky's relatives reported that he wrote a little ballet "The Lake of the Swans" for the children at his sister's home (Kamenka) in 1871 [documented as a family report: Wikipedia; probable].
- Wagner's *Lohengrin* (swan, sorcerer, heraldic trumpets) and Ludwig II of Bavaria as a possible model for Siegfried [documented as suggested parallels: Wikipedia; argued].
- Tchaikovsky's own earlier music: the Undina/Huldbrandt duet from his destroyed opera *Undina* (1869) became the Act II love duet; music from *The Voyevoda* (1867–68) became the Act IV entr'acte and opening [documented: Tchaikovsky Research]. The composer recycled his own abandoned worlds.

**3. Who had it and how it was developed.**
- *1877 commission.* The Bolshoi Theatre Directorate in Moscow commissioned it in spring 1875; Kashkin recalled Tchaikovsky being persuaded to a "medieval-knight" subject [documented: Tchaikovsky Research].
- *Libretto authorship is contested.* Kashkin named Vladimir Begichev (Intendant of the Moscow theatres); Modest reported the Directorate's copy marked "property of Geltser" (the dancer Vasily Geltser, probably the one who wrote it down); Modest later named both as joint authors; new Bolshoi archive materials support Kashkin; the printed libretto matches the scenario Tchaikovsky wrote in his manuscript score, so the composer shaped the plot outline [documented: Tchaikovsky Research, citing Wiley 1985, pp. 32–38, and Konayev 2015]. Teaching point: four candidate "authors" of one concept, and the composer among them.
- Composition: two acts outlined by August 1875; orchestration begun by 13 October 1875; full score finished at Glebovo, dated 10 April 1876; payment of 400 roubles still outstanding in April 1876, to come from the first four performances' receipts [documented].
- *1895 remake.* After Tchaikovsky's death (October 1893), Lev Ivanov staged Act II for a memorial evening on 17 Feb/1 Mar 1894 [documented]. Vsevolozhsky asked Modest to rewrite the libretto; Drigo consulted Petipa and Ivanov on each number and orchestrated three of Tchaikovsky's piano pieces (Op. 72 nos. 11, 12, 15) for insertion [documented: Tchaikovsky Research]. Here the "development" is by the institution, the brother, the conductor and two choreographers, without the composer [argued].

**4. The world it built.**
- Two-world grammar, sharpened: court (Acts I and III, Petipa) vs lake (Acts II and IV, Ivanov) [documented split of acts].
- Movement: Ivanov's swans built from arm and neck movements that mimic wings, with the corps as a flock in lines and wedges; Petipa's court with national dances (Spanish, Neapolitan, Hungarian, Mazurka) and the Black Swan *pas de deux*, where Legnani's *fouettés* (32, a feat she had already shown in *Cinderella*, 1893) became Odile's signature [probable].
- Doubling rule: one ballerina dances Odette and Odile, though the original may have used two dancers [documented: Wikipedia notes this uncertainty].
- Music: symphonic, leitmotivic (the swan theme), which 1877 critics called "too noisy, too 'Wagnerian' and too symphonic" [documented as quoted, Wikipedia].
- Palette: white for the lake, colour and black for the court; blue night lake [probable].
- Anti-DNA: a happy ending (until Soviet versions imposed one) [argued].

**6. Production.** 1877: 20 Feb/4 Mar 1877, Bolshoi Theatre, Moscow, a benefit for Karpakova; in repertory to 1882/83 [documented]. 1895: 15/27 Jan 1895, Mariinsky, St Petersburg, a benefit for Legnani; Moscow followed 5/17 May 1896 [documented].

**7. Reception and afterlife.**
- 1877: critics found Reisinger's choreography "unimaginative and altogether unmemorable" [documented as quoted, Wikipedia]. Tchaikovsky himself, after hearing Delibes's *Sylvia* in Vienna, told Nadezhda von Meck that *Swan Lake* was far poorer [documented]. Joseph Hansen's Moscow revivals (1880, 1882) cut music and added a Pugni number [documented].
- 1895 onward: Stepanov notation of the Petipa–Ivanov production in the Sergeyev Collection (Harvard MS Thr 186, scope 02); Ratmansky's Zurich reconstruction (2016) used it [probable].
- Soviet: Gorsky's Moscow versions; Konstantin Sergeyev (Kirov 1950) and Grigorovich (Bolshoi 1969) imposed happy endings; Grigorovich made it tragic again in 2001 [documented: Wikipedia]. Soviet television broadcast it on a loop during the August 1991 coup, and at earlier leadership crises (1982, 1984, 1985) [documented: Wikipedia]. A ballet became a political signal [argued].
- Remakes: Nureyev (Vienna 1966; Paris 1986); Mats Ek (1987) [probable]; Matthew Bourne (1995), male swans [probable]; Dada Masilo (2010) [documented]; Alexander Ekman (Oslo), 5,000 litres of water [documented]. Japan's earliest complete *Swan Lake* in 1946 (scope 01).
- Adjacent translation: Darren Aronofsky's *Black Swan* (2010) [documented]; the white tutu and swan as fashion and advertising images; the Bolshoi's own brand [probable].

**10. Concept DNA reading [argued].**
- *Premise:* a prince's vow to an enchanted swan-maiden, broken by deception.
- *Sources:* swan-maiden myth, Musäus, *Lohengrin*, the composer's own abandoned operas.
- *Rules:* the vow must be kept; the double (Odile) is the test.
- *Anchor expression:* the lakeside white act, and Odile's *fouettés*.
- *Lifecycle:* a failed concept, re-specified by institution and family after the maker's death, then remade by every regime: tragic, happy, tragic again.
- *Rights:* who owns the story was never settled; the dead composer had no say in 1895.

## 11. Cross-work patterns: how ballet concepts are born and developed

All of this section is **[argued]**: our reading across the ten entries above, for the course to test.

**11.1 Who originates.** In this sample the choreographer is rarely the person who had the idea.
| Work | Originator | Their job |
|---|---|---|
| *Balet comique* (1581) | the Queen's commission; d'Aubigné claims the plot | patron; Protestant poet (self-claim) |
| *Nuit* (1653) | Mazarin; Hesselin or Clément organising | first minister; court official |
| *Médée et Jason* (1763) | Noverre | choreographer-theorist (the exception) |
| *La Sylphide* (1832) | Adolphe Nourrit | opera tenor |
| *Giselle* (1841) | Théophile Gautier | poet and critic |
| *Coppélia* (1870) | Charles Nuitter, with Saint-Léon | Opéra archivist and librettist |
| *La Bayadère* (1877) | Khudekov or Petipa | journalist-historian or ballet master (contested) |
| *Sleeping Beauty* (1890) | Ivan Vsevolozhsky | theatre director (administrator) |
| *Nutcracker* (1892) | Vsevolozhsky | administrator |
| *Swan Lake* (1877) | Moscow directorate; Begichev or Geltser; Tchaikovsky | administrator, dancer, composer (contested) |

Pattern: ideas come from the edges of the institution (singers, critics, archivists, managers), and the institution assigns a team. Where authorship of the idea is recorded at all, it is often claimed later by one party (d'Aubigné, Gautier, Petipa's 1900 letter) and disputed by another. Origin stories are themselves designed (Gautier's "three days").

**11.2 What sources.** Four recurring source types:
1. A literary text, usually foreign and usually changed in gender or tone: Homer's Circe; Corneille's *Médée*; Nodier's male sprite becomes a female sylph; Hoffmann's male delusion becomes a woman's trick; Hoffmann's dark tale is softened via Dumas; Perrault's tale is cut in half.
2. A previous stage success the new work answers: the Nuns of *Robert* behind *La Sylphide*; *La Sylphide* behind *Giselle*; Taglioni's 1830 *Bayadère* and Lucien Petipa's *Sacountala* behind *La Bayadère*; Aumer's 1829 *Belle au bois dormant* behind Petipa's.
3. A visual or cultural fashion: Scott's Scotland; Doré's Dante; Louis XIV revival; automata; the 1838 devadasi tour; the Prince of Wales in India.
4. The makers' own reserves: Tchaikovsky's music from *Undina* and *The Voyevoda* in *Swan Lake*; Ciceri's reused set in *Giselle*; Drigo's insertions of piano pieces.

Borrowing from other cultures appears as costume and "character" dance, never as collaboration (*Bayadère*, *Coppélia*'s automata, *Nutcracker*'s Act II); this is where today's remakes intervene.

**11.3 How teams form.** Three models appear in sequence:
- *Court model* (1581, 1653): a patron commands, a deviser coordinates named specialists (poet, musicians, painter, machinist); the patron and nobles perform.
- *Theatre-enterprise model* (Paris 1832–70): a director-manager (Véron, Pillet) needs a vehicle for a star; a librettist and choreographer pair work from a scenario; resident designers (Ciceri, Lormier) and house composers deliver; uncredited specialists (Perrot) fill gaps.
- *Imperial bureaucracy model* (St Petersburg 1877–95): the Director of Theatres originates, the ballet master writes a numbered plan, the composer delivers to specification, workshop painters divide the acts, and the conductor (Drigo) edits. The star's benefit night often sets the date.

**11.4 How worlds get rules.** Recurring grammars:
- *Two worlds.* Court and wilderness, day and night, real and ideal, house and workshop, parlour and kingdom of sweets, court and lake. From *La Sylphide* on, almost every work in this file is built on a two-world opposition with a crossing.
- *Two movement registers.* Character or national dance for the "real" or "foreign" world; pure classical dance in white for the ideal (the Shades, Wilis, swans, snowflakes).
- *Ending in order.* The court works end in royal restoration (Circe captive, the Sun rising); the Romantic and Imperial works replace the king with love, death, apotheosis or wedding.
- *Written grammars.* Two works have rules written down before the work: Noverre's *Lettres* (a general grammar) and Petipa's plan for Tchaikovsky (a specific brief by number, metre, bars and mood). Both are teachable as the earliest known "Concept DNA" documents in this history.
- *Anti-DNA is often stated by contrast*: Beaujoyeulx against the plotless masquerade; Noverre against masks and symmetry; Gautier's own rejected ballroom draft; *Coppélia* against the supernatural.

**11.5 How works are remade.**
- *Loss and replacement.* Most first productions are lost. *La Sylphide* survives as a Danish remake; *Giselle* and *Coppélia* survive through Petersburg; Taglioni's original is a 1972 "reconstruction" that critics call a pastiche.
- *Institutional remaking after death.* *Swan Lake* became itself only after its composer died, with a new libretto by his brother and new music orders from the conductor.
- *Notation as afterlife.* The Stepanov notations made in St Petersburg c. 1899–1903 are the source of the "Petipa" ballets in the West and of reconstructions (Vikharev 1999, 2002; Ratmansky 2015, 2016, 2019). Reconstructions are contested and often retired.
- *Political remaking.* Soviet happy endings for *Swan Lake*; the TV loop of 1991; Daucé's 2020 *Nuit* that adds operas not in 1653.
- *Critical remaking.* Creole *Giselle*, Masilo, Khan, Bourne, Jeyasingh, redesigned *Coppélia* automata and *Nutcracker* "Tea": the same rules, a changed perimeter or a rewritten anti-DNA.
- *Adjacent translation.* Concepts escape the stage as images and products: the Sun King's costume as reign emblem; Taglioni as fashion name; Disney's Aurora as trademark; *Fantasia*'s *Nutcracker*; *Black Swan*; a 1581 bell tune as a Japanese rice-cooker chime.

**11.6 Credit.** Credit lines routinely drop: the source author (Nodier, Dumas), the idea person (Nourrit on the 1832 livret), the co-choreographer (Perrot; Ivanov under "after Petipa"), the scene painters under a chief, and every workshop. The surviving printed livret or programme is usually the evidence of who was named, and the memoirs and letters are the evidence of who did the work. The course can teach the gap between the two as a design problem.

**11.7 Teaching sequence suggested by the sample.**
1. *Balet comique*: a concept that exists only as a book.
2. Noverre: a written grammar before a work.
3. *Giselle*: an idea told as a story by its originator, and the uncredited maker.
4. *Sleeping Beauty*: a brief, a plan, a score, a rehearsal change, dated document by document.
5. *Swan Lake*: a failed concept remade into the canon.
6. *Bayadère* and *Coppélia*: the collection map that carries a hierarchy, and remakes that redesign it.
